## MPC is truncated dynamic programming {#truncated}

The MPC problem of chapter [[a11]] at the state $x$ is the finite-horizon problem of chapter [[a14]], so its value and its law are

$$J_N(x) = \big( T^N \bar V_f \big)(x), \qquad \kappa_N(x) \in \arg\min_u \big\{ \bar q(x, u) + \big( T^{N-1} \bar V_f \big)\big( f(x, u) \big) \big\},$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} J_N(x) &= ( T^N \bar V_f )(x), \\ \kappa_N(x) &\in \arg\min_u \{ \bar q(x, u) + ( T^{N-1} \bar V_f )( f(x, u) ) \}, \end{aligned}

with $T$ the undiscounted constrained Bellman operator and $\bar V_f$ the terminal cost, infinite outside $\mathcal X_f$. MPC is the greedy policy with respect to $T^{N-1}\bar V_f$, evaluated only at the current state by solving an optimisation over $N$ inputs instead of tabulating; its feasible set is the controllable set $\mathcal K_N(\mathcal X_f)$ of chapter [[a09]]. On the lattice the law can be tabulated after all, and that is what makes this lab exact: $T^{N-1}\bar V_f$ is computed by $N - 1$ sweeps, the greedy policy is read off one more, and the closed loop is run from every feasible state.

## What a good terminal cost buys {#terminal}

::: define What a good terminal cost buys
**In plain words:** the horizon only has to reach the terminal set; if the terminal cost tells the truth about what the rest of the journey costs, the finite-horizon plan is the infinite-horizon plan.

**More precisely, an exact tail gives exact MPC.** If $V_f = J^\star$ on $\mathcal X_f$, then $\bar V_f \ge J^\star$ and monotonicity gives $T^N\bar V_f \ge T^N J^\star = J^\star$, with equality wherever an optimal trajectory enters $\mathcal X_f$ within $N$ steps.

**And a terminal decrease gives a monotone value and stability.** The terminal assumptions of chapter [[a11]], for every $x \in \mathcal X_f$ an admissible $u$ with $f(x, u) \in \mathcal X_f$ and $V_f(f(x, u)) - V_f(x) \le -q(x, u)$, say exactly $\bar V_f \ge T\bar V_f$. Applying the monotone $T^N$ gives $J_N \ge J_{N+1}$, and with $x^+ = f(x, \kappa_N(x))$,
$$J_N(x) = q\big( x, \kappa_N(x) \big) + J_{N-1}(x^+) \;\ge\; q\big( x, \kappa_N(x) \big) + J_N(x^+).$$
So $J_N$ is a Lyapunov function, $x^+ \in \operatorname{dom} J_{N-1} \subseteq \operatorname{dom} J_N$ gives recursive feasibility, and the closed-loop cost is at most $J_N(x_0)$: chapter [[a11]]'s theorem in two lines of operator algebra.

- Without terminal ingredients :: With $V_f = 0$ and $\mathcal X_f = \mathcal X$, $T^N 0 \le J^\star$ underestimates the tail, the feasible set $\mathcal C_N$ contains doomed states for small $N$, and the decrease is unavailable.
- With discounting :: The effect is quantitative: the greedy policy of $T^{N-1} V_f$ is within $\frac{2\gamma^N}{1 - \gamma}\|V_f - J^\star\|_\infty$ of optimal, so a longer horizon or a better terminal cost both help, and either can compensate the other.
:::

## Three designs on the lattice {#designs}

Design A: $V_f = 0$, $\mathcal X_f = \mathcal X$, the textbook MPC without terminal ingredients. Design B: $V_f = x^\top P x$ on $\mathcal X_f = \mathcal O_\infty$, the LQR ingredients of chapter [[a11]]. Design C: $V_f = J^\star$ on the same set, the exact tail. **Reading the table.** Design A at $N = 1$ sees only the stage cost and coasts: $u = 0$ minimises $q(x, u)$ whenever $Ax \in \mathcal X$, so from $(-1, 2)$ the controller drifts to $(1, 2)$ and $(3, 2)$ and then finds no admissible input, and the closed loop fails from 702 of its 703 feasible starts, all but the origin. At $N = 2$ the six doomed feasible states, those outside $\mathcal C_\infty$ that the two-step problem still accepts, fail, and the rest converge at up to 2.47 times the optimal cost. From $N = 3$ on nothing visibly goes wrong, and from $N = 5$ the closed loop is optimal everywhere, but nothing certifies that threshold: it was found by running every start. Designs B and C never fail, because $\mathcal X_f = \mathcal O_\infty$ is control invariant on the lattice (all 117 of its lattice states have a lattice input that stays inside); their feasible set grows through 313, 449, 561, 629 to all 655 viable states at $N = 5$, and at $N = 1$ the closed loop is already within 0.33 percent of optimal on average.

**What the certificate needs.** The guarantees rest on the decrease condition $\bar V_f \ge T\bar V_f$, and on the lattice only design C passes the three checks that follow from it: $J_N \ge J^\star$ for every $N$, $J_N$ non-increasing in $N$, and every closed-loop cost at most $J_N(x_0)$. Design B fails all three, although its closed loops are almost identical to C's: rounding the LQR input to the lattice breaks the exact decrease $V_f(A_{\mathrm{cl}} x) - V_f(x) = -q(x, -Kx)$ at 114 of the 117 terminal states, by at most 0.0432. A terminal cost valid for the model is not automatically valid for the implementation; here the implementation is the lattice, on a real system it is the quantised actuator. The terminal set buys safety; the terminal cost buys performance at short horizons; the decrease condition is what turns observations into theorems.

## Learning MPC learns an upper bound of the value {#lmpc}

Chapter [[a13]] stores the realised cost-to-go $J_t^i = \sum_{k \ge t} q(x^i[k], u^i[k])$ of every state visited in a successful iteration $i$. The visited states form the sampled safe set $SS^j$, and $V^j(x) = \min\{J_t^i : x^i[t] = x\}$ is the terminal cost on $\mathcal X_f = SS^j$. Two facts place it in the dynamic-programming picture.

**Realised costs are upper bounds.** $J_t^i$ is the cost of an admissible trajectory to the target, so $V^j \ge J^\star$ on $SS^j$. Data approach $J^\star$ from above; value iteration from $J_0 = 0$ approaches it from below.

**The data satisfy the terminal decrease.** If $V^j(x) = J_t^i$, the stored successor $x^i[t+1] \in SS^j$ gives $V^j(x) = q(x, u^i[t]) + J_{t+1}^i \ge q(x, u^i[t]) + V^j(x^i[t+1]) \ge (T\bar V^j)(x)$. The decrease fact of [[#terminal|Part 2]] then gives recursive feasibility and a closed-loop cost at most $J_N(x_S) \le V^j(x_S)$, the previous iteration's cost: iteration costs never increase, chapter [[a13]]'s main result in operator form.

**On the lattice.** From $x_S = (-4, 0)$, where $J^\star(x_S) = 34.500$, a sluggish feasible trajectory seeds the iterations: the optimal policy for the energy-dominated cost $0.005\, x^\top Q x + u^2$, 10 steps long and costing 66.456, 92.6 percent above $J^\star$. LMPC is then the greedy policy of $T^{N-1}\bar V^j$ with the sampled, not convexified, safe set.

::: table num
| $N$ | iteration 0 (seed) | iteration 1 | iterations 2 to 8 | final gap to $J^\star$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 66.456 | 66.456 | 66.456 | 92.6 % |
| 2 | 66.456 | 38.369 | 38.369 | 11.2 % |
| 3 | 66.456 | 34.500 | 34.500 | 0 |
:::

With $N = 1$ nothing beats replaying the seed; with $N = 2$ the cost stalls at 38.369; with $N = 3$ the first iteration is already optimal, and its stored costs-to-go lie on the diagonal $J_t^j = J^\star(x^j[t])$ to the last bit, the principle of optimality visible in the data. No stored cost ever lies below $J^\star$ (the smallest difference over all runs is exactly zero) and no iteration cost ever increases. On this lattice, with its sampled safe set, the horizon decides how much of the data is usable; the convex safe set of chapter [[a12]] gives short horizons more room, which lab [[b02]] shows on the same system.

## Control vocabulary, reinforcement-learning vocabulary {#rl}

So far the model was used either to tabulate $J$ or to predict over a horizon. Reinforcement learning works from sampled transitions. The vocabulary differs, the objects do not.

::: table
| Control | Reinforcement learning |
|---|---|
| plant, controller $\mu$ | environment, agent with policy $\pi$ |
| input $u$ | action $a$ |
| stage cost $q(x, u)$ | negative reward $-r$ |
| cost-to-go $J$ (minimised) | value $V = -J$ (maximised) |
| state-input cost-to-go $Q$ | action value $Q$ |
| system identification | model learning |
| solve DP with a known model | planning |
| infer a value or a policy from data | learning |
| repeated task, iteration | episode |
:::

**The Q-function.** Define the state-input cost-to-go and read off the value and the policy:

$$Q^\star(x, u) = q(x, u) + \gamma J^\star\big( f(x, u) \big), \qquad J^\star(x) = \min_u Q^\star(x, u), \qquad \mu^\star(x) \in \arg\min_u Q^\star(x, u).$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} Q^\star(x, u) &= q(x, u) + \gamma J^\star( f(x, u) ), \\ J^\star(x) &= \min_u Q^\star(x, u), \quad \mu^\star(x) \in \arg\min_u Q^\star(x, u). \end{aligned}

With $Q^\star$ in hand, acting optimally needs no model, only a minimisation over inputs. Substituting $J^\star = \min_{u'} Q^\star(\cdot, u')$ gives the Bellman equation for $Q$, $Q^\star(x, u) = q(x, u) + \gamma \min_{u'} Q^\star(f(x, u), u') =: (FQ^\star)(x, u)$, and $F$ is a $\gamma$-contraction by the two-property proof of chapter [[a14]]: it is monotone and shifts constants by $\gamma$.

## Model-free: tabular Q-learning {#qlearning}

After observing one transition $(x, u, c, x^+)$ of the real system, update a table:

$$\boxed{Q(x, u) \leftarrow Q(x, u) + \alpha \Big[ c + \gamma \min_{u'} Q(x^+, u') - Q(x, u) \Big].}$$

The bracket is the *temporal-difference error*; its target is a sample of $(FQ)(x, u)$, exact for deterministic transitions, and just $c$ after a crash. Convergence (Watkins and Dayan [[ref:4]], Tsitsiklis [[ref:5]]): with finite sets, bounded costs and $\gamma < 1$, if every pair is updated infinitely often with step sizes satisfying $\sum \alpha_t = \infty$, $\sum \alpha_t^2 < \infty$, then $Q \to Q^\star$ with probability one, as a noisy asynchronous iteration of $F$. Exploration is thus an assumption, met by $\varepsilon$-greedy inputs or by optimistic initialisation ($Q_0 = 0$ lies below every $Q^\star$ here, so untried inputs look attractive). Learning is off-policy: the limit is $Q^\star$ whatever policy generated the data.

**On the lattice.** The learner faces the discounted crash problem of chapter [[a14]], $h = 0.5$, $\gamma = 0.95$, crash cost 100, but only sees transitions. Episodes start at random states, a luxury that real systems rarely allow, and end after 30 steps or at a crash. The score is the fraction of the 171 viable states at which the greedy input is optimal. The widget runs five configurations with its own random stream; the numbers script's runs with NumPy's stream, seed 0, are drawn dotted for comparison.

**The script's runs.** With $\alpha = 1$ and $\varepsilon = 0.1$ the greedy policy is optimal everywhere after 40 000 transitions and 972 crashes; value iteration needed 6 sweeps, 6 450 backups, and no crash. The decaying $\alpha = n^{-0.6}$ needs 68 000 transitions; the constant $\alpha = 0.1$ is still at 93 percent after 200 000. After the first run, $Q = Q^\star$ at the optimal input of every viable state, while other pairs are off by up to 95.0: accurate only where it matters.

## Function approximation, DQN and what the contraction no longer covers {#dqn}

DQN (Mnih et al. [[ref:7]]) replaces the table by a network $Q_\theta$ over a finite input set and minimises the squared temporal-difference error

$$\mathcal L(\theta) = \mathbb E_{(x, u, c, x^+) \sim \mathcal D} \Big[ \big( c + \gamma \min_{u'} Q_{\theta^-}(x^+, u') - Q_\theta(x, u) \big)^2 \Big].$$
%narrow \mathcal L(\theta) = \mathbb E_{\mathcal D} \big[ ( c + \gamma \min_{u'} Q_{\theta^-}(x^+, u') - Q_\theta(x, u) )^2 \big]

The replay buffer $\mathcal D$ decorrelates the samples and reuses costly data; the target network $\theta^-$, refreshed only occasionally, makes each phase a regression towards a fixed target. **Why the contraction argument no longer applies.** With exact regressions DQN would iterate $Q_{k+1} = \Pi F Q_k$, where $\Pi$ projects onto the network class in a norm weighted by the replay distribution. $F$ contracts in the sup-norm, but $\Pi$ is non-expansive only in its weighted $L^2$ norm, so $\Pi F$ need not contract in any norm; with bootstrapping and off-policy data, divergence is possible (Tsitsiklis and Van Roy [[ref:6]]). The minimum over noisy estimates is also biased low, which double Q-learning corrects [[ref:8]]. Chapter [[a14]] says what is approximated, not that the approximation converges.

::: callout warm
**Compared with MPC and LMPC.** DQN learns a global $Q$ and meets constraints only through penalties, by violating them during learning. MPC keeps the model, enforces the constraints online and needs only a local tail approximation, which Learning MPC learns from its own data as a guaranteed upper bound. The lattice makes the trade explicit: 6 450 backups and no crash, or 40 000 transitions and 972 crashes, for the same policy.
:::

## The code behind it {#code}

The closed loops of the hero come from two routines of the course's browser toolkit, `interactive/mpc-dp.js`, repeated in the numbers script. The first runs a policy table from every state of a feasible set and records whether the loop reached the origin without ever meeting an infeasible problem:

```js
function recedingHorizon(L, kappa, F, steps = 80) {
  const out = [];
  for (let s0 = 0; s0 < L.n; s0++) {
    if (!F[s0]) continue;
    let s = s0, cost = 0, ok = true;
    for (let t = 0; t < steps; t++) {
      ok = ok && F[s];                       // the MPC problem must be feasible at every visit
      const a = kappa[s];
      if (ok) cost += L.q[s][a];
      const sn = L.succ[s][a];
      ok = ok && sn >= 0;
      if (sn >= 0) s = sn;
    }
    out.push({ start: s0, cost, ok: ok && s === L.origin });
  }
  return out;
}
```

`F[s]` is the feasible set of the $N$-step problem, the finite-cost states of $T^N \bar V_f$; `kappa` its greedy policy. The line `ok = ok && F[s]` is recursive feasibility checked by brute force, and the final `s === L.origin` is convergence. The second routine builds $J_N$ for every horizon by repeated Bellman steps, runs the loops, and evaluates the three checks that the decrease condition implies:

```js
function designTable(L, Vf, Jstar, fin, Nmax = 12) {
  let Jprev = Vf.slice(); const rows = []; let upper = true, mono = true, bounded = true;
  for (let N = 1; N <= Nmax; N++) {
    const { TJ, act } = bellmanHard(L, Jprev), F = TJ.map(Number.isFinite);
    const rh = recedingHorizon(L, act, F);
    rows.push({ N, feasible: F.filter(Boolean).length,
      doomed: F.filter((f, i) => f && !fin[i]).length, failed: rh.filter(r => !r.ok).length,
      optimal: rh.filter(r => r.ok && Math.abs(r.cost - Jstar[r.start]) < 1e-9).length });
    upper = upper && TJ.every((v, i) => !F[i] || v >= Jstar[i] - 1e-9);       // J_N >= J*
    mono = mono && TJ.every((v, i) => v <= Jprev[i] + 1e-9);                   // J_N non-increasing in N
    bounded = bounded && rh.every(r => !r.ok || r.cost <= TJ[r.start] + 1e-9); // closed-loop cost <= J_N(x0)
    Jprev = TJ;
  }
  return { rows, upper, mono, bounded };
}
```

`Jprev` carries $T^{N-1}\bar V_f$ from one horizon to the next, so the whole table costs twelve sweeps. The Q-learning runner is the update rule of [[#qlearning|Part 6]] inside an episode loop:

```js
const target = s2 < 0 ? c : c + Pb.gamma * Math.min(...Qt[s2]);   // the temporal-difference target
visits[s][a]++;
const step = cfg.alpha === "decay" ? visits[s][a] ** -0.6 : cfg.alpha;
row[a] += step * (target - row[a]);
if (s2 < 0 || tEp === 30) { s = Math.floor(r() * n); tEp = 0; } else s = s2;
```

A crash (`s2 < 0`) has the target `c` alone, since the crash state is free and absorbing, and restarts the episode at a random state, as does the 30-step limit. The decaying step size `visits ** -0.6` satisfies the two convergence conditions; the constant ones do not, which is harmless on a deterministic problem with $\alpha = 1$ and slow with $\alpha = 0.1$. Finally, the stored costs-to-go of [[#lmpc|Part 4]] are the package's own recursion:

::: code advanced_mpc_lmpc/lmpc.py:trajectory_cost_to_go
The backward loop `J[k] = stage_cost(...) + J[k + 1]` with `J[T] = 0` is dynamic programming along one realised trajectory instead of over the whole state space: it needs $T$ evaluations rather than a table, and it produces an upper bound of $J^\star$ at every visited state, since the trajectory it sums is admissible but not necessarily optimal. The check `len(X) != len(U) + 1` enforces the convention of chapter [[a01]], one more state than inputs.
:::

## Common pitfalls {#pitfalls}

1. **"The contraction proof covers MPC."** No. It needs bounded costs and $\gamma < 1$; MPC is undiscounted with infinite constraint costs, and rests on monotonicity and the terminal decrease.
2. **"A long horizon without terminal ingredients is a safe default."** $T^N 0$ underestimates $J^\star$ and its domain contains doomed states: 48 at $N = 1$, 6 at $N = 2$. It works eventually, but nothing certifies when.
3. **"A terminal cost valid for the model is valid for the implementation."** $x^\top P x$ on $\mathcal O_\infty$ is exact for the continuous problem, but restricting the inputs to the lattice broke its decrease at 114 of 117 states. A quantised actuator does the same.
4. **"LMPC's terminal cost is a Q-function."** It is a state function, an upper bound of $J^\star$ built from realised costs, not a state-input function learned by bootstrapping.
5. **"A penalty is a constraint."** It leaves the optimal policy unchanged only if it is large enough, and a learner respects it only after violating it: 972 crashes before the policy became optimal.
6. **"Q-learning converged, so the network will too."** The tabular proof is a stochastic approximation of a sup-norm contraction; a projected iteration need not contract in any norm. Replay and target networks are remedies, not guarantees.

## Check your understanding {#check}

:::: qa
Try to answer each question before opening it.

::: q 1. Describe MPC in dynamic-programming terms. Why does $V_f = x^\top P x$ on $\mathcal O_\infty$ allow a short horizon?
MPC is greedy with respect to $T^{N-1}\bar V_f$, evaluated at the current state. If $V_f = J^\star$ on $\mathcal X_f$, as $x^\top P x$ is on $\mathcal O_\infty$ for the constrained linear-quadratic problem, then $T^N\bar V_f = J^\star$ wherever an optimal trajectory enters $\mathcal X_f$ within $N$ steps. The decrease $\bar V_f \ge T\bar V_f$ then gives recursive feasibility and a Lyapunov function.
:::
::: q 2. Why does design A fail from almost every state at $N = 1$?
With $V_f = 0$ the one-step problem sees only $q(x, u)$, minimised by $u = 0$ whenever coasting stays in the box. The controller coasts until a bound forces it to brake, then brakes as little as possible, and from most states that is too late: 702 of 703 feasible starts lose feasibility.
:::
::: q 3. Design B's closed loops are almost identical to design C's. Why does only C carry a certificate?
The certificate needs $\bar V_f \ge T\bar V_f$. Design C's terminal cost is the exact $J^\star$, which satisfies it by definition. Design B's is the continuous LQR cost, whose decrease holds for the continuous input $-Kx$ but fails once the input is rounded to the lattice, at 114 of 117 terminal states.
:::
::: q 4. Why are LMPC's stored costs-to-go upper bounds of $J^\star$, and why does that matter?
Each is the realised cost of an admissible trajectory to the target, so it is at least optimal. Each also comes with a stored feasible continuation, so $\bar V^j \ge T\bar V^j$, which yields recursive feasibility and non-increasing iteration costs. A lower bound such as $T^N 0$ gives neither.
:::
::: q 5. Why does the horizon decide what LMPC can learn on the lattice?
The sampled safe set is a finite set of points; a plan must end exactly on one of them. With $N = 1$ the only reachable stored state is the seed's next state, so the seed is replayed; with $N = 3$ enough stored states are reachable that the optimal path can be assembled from the first iteration.
:::
::: q 6. What is the temporal-difference error, and why is its target exact here?
The bracket $c + \gamma \min_{u'} Q(x^+, u') - Q(x, u)$. Its target is a sample of $(FQ)(x, u)$; with deterministic transitions the sample is the expectation, so $\alpha = 1$ is admissible and fastest.
:::
::: q 7. Why does the pessimistic initialisation fail without exploration?
A crash target is the one-step cost alone, at most 118, while every other target bootstraps from successors still valued near 200. Crashing therefore looks cheaper than staying, the greedy policy keeps crashing, and without exploration nothing corrects the values along the way: 70 217 crashes and a policy that crashes from 29 viable states.
:::
::: q 8. What does a DQN target network do, and why does the tabular convergence proof not carry over?
It freezes the parameters used in the targets, so each phase is a regression towards a fixed target. With function approximation the update is $\Pi F$, and since $\Pi$ is non-expansive only in a weighted $L^2$ norm, $\Pi F$ need not contract and can diverge.
:::

:::: exercise Build the three checks
Starting from the lattice and `bellman_hard`, implement the three checks of the decrease condition for design B ($J_N \ge J^\star$, $J_N$ non-increasing in $N$, closed-loop cost at most $J_N(x_0)$) and find the first horizon at which each one fails.
::: hint
Keep $T^{N-1}\bar V_f$ from one horizon to the next; the closed-loop costs come from `receding_horizon`.

```python
Vf = np.where(in_O, xPx4, np.inf)            # design B
J_prev = Vf.copy()
for N in range(1, 13):
    J_N, a_N = bellman_hard(J_prev)
    F = np.isfinite(J_N)
    start, cost, ok = receding_horizon(a_N, F)
    print(N, bool(np.all(J_N[F] >= J_star4[F] - 1e-9)), bool(np.all(J_N <= J_prev + 1e-9)),
```

:::: exercise Build Q-learning yourself
Implement the tabular update with $\alpha = 1$, $\varepsilon = 0.1$, episodes of at most 30 steps from random starts, and NumPy's `default_rng(0)` drawn exactly as in the numbers script (exploration flags, random actions and starts drawn in advance). Reproduce: optimal everywhere from step 40 000, 972 crashes by then, 2 776 in all.
::: hint
Draw `explore`, `rand_a` and `starts` as three arrays before the loop; the order of the draws fixes the stream.
:::
::: solution
```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
explore = rng.random(n_steps) < 0.1
rand_a = rng.integers(m3, size=n_steps)
starts = rng.integers(n3, size=n_steps)
Q = [[0.0] * m3 for _ in range(n3)]
s, t_ep, crashes = int(starts[0]), 0, 0
for t in range(n_steps):
    row = Q[s]
    a = int(rand_a[t]) if explore[t] else row.index(min(row))
    s2, c = succ3[s, a], cost_c[s, a]
    target = c if s2 < 0 else c + gamma * min(Q[s2])
    row[a] += 1.0 * (target - row[a])
    crashes += s2 < 0; t_ep += 1
    if s2 < 0 or t_ep == 30: s, t_ep = int(starts[t]), 0
    else: s = s2
```
Plain lists are deliberate: the updates are scalar and a NumPy row would cost more per step than it saves. The browser's run uses its own generator, so its step counts differ from these while the conclusions, and the final $Q$ at the optimal inputs, do not.

:::: exercise A penalty that is too small
Lower the crash cost in the value-iteration widget of chapter [[a14]] (or in the numbers script) until the optimal policy of the crash problem crashes from some viable state. What is the mechanism, and what does it say about penalties as constraints?
::: hint
Compare the crash cost with the discounted cost of staying in the box for ever from a fast state near the edge.

::: solution
Below a crash cost of about 40 the optimal policy prefers to leave the box from some viable states: from a fast state near a wall, braking hard and then paying $x^\top Q x$ for many steps costs more than $c_{\mathrm{crash}}$ once and nothing after, since the crash state is free. A penalty encodes a constraint only when it exceeds the worst admissible cost-to-go; at 100 it does (the largest viable value is 47.06), at 40 it does not. :::: exercise Where is Q accurate?
After the $\alpha = 1$, $\varepsilon = 0.1$ run, compare the learned $Q$ with $Q^\star$ at the optimal input of each viable state and over all pairs. Explain the two numbers.

`Q_star3 = cost_c + gamma * J_star3[succ_c]`; the optimal input is `Q_star3.argmin(axis=1)`.

## The course's methods on one page {#summary}

::: table
| Method | Computes | Where | Model | Guarantees from |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dynamic programming (VI, PI) | $J^\star$, $\mu^\star$ exactly | every state, offline | yes | contraction or monotonicity |
| MPC | the greedy policy of $T^{N-1}\bar V_f$ | current state, online | yes | terminal set and decrease |
| Learning MPC | MPC with $V_f$ from realised costs | current state, online | yes | the data satisfy the decrease |
| Q-learning | $Q^\star$ from samples | visited pairs | no | stochastic approximation |
| DQN | $Q_\theta \approx Q^\star$ | wherever the network generalises | no | no general guarantee |
:::

::: steps
- **MPC** is greedy with respect to $T^{N-1}\bar V_f$; $\bar V_f \ge T\bar V_f$ is its stability proof in one line. The set buys safety, the cost buys short-horizon performance.
- **Three designs**: without terminal ingredients, 702 of 703 starts fail at $N = 1$ and doomed states are accepted at $N = 2$; with them, no start ever fails and the feasible set reaches all 655 viable states at $N = 5$. Only the exact tail carries the certificate on the lattice.
- **Learning MPC** stores realised costs-to-go: upper bounds of $J^\star$ that satisfy the decrease automatically, so iteration costs never increase; from 92.6 percent above optimal, $N = 3$ reaches the optimum in one iteration.
- **Q-learning**: samples and exploration instead of a model; 40 000 transitions and 972 crashes against 6 450 backups and none; accurate where it acts.
- **DQN**: a projected contraction need not contract; replay and a target network are remedies, not a proof.
:::

### The vocabulary, in one table

| Term | Meaning here |
|---|---|
| Truncated dynamic programming | MPC: greedy with respect to $T^{N-1}\bar V_f$ at the current state. |
| Terminal decrease | $\bar V_f \ge T\bar V_f$: the stability condition of chapter A11 in operator form. |
| Doomed state | Feasible for the plain problem now, outside $\mathcal C_\infty$, bound to leave $\mathcal X$. |
| Sampled safe set | The states of successful runs, with their realised costs-to-go as a terminal cost. |
| Q-function | State-input cost-to-go; the greedy policy needs no model. |
| Temporal-difference error | The sampled Bellman residual used by Q-learning and DQN. |
| Off-policy | The limit is $Q^\star$ whatever policy generated the data. |
| Replay buffer, target network | DQN's remedies for correlated samples and moving targets. |

**Scope.** Deterministic, discrete-time problems on an exact lattice; with noise, $J(f(x, u))$ becomes an expectation and MPC needs the robust or stochastic formulations of lab [[b06]]. All lattice computations on this page run in the browser with the same code as the numbers script; only the Q-learning random stream differs, and the script's logs are shipped for comparison.

## The problem, and what makes it nonlinear {#problem}

**The unicycle.** A robot at position $(X, Y)$ with heading $\theta$, driven by a forward speed $v$ and a turning rate $\omega$: $\dot X = v\cos\theta$, $\dot Y = v\sin\theta$, $\dot\theta = \omega$. One Euler step of length $T_s = 0.2$ is

$$z_{k+1} = f(z_k, u_k) = \begin{pmatrix} X_k + T_s v_k \cos\theta_k \\ Y_k + T_s v_k \sin\theta_k \\ \theta_k + T_s \omega_k \end{pmatrix}, \qquad z = (X, Y, \theta),\quad u = (v, \omega).$$
%narrow z_{k+1} = \begin{pmatrix} X_k + T_s v_k \cos\theta_k \\ Y_k + T_s v_k \sin\theta_k \\ \theta_k + T_s \omega_k \end{pmatrix}

**The task of chapter [[a05]].** Reach the pose $z_f = (2, 1, 0)$ from $z_0 = (0, 0, 0)$ in exactly $N = 30$ steps, with $|v_k| \le 1$, $|\omega_k| \le 1$ and $|u_k - u_{k-1}| \le 0.2$ componentwise, minimising effort and roughness,

$$J(U) = 0.05 \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \lVert u_k \rVert^2 + 0.10 \sum_{k=1}^{N-1} \lVert u_k - u_{k-1} \rVert^2 .$$

**Where the nonlinearity sits.** The cost is a quadratic in the stacked inputs $U$, the input and rate bounds are linear in $U$. Only one thing is not: the final pose $z_N$ depends on $U$ through thirty compositions of $f$, with the heading entering through $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$. The terminal equality $z_N(U) = z_f$ is a nonlinear equation in $U$, and that one equation makes the whole problem a nonlinear program: there is no prediction identity $X = \mathcal S_x x_0 + \mathcal S_u U$ as in chapter [[a02]], and chapter [[a05]] had to hand the problem to SciPy's SLSQP, a sequential quadratic programming method, which found cost $0.3167$ from the guess "drive, turn, unwind" (ten steps at $v = 0.8$, $\omega = 0.3$, ten at $0.6$, $-0.3$, ten at $0.4$, $0$). That guess costs $0.7230$ and ends at $(3.44, 0.80, 0)$, a whole 1.44 past the target in $X$.

::: define What is successive linearisation?
**In plain words:** pretend the robot is linear around the plan you have, solve the resulting QP for a correction to the plan, apply the correction, and repeat with the corrected plan as the new linearisation point. Each round is a problem the course already knows how to solve; the rounds converge to a plan that satisfies the true, nonlinear pose constraint.

**More precisely:** with a plan $\bar U$ and its rollout $\bar Z$, the dynamics are replaced by their first-order expansion $\delta z_{k+1} = A_k \delta z_k + B_k \delta u_k$ along $(\bar Z, \bar U)$, the terminal map by $z_N(\bar U + \delta U) \approx \bar z_N + M \delta U$ with $M = \partial z_N / \partial U$, and the problem by the QP $\min_{\delta U} J(\bar U + \delta U)$ subject to the bounds on $\bar U + \delta U$ and $M \delta U = z_f - \bar z_N$. Then $\bar U \leftarrow \bar U + \delta U$. It is sequential quadratic programming without the second-order terms of the constraint, a Gauss–Newton iteration on the terminal map.

- Linearisation point :: The current plan and its rollout: the place where the Jacobians are evaluated.
- Defect $z_f - \bar z_N$ :: How far the current plan ends from the pose; the right-hand side of the linearised equality.
- Correction $\delta U$ :: The QP's decision variable: the change to make to the plan.
- Real-time iteration :: One such QP per sample, in closed loop, instead of iterating to convergence.
:::

## Linearise along the plan {#linearise}

**The Jacobians.** Differentiating the Euler step at a point $(z, u)$,

$$A_k = \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -T_s v_k \sin\theta_k \\ 0 & 1 & T_s v_k \cos\theta_k \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B_k = \frac{\partial f}{\partial u} = \begin{pmatrix} T_s \cos\theta_k & 0 \\ T_s \sin\theta_k & 0 \\ 0 & T_s \end{pmatrix}.$$
%narrow A_k = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -T_s v_k \sin\theta_k \\ 0 & 1 & T_s v_k \cos\theta_k \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B_k = \begin{pmatrix} T_s \cos\theta_k & 0 \\ T_s \sin\theta_k & 0 \\ 0 & T_s \end{pmatrix}

Read them: a change of heading moves the next position sideways, in proportion to the speed; a change of speed moves it along the current heading; the turning rate acts only on the heading. At step 10 of the guess, where $\theta = 0.600$ and $v = 0.6$, the third column of $A_{10}$ is $(-0.0678, 0.0990, 1)$ and the first column of $B_{10}$ is $(0.1651, 0.1129, 0)$. The matrices change along the plan: the model is linear time-varying, the LTV class of chapter [[a01]].

**Time-varying prediction matrices.** Chapter [[a02]] stacked the states of an LTI system with powers of $A$. With a different $A_k$ at every step the powers become products, and the identity reads

$$\begin{pmatrix} \delta z_1 \\ \delta z_2 \\ \vdots \\ \delta z_N \end{pmatrix} = \mathcal S_x \delta z_0 + \mathcal S_u \delta U, \qquad [\mathcal S_u]_{i, j} = A_{i-1} A_{i-2} \cdots A_{j+1} B_j \quad (j < i), \qquad [\mathcal S_u]_{i, i} = B_i .$$
%narrow [\mathcal S_u]_{i, j} = A_{i-1} \cdots A_{j+1} B_j,\ j < i;\quad [\mathcal S_u]_{i,i} = B_i

For the pose only the last block row matters, $M = \partial z_N / \partial U$, a $3 \times 60$ matrix whose block $j$ is $A_{N-1} \cdots A_{j+1} B_j$: how an input change at step $j$, propagated through every later step, moves the final pose. Along the guess, perturbing the inputs by $10^{-4}$ and comparing the linear prediction with the true rollout gives an error of $9.6 \times 10^{-9}$: second order in the perturbation, as a first-order expansion should be. The matrix $M$ has rank 3 there, so every direction of the pose can be reached by a first-order change of the inputs.

## One QP in the correction {#step}

With the plan $\bar U$ and its rollout $\bar Z$, the step solves

$$\min_{\delta U}\ \tfrac12\, \delta U^\top H \delta U + (H \bar U)^\top \delta U \quad \text{s.t.}\quad -1 \le \bar U + \delta U \le 1, \quad |D(\bar U + \delta U)| \le 0.2, \quad M \delta U = z_f - \bar z_N ,$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} \min_{\delta U}\ & \tfrac12\, \delta U^\top H \delta U + (H \bar U)^\top \delta U \\ \text{s.t. } & -1 \le \bar U + \delta U \le 1, \\ & |D(\bar U + \delta U)| \le 0.2, \\ & M \delta U = z_f - \bar z_N . \end{aligned}

where $H = 2(0.05\, I + 0.10\, D^\top D)$ is the cost's Hessian and $D$ the difference matrix of chapter [[a02]], so that $J(\bar U + \delta U) = J(\bar U) + (H\bar U)^\top \delta U + \tfrac12 \delta U^\top H \delta U$ exactly, with no approximation at all. The bounds are linear in $\delta U$ since $\bar U$ is a constant. The only approximation is the last row: the pose at the end of the corrected plan is predicted by the linearisation, $\bar z_N + M \delta U$, and asked to equal $z_f$; the right-hand side is the defect, how far the current plan misses.

This is a convex QP with 60 variables, 116 inequality rows and 3 equalities, handed to the solver of chapter [[a04]], which returns the correction and, with it, the multipliers of the three pose rows. After the update $\bar U \leftarrow \bar U + \delta U$ the true rollout ends somewhere else than the linear prediction said, because the prediction was first order; the next iteration linearises there. Convergence means that the defect, and with it the correction, go to zero.

## Fifteen steps to the pose {#convergence}

From chapter A5's guess the iteration goes like this (the numbers script prints the table):

::: table num
| iteration | cost | terminal residual | largest $\lvert \delta U \rvert$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.270984 | $1.85 \times 10^{-1}$ | 0.476 |
| 2 | 0.317834 | $7.84 \times 10^{-3}$ | 0.115 |
| 3 | 0.316598 | $5.89 \times 10^{-4}$ | 0.038 |
| 4 | 0.316679 | $1.17 \times 10^{-4}$ | $8.3 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| 5 | 0.316694 | $1.92 \times 10^{-5}$ | $3.7 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| 7 | 0.316697 | $6.02 \times 10^{-7}$ | $6.0 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| 10 | 0.316697 | $3.21 \times 10^{-9}$ | $3.8 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| 15 | 0.316697 | $4.97 \times 10^{-13}$ | $4.9 \times 10^{-7}$ |
:::

**Read the table.** The first step is large: it repairs the guess's rate violations (the guess jumps $\omega$ from $0.3$ to $-0.3$, three times the allowed rate) and moves the end of the plan from 1.44 off to 0.19 off, at a cost that is still too low because the linear prediction overestimated what the step achieves. From the second step on, the residual falls by a factor of about six per iteration: $0.075, 0.199, 0.164, 0.187, 0.168, 0.179$ between consecutive rows. A constant factor is linear convergence. Newton's method on the full KKT system, with the second derivatives of the terminal map in the Lagrangian's Hessian, would converge quadratically; the Gauss–Newton step drops those terms, and pays with a few more iterations, each of which is a convex QP that the solver finishes in 11 Newton steps of its own.

**The answer.** After 15 iterations the pose is met to $5 \times 10^{-13}$ and the cost is $0.316697$; the whole run takes about 60 ms. No input or rate bound is active at the solution: the largest speed is $0.4585$, the largest turning rate $0.3919$, the largest rate $0.0298$ against $0.2$. The robot pulls away at $(v, \omega) = (0.224, 0.390)$, turning hard while slow, carries most of its speed through the middle at $v \approx 0.45$ with the heading unwinding, and arrives aligned.

**SLSQP, for comparison.** SciPy's general-purpose sequential quadratic programming, from the same guess: 27 iterations, about three times the time, cost $0.316697$, pose met to $5 \times 10^{-12}$, and inputs that agree with the successive-linearisation plan to $6.7 \times 10^{-6}$. The two methods are the same idea; SLSQP additionally updates a quasi-Newton approximation of the Lagrangian's Hessian and takes a line search, which is what a general solver must do when it knows nothing about the problem's structure. Here the structure is known, the cost is exactly quadratic, and the simplest version suffices. From the straight-ahead guess $v = 0.5$ the iteration needs 11 steps and lands on the same plan to $2.4 \times 10^{-7}$.

## When the linearised equality cannot be met {#elastic}

**Two guesses the hard equality cannot start from.** From all-zero inputs the first QP is infeasible. The reason is in the Jacobians: with $v = 0$ and $\theta = 0$ along the whole plan, the third column of every $A_k$ is zero and the second row of every $B_k$ is zero, so no first-order change of the inputs moves $Y$. The matrix $M$ has rank 2 and the row $\delta Y_N = 1$ has no solution: a parked robot's sideways position is invisible to the linearised model. From random inputs in $[-0.5, 0.5]$ the rank is 3, but the defect of about 2 asks for a correction that the box $|u| \le 1$ and the rate bounds cannot deliver in one step. In both cases the hard equality is inconsistent with the other constraints, and the solver's independent certificate of chapter [[a04]] says so.

**The elastic mode.** Replace the equality by $M \delta U + s^+ - s^- = z_f - \bar z_N$ with slacks $s^\pm \ge 0$, and add $\rho\, \mathbf 1^\top (s^+ + s^-)$ to the cost: the $\ell_1$ exact penalty of chapter [[a05]], applied to the pose. The QP is now always feasible, and whenever the pose can be reached at a multiplier below $\rho$ the slacks are zero and the solution is unchanged. The multipliers of the three pose rows at the solution are $(-0.093, -0.261, 0.210)$, the price of moving the required pose by one unit in $X$, $Y$ and $\theta$; so any $\rho$ above $0.261$ is exact. With $\rho = 20$ the iteration from the guess takes the same 15 steps to the same plan, to $6 \times 10^{-14}$; from all zeros it takes 13 and from random inputs 19, all to the same cost $0.316697$. With $\rho = 1$ it still converges in 15; with $\rho = 0.1$, below the multiplier, it settles after 50 iterations on a plan that misses the pose by $0.85$ at the cheaper cost $0.0797$: missing costs less than reaching, which is what a penalty that is not exact permits. The widget above draws the two paths.

**A trust region.** The step can also be bounded, $|\delta U| \le \Delta$ entrywise, to keep the linearisation honest when the guess is poor. With $\rho = 20$ and $\Delta = 0.25$ the iteration from the guess is unchanged, 15 steps; with $\Delta = 0.1$ the first QP is infeasible, because the guess's rate violation of $0.6$ cannot be repaired within the radius. On this problem the full step never overshoots and no trust region is needed; on a problem where it does, the standard remedy is to shrink $\Delta$ when the true cost or residual after the step is worse than the linear prediction promised, and to grow it when the prediction was good.

**A target behind the start.** For $z_f = (0, 1, \pi)$, chapter A5's second target, the hard equality converges in 11 iterations to cost $0.4941$, the elastic mode likewise, and SLSQP found the same. A reversing manoeuvre is no harder for the method than a forward one.

## The real-time iteration: one QP per sample {#rti}

Nonlinear MPC in closed loop would, at every sample, iterate the linearisation to convergence from the measured state and apply the first move. The real-time iteration does less: one linearisation and one QP per sample, along the plan shifted from the previous sample, and applies the first move of the corrected plan. The iterations that converge the open-loop problem are spread across the samples of the closed loop, and because the measured state moves only a little between samples, the one step per sample keeps up.

**The experiment.** The horizon shrinks with the time left, $N - t$ moves at sample $t$, so that the pose is still required at step 30. The first move is rate-bounded against the input just applied, which the open-loop problem had no need for. The pose constraint is elastic with $\rho = 20$, because a push late in the run can make the pose unreachable within the input and rate bounds in the samples that remain, and the QP must still return a move. At the chosen sample the state is pushed by $(0, 0.15, 0.10)$, a sideways shove and a twist.

::: table num
| push | at sample | final pose error | closed-loop cost | bounds respected |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| none | – | $0$ | 0.3177 | yes |
| $(0, 0.15, 0.10)$ | 10 | $0$ | 0.2886 | yes |
| $(0, 0.40, 0.30)$ | 10 | $0$ | 0.3409 | yes |
| $(0, 0.15, 0.10)$ | 25 | $(0, 0.139, 0)$ | 0.3750 | yes |
| $(0, 0.15, 0.10)$ | 28 | $(0, 0.154, 0.065)$ | 0.3307 | yes |
| noise of 0.01 on every state | every | $(0.008, 0.012, 0.015)$ | 0.3601 | yes |
:::

**Read the table.** Without a push the closed loop re-solves the same problem from the states the plan predicted and costs $0.3177$, a hair above the open-loop $0.3167$ because the one QP per sample is a step short of convergence at every sample. A push at sample 10 leaves twenty samples to recover, and the loop reaches the pose exactly; the shove in the right direction even lowers the cost, since it does part of the turning for free. A push at sample 25 leaves five samples, and the pose is missed by $0.139$ in $Y$: with rates bounded by $0.2$ and $\omega$ by 1, the robot cannot turn and side-step that far in one second, and the elastic constraint reports the shortfall instead of an infeasible QP. A weaker penalty gives up more of the pose for a cheaper loop: $\rho = 2$ misses by $0.146$ at cost $0.3555$, $\rho = 0.5$ by $0.154$ at $0.3451$. Iterating to convergence at every sample instead, 153 QPs in all rather than 30, reproduces the one-QP loop's result for the push at sample 10 to the fourth decimal: the extra QPs buy nothing here.

## What is and is not certified {#guarantees}

::: callout warm
**Convergence of the iteration is local, and the closed loop has no stability theorem.** The successive linearisation converges to a point where the linearised problem has no correction, a KKT point of the nonlinear program, and only when started close enough to one; the elastic mode and the trust region widen the basin, they do not make it global. Chapter [[a05]]'s lesson stands: agreeing restarts, here the guess, straight ahead, zeros and random inputs all reaching $0.316697$, are evidence about this problem, not a proof. The real-time iteration adds a second gap: its closed loop is not the closed loop of the converged nonlinear MPC, and neither has the terminal ingredients of chapter [[a11]]. The recursive feasibility and the Lyapunov decrease proved there for linear MPC are not available; the elastic terminal constraint keeps the QP feasible, which is a property of the optimisation, not of the robot. Nonlinear MPC theory recovers the guarantees with a terminal set and cost built from the linearisation at the target, the quasi-infinite-horizon construction cited in lab [[b06]], and with conditions on how far one QP per sample may lag the converged solution.
:::

What the chapter does certify, numerically: every QP solved has its KKT residual below $10^{-10}$ and its multipliers in hand; the pose is met to round-off by the converged plan; every input and rate bound holds along every closed-loop run in the table; and the plan agrees with an independent solver's. That is the same standard the linear chapters hold themselves to, applied one linearisation at a time.

## The code behind it {#code}

Nothing in this chapter is in the course package except the solver. The numbers script contains the new functions; the demo script holds the same ones in JavaScript. Three of them are quoted here.

```python
def jacobians(z, u):
    """A_k = df/dz and B_k = df/du at the linearisation point (z, u)."""
    th = z[2]; v = u[0]
    A = np.array([[1.0, 0.0, -TS * v * np.sin(th)],
                  [0.0, 1.0, TS * v * np.cos(th)],
                  [0.0, 0.0, 1.0]])
    B = np.array([[TS * np.cos(th), 0.0],
                  [TS * np.sin(th), 0.0],
                  [0.0, TS]])
    return A, B
```

The derivatives of the Euler step, written out rather than obtained by automatic differentiation; for a model with more than three states that is the first thing to automate, and the Kalman course on this site shows how.

```python
def ltv_prediction(As, Bs):
    """[dz_1; ...; dz_N] = Sx dz_0 + Su [du_0; ...; du_{N-1}] for dz_{k+1} = A_k dz_k + B_k du_k."""
    n, m = Bs[0].shape; N = len(As)
    Sx = np.zeros((N * n, n)); Su = np.zeros((N * n, N * m))
    Phi = np.eye(n)
    for i in range(N):
        Phi = As[i] @ Phi                             # A_i ... A_0
        Sx[i * n:(i + 1) * n] = Phi
        P = np.eye(n)                                 # P = A_{i-1} ... A_{j+1}, built backwards in j
        for j in range(i, -1, -1):
            Su[i * n:(i + 1) * n, j * m:(j + 1) * m] = P @ Bs[j]
            P = P @ As[j]
    return Sx, Su
```

Compare with `prediction_matrices` of chapter [[a02]]: the matrix powers have become ordered products, built backwards from the diagonal block $B_i$ so that each product is extended by one factor at a time. The demo needs only the last block row and builds it the same way.

```python
# the core of sqp(): one linearise-and-solve step with the hard terminal equality
Z = rollout(z0, U)
As, Bs = zip(*[jacobians(Z[k], U[k]) for k in range(N)])
Sx, Su = ltv_prediction(list(As), list(Bs))
M = Su[-n:]                                   # d z_N / d U along the plan
defect = zf - Z[-1]                           # how far the plan's end is from the pose
f = H @ U.reshape(-1)                         # J(U + dU) = 0.5 dU'H dU + (H U)' dU + J(U)
lb = -UMAX - U.reshape(-1); ub = UMAX - U.reshape(-1)
res = solve_qp(H, f, A_ub=G, b_ub=g - G @ U.reshape(-1), A_eq=M, b_eq=defect, lb=lb, ub=ub)
U = U + res.x.reshape(N, m)
```

Five lines of setup and one call: the linearisation point, the terminal map and its defect, the cost's linear term at the current plan, the bounds shifted by the current plan, the solve, the update. The elastic mode adds six slack variables and the penalty to the same call; the closed loop calls it with `maxiter=1`.

## Common pitfalls {#pitfalls}

1. **"The QP solved, so the pose is met."** The QP met the *linearised* pose. The true rollout of the corrected plan ends somewhere else, by an amount that is second order in the correction, and only the converged iteration meets the pose itself. Always roll the plan out and measure the defect.
2. **"The cost is approximated too."** Not here: the cost is a quadratic in $U$ and the QP carries it exactly. Only the terminal map is linearised. For a cost that is not quadratic, its Hessian would be approximated as well, and that is what SLSQP's quasi-Newton update is for.
3. **"A linearisation always has a solution."** From a standstill the linearised unicycle cannot move sideways; the rank of $M$ drops and the hard equality is inconsistent. The elastic mode exists for this.
4. **"A small penalty is a soft version of the constraint."** Below the constraint's multiplier the penalty is not exact and the pose is simply missed, by $0.85$ at $\rho = 0.1$. Exactness needs $\rho$ above the multiplier, which the solver reports.
5. **"One QP per sample is an approximation of nonlinear MPC."** It is a different controller, whose closed loop here matches the fully converged one to four decimals, but that agreement was measured, not proved; and neither controller carries the guarantees of chapter [[a11]] without terminal ingredients built for the nonlinear model.

## Check your understanding {#check}

:::: qa
Try to answer each question before opening it.

::: q 1. Which part of the unicycle problem is nonlinear, and which parts are not?
Only the terminal pose $z_N(U) = z_f$, through thirty compositions of the Euler step with its sines and cosines. The cost is an exact quadratic in $U$ and the input and rate bounds are linear in $U$.
:::
::: q 2. What does the entry $-T_s v_k \sin\theta_k$ of $A_k$ say?
That a small change of heading at step $k$ moves the next $X$ position backwards by that amount: the robot drives along a slightly rotated direction, and the effect is proportional to the speed. At $v = 0$ it vanishes, which is why a parked robot's position does not respond to its heading at first order.
:::
::: q 3. Why does the time-varying prediction matrix contain products instead of powers?
Because $A_k$ differs at every step: propagating a change from step $j$ to step $i$ multiplies it by $A_{i-1}$, then $A_{i-2}$, down to $A_{j+1}$, an ordered product that collapses to a power only when all the factors are equal.
:::
::: q 4. What is the defect, and where does it appear in the QP?
The difference $z_f - \bar z_N$ between the required pose and the end of the current plan's true rollout. It is the right-hand side of the linearised terminal equality $M \delta U = z_f - \bar z_N$: the correction must move the predicted end by exactly that amount.
:::
::: q 5. Why does the residual shrink by a constant factor rather than quadratically?
The step uses the Jacobians of the dynamics but not the second derivatives of the terminal map, so it is a Gauss–Newton rather than a Newton step on the KKT conditions. Linear convergence at a rate set by the neglected curvature, about $0.17$ here, is the price; the benefit is that every step is a convex QP with a known Hessian.
:::
::: q 6. When is the elastic penalty exact?
When $\rho$ exceeds the largest multiplier of the pose rows at the solution, $0.261$ here: then reaching the pose is cheaper than paying the penalty for missing it, the slacks are zero, and the solution is that of the hard problem. Below it, the pose is missed by whatever the penalty tolerates.
:::
::: q 7. What does the real-time iteration do differently from nonlinear MPC?
One linearisation and one QP per sample, along the plan shifted from the previous sample, instead of iterating to convergence at every sample. The convergence is spread over the samples; the measured state moves little between them, so one step keeps up, as the matching costs of the two closed loops show here.
:::
::: q 8. Why is the pose missed by 0.139 after a push at sample 25 but met after a push at sample 10?
Because recovering a sideways displacement of 0.15 with a twist of 0.10 needs several samples of turning and driving within $|\omega| \le 1$ and rates of $0.2$ per step; five samples are not enough, twenty are. The elastic constraint turns "infeasible" into "this close, at this cost".
:::

::::: exercises
:::: exercise Derive the Jacobians
Starting from the Euler step, derive $A_k$ and $B_k$ by hand, and check them against `jacobians` with a finite difference at $z = (0.5, 0.2, 0.6)$, $u = (0.6, 0.3)$.
::: hint
Differentiate each of the three components with respect to each of $X, Y, \theta, v, \omega$; only $\theta$ and $v$ appear nonlinearly.
:::
::: solution
$\partial X^+ / \partial\theta = -T_s v\sin\theta$, $\partial Y^+ / \partial\theta = T_s v\cos\theta$, $\partial X^+ / \partial v = T_s\cos\theta$, $\partial Y^+ / \partial v = T_s\sin\theta$, $\partial\theta^+ / \partial\omega = T_s$, and the identity on the diagonal. A central difference with step $10^{-6}$ agrees to about $10^{-10}$.

```python
z, u, h = np.array([0.5, 0.2, 0.6]), np.array([0.6, 0.3]), 1e-6
A, B = jacobians(z, u)
A_fd = np.column_stack([(step(z + h * e, u) - step(z - h * e, u)) / (2 * h) for e in np.eye(3)])
B_fd = np.column_stack([(step(z, u + h * e) - step(z, u - h * e)) / (2 * h) for e in np.eye(2)])
print(np.abs(A - A_fd).max(), np.abs(B - B_fd).max())     # both about 1e-10
```

:::: exercise Build it: the terminal map by chain rule
Compute $M = \partial z_N / \partial U$ along the guess in two ways: from the last block row of `ltv_prediction`, and by propagating a sensitivity matrix forward, $S_{k+1} = A_k S_k + B_k E_k$ with $E_k$ selecting the inputs of step $k$. Compare, then compare with a finite difference of the rollout.
::: hint
Forward: keep $S_k = \partial z_k / \partial U$, a $3 \times 2N$ matrix that starts at zero; each step multiplies it by $A_k$ and adds $B_k$ into the columns of step $k$.
:::
::: solution
The two constructions agree to round-off, and both agree with a central difference of the rollout to about $2 \times 10^{-10}$. The forward recursion costs one $3 \times 3$ product per step and is what a real implementation uses; the full $\mathcal S_u$ is needed only when intermediate states are constrained.

```python
Z = rollout(Z0, U0); S = np.zeros((3, 2 * N)); M_fwd = None
for k in range(N):
    A, B = jacobians(Z[k], U0[k])
    S = A @ S; S[:, 2 * k:2 * k + 2] += B
M_fwd = S
M_ltv = ltv_prediction(*map(list, zip(*[jacobians(Z[k], U0[k]) for k in range(N)])))[1][-3:]
print(np.abs(M_fwd - M_ltv).max())                           # round-off
```

:::: exercise The first step repairs the guess
The guess violates the rate bound (its $\omega$ jumps by $0.6$). Show that the first QP must change at least some inputs by $0.2$, and find in the table which iteration first satisfies every bound.
::: hint
A jump of $0.6$ between two consecutive inputs must become at most $0.2$; the correction to the two inputs must absorb $0.4$ between them.
:::
::: solution
If $\bar u_{10} - \bar u_9 = -0.6$ in $\omega$ and the corrected difference must lie in $[-0.2, 0.2]$, then $\delta\omega_{10} - \delta\omega_9 \ge 0.4$, so at least one of the two corrections is $0.2$ or more in size; the table's first step has largest $|\delta U| = 0.476$. Every iterate after the first satisfies all bounds, because the bounds are imposed on $\bar U + \delta U$ exactly and the QP enforces them: the linearisation touches only the pose.

:::: exercise The penalty threshold
Run the elastic mode with $\rho \in \{0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 1\}$ and report the terminal residual at convergence. Where does the behaviour change, and why there?
::: hint
The pose multipliers at the solution are $(-0.093, -0.261, 0.210)$.
:::
::: solution
For $\rho = 0.3$ and $1$ the residual is at round-off after 15 iterations and the cost is $0.316697$; for $\rho = 0.2$ the $Y$ row, whose multiplier $0.261$ exceeds $\rho$, is given up: the iteration runs its 50 iterations and the pose is missed by $0.34$ in $Y$ alone, at cost $0.2386$; for $\rho = 0.1$ the $X$ row is given up as well and the plan drifts to cost $0.0797$, missing by $0.69$ in $X$ and $0.85$ in $Y$ while the heading is still met. The threshold is the largest multiplier, as the exact-penalty theorem of chapter A5 says.

```python
for rho in (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 1.0):
    U, it, hist = sqp(Z0, ZF, U0, N, rho=rho)
    print(rho, it, round(cost(U), 6), f"{hist[-1][1]:.1e}")
```

The error is zero up to a push at sample 20 and grows from 21 on: $0.0002$ at 21, $0.054$ at 22, $0.094$ at 23, $0.139$ at 25 and $0.154$ at 27. for t in range(5, 29):

:::: exercise A Newton step instead of Gauss–Newton
The Lagrangian's Hessian adds $\sum_i \nu_i\, \partial^2 z_{N,i} / \partial U^2$ to $H$, with $\nu$ the pose multipliers. Estimate the size of that term from the multipliers and the curvature of the terminal map, and say why dropping it costs a constant factor per iteration rather than divergence.
::: hint
The multipliers are at most $0.26$ and the terminal map's second derivatives are of order $T_s^2 v$, which is small; compare with the diagonal of $H$, $2(0.05 + 2 \cdot 0.10) = 0.5$.
:::
::: solution
The neglected term is at most a few hundredths against a Hessian diagonal of $0.5$: a relative perturbation of order $0.1$, which is roughly the contraction factor $0.17$ observed. Gauss–Newton converges linearly at a rate set by the ratio of the neglected curvature to the kept Hessian, so a small ratio means fast linear convergence and a large one means slow convergence or none; here it is small because the cost's curvature dominates the constraint's. Adding the term and solving the resulting indefinite-Hessian QP would give quadratic convergence at the price of a nonconvex subproblem.
:::

## The chapter on one page {#summary}

::: steps
- **The nonlinearity** is one equation: the terminal pose $z_N(U) = z_f$ through thirty Euler steps; the cost and bounds are exact quadratics and linear rows in $U$.
- **Linearise along the plan**: Jacobians $A_k$, $B_k$ of the Euler step, time-varying prediction matrices with ordered products in place of powers, the terminal map $M = \partial z_N / \partial U$, rank 3 along a moving plan.
- **One QP per step**: the exact quadratic cost in $\delta U$, bounds on $\bar U + \delta U$, and $M \delta U = z_f - \bar z_N$; solved by chapter A4's method in 11 Newton steps.
- **Convergence**: 15 iterations from the guess to cost $0.316697$ and a residual of $5 \times 10^{-13}$, a factor of about six per iteration, linear; SLSQP takes 27 iterations and three times the time for the same inputs to $7 \times 10^{-6}$.
- **Elastic mode**: slacks on the pose with an $\ell_1$ penalty above the largest multiplier $0.261$ keep every QP feasible, from all-zero inputs (rank 2) or random ones, and are exact; below it, the pose is missed.
- **Real-time iteration**: one QP per sample along the shifted plan; a push at sample 10 is absorbed exactly, one at sample 25 leaves $0.139$ in $Y$; 30 QPs match 153.
- **Certified**: KKT residuals, bounds and the pose, numerically; not certified: global optimality or closed-loop stability.
:::

### The vocabulary, in one table

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Linearisation point | The plan and its rollout at which the Jacobians are evaluated. |
| Jacobians $A_k$, $B_k$ | Derivatives of the one-step map with respect to the state and the input. |
| Time-varying prediction matrices | $\mathcal S_x$, $\mathcal S_u$ with ordered products $A_{i-1} \cdots A_{j+1} B_j$. |
| Terminal map $M$ | $\partial z_N / \partial U$, the last block row of $\mathcal S_u$. |
| Defect | $z_f - \bar z_N$, the pose error of the current plan. |
| Gauss–Newton step | A QP with the exact cost Hessian and the linearised constraint, no constraint curvature. |
| Elastic mode | Slacks on the equality with an $\ell_1$ penalty: always feasible, exact above the multiplier. |
| Trust region | A bound $|\delta U| \le \Delta$ on the correction. |
| Real-time iteration | One linearisation and one QP per sample in closed loop. |
| Shrinking horizon | The number of moves left to the fixed final time. |

**Scope.** A deterministic nonlinear plant with a measured state, a quadratic cost and a terminal equality; the simplest SQP, without second-order constraint terms, line search or globalisation beyond the elastic mode and an optional trust region. Nonlinear MPC with stability guarantees, state estimation and model error are out of scope; lab [[b04]] shows the other route to an affine prediction model, from data rather than from Jacobians.

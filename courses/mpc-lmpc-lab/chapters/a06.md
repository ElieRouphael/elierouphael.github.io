## From a plan to a feedback law {#law}

**The ideal problem.** For $x_{k+1} = Ax_k + Bu_k$ with the state in $\mathcal X$ and the input in $\mathcal U$, the controller one would like is the minimiser of the infinite sum

$$J_\infty^\star(x(0)) = \min_{u_0, u_1, \dots} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} q(x_k, u_k) \quad \text{subject to}\quad x_k \in \mathcal X,\ u_k \in \mathcal U,$$

with the stage cost $q$ pricing state and input. It has infinitely many decisions. Chapter [[a05]] truncated it to a horizon of $N$ steps and solved that as one quadratic program; this chapter repeats the truncated problem in feedback.

**The problem solved at real time $t$.** Predicted quantities carry two indices: $x_{k|t}$ is the state at future time $k$ as predicted at time $t$, and $x_{t|t} = x(t)$ is the measurement. The horizon-$N$ problem at time $t$ is

$$\begin{aligned} J_N^\star(x(t)) = \min_{\{u_{k|t}\}}\ & V_f(x_{t+N|t}) + \sum_{k=t}^{t+N-1} q(x_{k|t}, u_{k|t}) \\ \text{s.t. } & x_{k+1|t} = A x_{k|t} + B u_{k|t}, \quad x_{k|t} \in \mathcal X, \quad u_{k|t} \in \mathcal U, \quad x_{t+N|t} \in \mathcal X_f, \quad x_{t|t} = x(t). \end{aligned}$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} &J_N^\star(x(t)) = \min_{\{u_{k|t}\}}\ V_f(x_{t+N|t}) \\ &\qquad + \textstyle\sum_{k=t}^{t+N-1} q(x_{k|t}, u_{k|t}) \\ &\text{s.t. } x_{k+1|t} = A x_{k|t} + B u_{k|t}, \\ &\quad x_{k|t} \in \mathcal X,\ u_{k|t} \in \mathcal U, \\ &\quad x_{t+N|t} \in \mathcal X_f,\ x_{t|t} = x(t). \end{aligned}

**The receding-horizon law.** Of the optimal sequence $(u_{t|t}^\star, \dots, u_{t+N-1|t}^\star)$, MPC applies only

$$\boxed{u(t) = u_{t|t}^\star}\,,$$

discards the rest, lets the system move to $x(t+1)$, and solves a new problem from there.

::: define What is receding-horizon control?
**In plain words:** a controller that, at every sample, uses a model to predict what a sequence of future inputs would do, picks the sequence that costs least while respecting the limits, applies its first move only, and starts over from the newly measured state.

**More precisely:** the feedback law $\kappa_N(x) = u_0^\star(x)$, where $u_0^\star(x)$ is the first element of the minimiser of the horizon-$N$ problem solved at the current state $x$. The optimiser is part of the controller, not an offline trajectory generator: the law is defined implicitly, by what the optimisation returns, and for a time-invariant model, cost and constraints it is itself time invariant. Chapter [[a11]] shows that with a suitable terminal cost $V_f$ and terminal set $\mathcal X_f$ the closed loop is recursively feasible and asymptotically stable.

- Horizon $N$ :: How many steps ahead the controller plans; it slides forward by one at every sample.
- Stage cost $q$, terminal cost $V_f$ :: The price of one step; the estimated price of everything after the horizon.
- Terminal set $\mathcal X_f$ :: Where the plan must end so that the future can still be handled safely.
- Implicit law :: $\kappa_N$ has no formula; it is evaluated by solving a QP at the current state.
:::

## Predicted against closed-loop trajectory {#predicted}

At time $t$ the optimiser predicts $x_{t|t}, x_{t+1|t}, \dots, x_{t+N|t}$. At $t + 1$ it predicts a new sequence $x_{t+1|t+1}, x_{t+2|t+1}, \dots$ from the measured state. Only the first transition of each plan is executed. MPC is not "optimise once and execute the whole sequence"; it is

$$\boxed{\text{optimise} \to \text{apply one move} \to \text{measure} \to \text{optimise again}}.$$
%narrow \boxed{\begin{aligned} &\text{optimise} \to \text{apply one move} \\ &\to \text{measure} \to \text{optimise again} \end{aligned}}

**Why re-optimise?** Because the next measured state is rarely the predicted one. Disturbances, estimation errors, model mismatch and changing references all move the system away from the plan, and re-optimising from the measured state absorbs them. In the hero demo nothing of the kind happens: the model is exact and nothing disturbs the cart, so with the LQR terminal cost the plan made at step 0 coincides with what is later applied to $10^{-14}$, and every re-solve finds the previous plan still optimal. That is instructive rather than disappointing. It says that in the nominal case the loop executes its first plan, and that re-optimisation earns its keep exactly when the plant deviates from the model (chapter [[b06]]). With $V_f = x^\top Qx$ instead, the first plan and the applied trajectory differ by up to 0.023: a terminal cost that underestimates the tail makes each solve end slightly differently from the last, so the plan changes at every step even without disturbances.

**The reference run.** From $x_0 = (-3.6, 2.4)$ with $N = 5$ and the full motor, all three designs take 9 moves to bring the state's norm under $10^{-3}$, with the input saturated on one move, a velocity peak of 2.4 and a closed-loop cost $\sum_t q(x_t, u_t)$ of 17.639. The first plan under the terminal design predicts the states $(-1.503, 1.794)$, $(-0.209, 0.794)$, $(0.162, -0.052)$, $(0.083, -0.106)$ and $(0.013, -0.034)$, and those are the states the loop visits. The three designs differ in what they certify, not in what they do from this start: the readout "steps where $J_N^\star$ fails to drop by $q$" is 4 of 8 for the first design and 0 of 8 for the other two, and chapter [[a11]] says why that distinction is the whole theory.

## Four properties that must be kept apart {#properties}

**Feasibility.** At state $x$, the horizon-$N$ problem is feasible if at least one admissible input sequence satisfies all predicted constraints and the terminal constraint. The feasible set is $\mathcal X_N = \{x : \text{feasible from } x\}$. A statement about the optimisation now.

**Recursive feasibility.** The law is recursively feasible if $x(t) \in \mathcal X_N$ implies $x(t+1) \in \mathcal X_N$ under the applied MPC input: feasible now implies feasible again at the next instant, so the optimisation never paints itself into a corner. Current feasibility alone does not imply it, as [[#lost|Part 4]] shows.

**Constraint satisfaction.** If the model is exact, the state is known and the problem stays recursively feasible, the executed first input and state inherit the constraints imposed on the prediction. Distinct from stability: a trajectory may satisfy every constraint and never converge, and a stable unconstrained controller may violate limits.

**Stability.** Asymptotic stability of the origin means $x(t) \to 0$ from every initial state in some set, with small starts staying small. The region of attraction is the set of initial states from which the closed loop converges while keeping the other properties. Feasibility says a legal trajectory exists; stability speaks about where it goes. A feasible MPC controller need not stabilise anything [[ref:2]].

| Property | Question it answers | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Feasibility | Is there a plan from here, now? | The constraints and the horizon |
| Recursive feasibility | Will there be a plan after I move? | The terminal set and the shifted sequence (chapter [[a11]]) |
| Constraint satisfaction | Do the executed moves obey the limits? | An exact model plus recursive feasibility |
| Stability | Does the state go home and stay? | The terminal cost and a Lyapunov argument (chapters [[a07]] and [[a11]]) |

::: callout warm
**"The optimiser solved successfully" is not a stability proof.** The hero's first design converges from its reference start, and its optimal value nevertheless fails to decrease by the stage cost on four of the eight steps. The convergence is a property of this run, not a theorem. Chapter [[a11]] gives the conditions under which it becomes one.
:::

## Feasible now, infeasible later {#lost}

The numbers script searches a grid of starts under the first design, $V_f = x^\top Qx$ with no terminal set, for horizons of one to three steps and both motors. It finds 122 combinations of input bound, horizon and start that are feasible at the start and lose feasibility later. The longest-lived is the weaker motor, $|u| \le 0.5$, with $N = 1$ from $(-4, 2)$: the problem is feasible, and feasible again after one, two and three moves, through the states $(-1.75, 2.5)$, $(0.5, 2)$, $(2.25, 1.5)$ and $(3.5, 1)$, and then no admissible plan exists. The cart has reached a position its one-step horizon never saw coming: from $(3.5, 1)$ even full braking lands at $p = 4.25$, outside the box. The inputs were $0.5, -0.5, -0.5, -0.5$: the controller pushed first, because its one-step objective saw a velocity it could still afford, and braked as hard as it could afterwards.

The second button under the demo loads this run. Switch to the terminal-set design from the same start: the first problem is reported infeasible at once, which is the honest answer. A state that can satisfy the constraints now but cannot keep satisfying them is what chapter [[a10]] calls a state outside the control-invariant set, and the terminal set keeps the horizon from entering such states. With the full motor the same phenomenon survives at $N = 1$ to $3$ on 24 grid starts, for instance $(-2.5, -2)$ at $N = 1$, which is feasible for one step only.

## The horizon-one pathology {#pathology}

Reducing the horizon can destroy stability even when every finite-horizon problem is solved exactly. A scalar example shows the mechanism in one line.

**One-step MPC without a terminal cost.** Take the unstable plant $x^+ = ax + u$ with $a = 1.2$ and the stage cost $q(x, u) = x^2 + ru^2$, $r > 0$. For $N = 1$ and no terminal cost the problem is $\min_{u_0} x_0^2 + ru_0^2$. The future state $x_1$ does not appear, so $u_0^\star = 0$, and the closed loop is $x(t+1) = 1.2\,x(t)$: unstable, 15.4 after fifteen steps from 1. The optimiser solves its problem perfectly; the design of the problem is the issue.

**Add the infinite-horizon LQR terminal cost.** With $V_f(x) = Px^2$, where $P$ solves the discrete algebraic Riccati equation of chapter [[a07]], the one-step problem is $\min_u x^2 + ru^2 + P(ax + u)^2$. Differentiating, $2ru + 2P(ax + u) = 0$, hence

$$u^\star = -\frac{Pa}{r + P}\, x,$$

which is exactly the scalar LQR feedback when $P$ is the Riccati solution. With $r = 4$: $P = 3.8099$, $K = 0.5854$, closed-loop eigenvalue $a - K = 0.6146$. A carefully chosen terminal cost injects the infinite future into a short horizon.

The same pathology hides inside the hero: with $V_f = x^\top Qx$ and $N = 1$ the cart's controller sees no reason to use its motor until a bound forces it to. ## The code behind it {#code}

The loop is nine lines of the course package.

::: code advanced_mpc_lmpc/mpc.py:simulate_receding_horizon
Four things to read. The controller is solved at the current state, `controller.solve(x, warm=warm)`, and `sol.U[0]` is the only input ever applied: the law $u(t) = u_{t|t}^\star$ is that one line. The state then moves under the plant, `x = controller.A @ x + controller.B @ u`, which here is the same matrix pair the controller predicts with; a mismatch experiment would change this line and nothing else. The loop breaks on `not sol.success`, which is where a loss of recursive feasibility shows up as a shortened trajectory rather than a crash. And the warm start is the shifted sequence, `sol.U[1:]` with the last input repeated: chapter [[a11]] proves that the shifted sequence is always feasible under the right terminal ingredients, which is what makes it a good starting guess and, in an embedded solver, a safe fallback when time runs out. The hero demo keeps the same structure in JavaScript and additionally stores every plan, to draw it against what was applied.
:::

## Common pitfalls {#pitfalls}

1. **"MPC executes its plan."** It executes the first move of each plan and throws the rest away. With an exact model the plans happen to agree; with a disturbed plant they do not, and that disagreement is the point.
2. **"A feasible QP at every step so far proves recursive feasibility."** No. The weaker-motor run above is feasible four times and infeasible the fifth.
3. **"A feasible controller is a stable controller."** Feasibility and stability are different properties; a loop can be recursively feasible and circle for ever.
4. **"A longer horizon is always safer."** It enlarges the feasible set, but without terminal ingredients the set still contains doomed states, and the objective may still rise along the loop.
5. **"The solver's objective is the value function."** Solvers drop the constant $x_0^\top Qx_0 + x_0^\top S_x^\top \bar Q S_x x_0$; the Lyapunov checks of this course always reconstruct the full $J_N^\star$, as the hero does.

## Check your understanding {#check}

:::: qa
Try to answer each question before opening it.

::: q 1. Why is MPC a feedback controller if it solves an open-loop optimisation?
At each sampling instant the optimisation is re-solved from the newly measured state. The applied law is $u(t) = u_{t|t}^\star(x(t))$: the first element of the optimiser's answer depends on the current state and so defines an implicit feedback map.
:::
::: q 2. What do the two indices in $x_{k|t}$ mean?
$t$ is when the prediction is made, $k$ the future time it refers to. $x_{t|t}$ is the measurement; $x_{t+N|t}$ the predicted terminal state of the plan made at $t$.
:::
::: q 3. Why does the hero's plan coincide with the applied trajectory under the Riccati terminal cost?
The model is exact, nothing disturbs the cart, and the terminal cost makes each truncated problem agree with the next one's tail, so re-solving reproduces the shifted plan. Under $V_f = x^\top Qx$ the tail is underestimated and the plans drift by up to 0.023.
:::
::: q 4. What is the difference between feasible and recursively feasible?
Feasible: an admissible sequence exists at the current state. Recursively feasible: applying the controller keeps the next state inside the set of states from which the problem is still feasible.
:::
::: q 5. Why does the one-step controller without terminal cost leave an unstable plant alone?
Its objective $x_0^2 + ru_0^2$ contains the input only through a penalty, and the next state not at all; the cheapest input is zero. Nothing in the problem tells it that the state will grow.
:::
::: q 6. What does the Riccati terminal cost change in that problem?
It adds $P(ax + u)^2$, the exact cost of the infinite tail, so the one-step problem is the infinite-horizon problem in disguise and its solution is the LQR law.
:::
::: q 7. Which of the four properties does an exact model plus recursive feasibility give you?
Constraint satisfaction of the executed moves. Not stability: that needs the terminal cost and a Lyapunov argument.
:::

::::: exercises
:::: exercise Open loop against closed loop
Solve the terminal-design problem once from $x_0 = (-3.6, 2.4)$ with $N = 5$ and execute all five inputs without re-solving. Compare the cost and the final state with the receding-horizon loop.
::: hint
The numbers script's last section does it; `controller.solve(x0).U` is the open-loop plan.
:::
::: solution
Executed open loop, the first plan costs 17.6386 over its five moves and ends at $(0.0129, -0.0335)$. The receding-horizon loop costs 17.6386 over its first five moves, exactly the same, and 17.6393 in all after nine moves, because it keeps going past the end of the plan. With an exact model and no disturbance the two agree to $10^{-16}$ over the planned moves: the loop re-derives the plan it already had. Add a disturbance to the plant line of `simulate_receding_horizon` and the two separate at the first step.

```python
from advanced_mpc_lmpc.mpc import LinearMPC
sol = ctrl.solve(x0)                 # ctrl: the terminal design of the numbers script
x, cost = x0.copy(), 0.0
for u in sol.U:
    cost += float(x @ Q @ x + u @ R @ u); x = A @ x + B @ u
print(cost, x)                       # 17.6386 [0.0129 -0.0335]
```

:::: exercise Find your own lost feasibility
With the full motor and $N = 2$ under $V_f = x^\top Qx$, find a start on the grid of spacing 0.5 that is feasible for exactly one step. Explain from the dynamics why the second problem has no solution.
::: hint
The numbers script lists six such starts; $(-3, -1.5)$ is one. Where is the cart after its first move?

From $(-3, -1.5)$ the two-step controller applies an input that leaves the cart at a position near $-4$ with a negative velocity: the next position $p + v + u/2$ cannot stay above $-4$ for any $|u| \le 1$ once $p + v < -4.5$. The first problem was feasible because its two-step prediction could still end inside the box; it ended at a state from which no two-step plan exists. The terminal-set design reports $(-3, -1.5)$ infeasible at once for $N = 2$.
:::

:::: exercise The pathology by hand
For $x^+ = 1.1x + u$ and $q = x^2 + 5u^2$, show that horizon-one MPC with no terminal cost chooses $u = 0$, and derive the feedback $u = -K(P)\,x$ obtained by adding the terminal cost $Px_1^2$.
::: hint
Which terms of the objective depend on $u_0$? Then differentiate $x^2 + 5u^2 + P(1.1x + u)^2$ in $u$.
:::
::: solution
The objective $x_0^2 + 5u_0^2$ contains $u_0$ only through $5u_0^2 \ge 0$, minimised at $u_0 = 0$; the loop is $x^+ = 1.1x$, eigenvalue 1.1, unstable. With the terminal cost, $10u + 2P(1.1x + u) = 0$ gives $u = -\frac{1.1P}{5 + P}\,x$. With the Riccati $P = 3.4848$, $K = 0.4518$ and the eigenvalue is $1.1 - 0.4518 = 0.6482$. Any $P > 0$ lowers the eigenvalue, but the loop is stable only when $K(P) > 0.1$, that is $P > 0.5$; and only the Riccati $P$ makes the one-step law optimal for the infinite horizon. Set $a = 1.1$, $r = 5$ in the widget of [[#pathology|Part 5]].

```python
from advanced_mpc_lmpc.linear import dlqr
K11, P11, e11 = dlqr(np.array([[1.1]]), np.array([[1.0]]), np.array([[1.0]]), np.array([[5.0]]))
print(P11.item(), K11.item(), 1.1 * P11.item() / (5.0 + P11.item()), e11[0].real)   # 3.4848 0.4518 0.4518 0.6482
```

:::: exercise Build the loop yourself
Write a receding-horizon loop that stores every plan, run it under the terminal design from $(-3.6, 2.4)$ with $N = 5$, and print the largest difference between each plan and the states that were later applied.
::: hint
Follow `simulate_receding_horizon`; `sol.X` holds the predicted states $x_{1|t}, \dots, x_{N|t}$.
:::
::: solution
Every plan agrees with the applied states to $10^{-14}$: with an exact model and the Riccati terminal cost, each solve returns the shifted previous plan plus one LQR move, which is the shifted sequence of chapter [[a11]].

```python
x, plans, xs = x0.copy(), [], [x0.copy()]
for t in range(9):
    sol = ctrl.solve(x); plans.append(sol.X)
    u = sol.U[0]; x = A @ x + B @ u; xs.append(x.copy())
for t, plan in enumerate(plans):
    actual = np.array(xs[t + 1: t + 1 + len(plan)])
    print(t, np.abs(plan[:len(actual)] - actual).max())    # all about 1e-14
```

:::: exercise Where re-optimisation matters
Add a disturbance $w_t$ drawn uniformly from $[-0.1, 0.1]$ to the velocity in the plant update of the loop, keep the controller unchanged, and compare the closed-loop cost over 20 steps with executing the first plan open loop for 5 steps and then holding $u = 0$.
::: hint
Only the plant line changes: `x = A @ x + B @ u + np.array([0, w])`. Seed the generator so that both runs see the same disturbances.

::: solution
The open-loop execution drifts: after the planned five moves the cart is not at rest, and holding $u = 0$ lets the disturbed velocity carry it away, so the cost keeps growing at the rate of the disturbance. The receding-horizon loop re-plans from each disturbed state and keeps the cart near the origin, its cost settling at a small value set by the disturbance's size. The exact numbers depend on the seed; the qualitative gap does not. This is the reason for re-optimisation that the nominal hero cannot show, and chapter [[b06]] asks what guarantees survive it.

## The chapter on one page {#summary}

::: steps
- **The law**: solve the horizon-$N$ problem at $x(t)$, apply $u_{t|t}^\star$, measure, repeat; the plan is replaced, not executed.
- **Two indices**: $x_{k|t}$ is the prediction for time $k$ made at time $t$; $x_{t|t}$ is the measurement.
- **Nominal case**: with an exact model and the Riccati cost the plans coincide with the applied trajectory to $10^{-14}$; with $V_f = x^\top Qx$ they drift by 0.023.
- **Four properties**: feasibility, recursive feasibility, constraint satisfaction, stability; none implies another.
- **Lost feasibility**: weaker motor, $N = 1$, from $(-4, 2)$: four feasible solves, then none; the terminal-set design refuses at once.
- **Horizon one**: $x^+ = 1.2x + u$ with no terminal cost gives $u = 0$; the Riccati cost $P = 3.81$ gives $u = -0.585x$ and eigenvalue 0.615.
- **The reference run**: $N = 5$ from $(-3.6, 2.4)$, 9 moves, cost 17.64 under all three designs; only the Riccati designs have a value that never rises.
:::

### The vocabulary, in one table

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $x_{k|t}$, $u_{k|t}$ | Prediction for time $k$ made at time $t$. |
| Receding-horizon law $\kappa_N$ | The first optimised input, as a function of the state. |
| Feasible set $\mathcal X_N$ | States from which the horizon-$N$ problem has a solution. |
| Recursive feasibility | Feasible now implies feasible after the applied move. |
| Region of attraction | Starts from which the loop converges while keeping its properties. |
| Doomed state | Feasible now, with no admissible way to stay feasible for ever. |
| Horizon-one pathology | A short horizon with no terminal cost ignores the future entirely. |
| Lyapunov residual | $J_N^\star(x_{t+1}) - J_N^\star(x_t) + q(x_t, u_t)$; non-positive under the theorem of chapter [[a11]]. |

**Scope.** Nominal receding-horizon control of a linear system with a measured state and an exact model: the law, the vocabulary of guarantees, and what can go wrong. The guarantees themselves are chapter [[a11]]'s; disturbances and mismatch are the labs'.

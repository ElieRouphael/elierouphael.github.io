## The idea: Newton's method on the KKT system, from inside {#idea}

Chapter [[a03]] ended with four conditions that the solution of

$$\min_z\ \tfrac12 z^\top H z + f^\top z \quad \text{s.t.}\quad G z \le g, \quad E z = e$$

must satisfy. Write the inequality with a slack $s \ge 0$, $Gz + s = g$, and the KKT system reads

$$H z + f + G^\top \lambda + E^\top \nu = 0, \qquad G z + s = g, \qquad E z = e, \qquad s \ge 0,\ \lambda \ge 0, \qquad s_i \lambda_i = 0 .$$

Three of these are linear in the unknowns $(z, s, \lambda, \nu)$. Only the last, complementary slackness, is not, and it is also the one that makes the problem combinatorial: which $s_i$ are zero (active rows) and which $\lambda_i$ are zero (inactive rows) is not known in advance.

::: define What is a primal-dual interior-point method?
**In plain words:** instead of guessing the active set, replace "$s_i \lambda_i = 0$" by "$s_i \lambda_i = $ a small positive number", solve the now smooth system by Newton's method while keeping $s$ and $\lambda$ strictly positive, and shrink the small number towards zero. The iterates approach the solution from inside the feasible region and settle on the active constraints only at the end.

**More precisely:** for a centring parameter $\sigma \in (0, 1)$ and the average complementarity $\mu = s^\top \lambda / m$, each iteration takes one Newton step on the perturbed system with $s_i \lambda_i = \sigma \mu$, shortens the step so that $s > 0$ and $\lambda > 0$ remain, and repeats until every residual is below a tolerance. The path $\{(z, s, \lambda) : s_i \lambda_i = \mu\}$ traced as $\mu \to 0$ is the central path.

- Primal-dual :: $z$ and the multipliers are updated together; the multipliers are part of the answer, not recovered afterwards.
- Interior :: $s > 0$ and $\lambda > 0$ at every iterate; no constraint is touched before convergence.
- Fraction to the boundary :: The step is cut to at most 99% of the distance to the first $s_i$ or $\lambda_i$ that would reach zero.
:::

The hero shows it: from $z^0 = 0$ the iterates move towards $c$, bend as the constraint comes into play, and converge to $(0.7, 0.3)$ along the inside of the half-plane. One Newton step puts the linear residuals at round-off; what the remaining steps do is shrink $s \lambda$ by a factor of about $\sigma$ each.

## Stacking the rows, and a starting point {#stacking}

The package's `solve_qp` accepts rows `A_ub z <= b_ub`, equalities `A_eq z = b_eq` and bounds `lb <= z <= ub`, the same signature as the SLSQP wrapper it replaced. Its first job is to turn all inequalities into one matrix.

::: code advanced_mpc_lmpc/qp.py:stack_inequalities
The user's rows come first, then one row $e_i^\top z \le u_i$ for every finite upper bound and one row $-e_i^\top z \le -l_i$ for every finite lower bound; infinite bounds are skipped. Treating bounds as ordinary rows costs a little efficiency and buys simplicity: there is one kind of inequality in the rest of the solver, and the multipliers come back in the same order, which is how chapter [[a05]] names the active bound of each input. For the hand QP with $z \ge 0$ and $z_1 \le 2$ the stacked system is four rows: $(1, 1) \le 1$, $(1, 0) \le 2$, $(-1, 0) \le 0$, $(0, -1) \le 0$.
:::

Then the starting point, lines `z = ...` to `nu = np.zeros(p)` of `solve_qp`: $z$ is zero, or the caller's `x0`, projected into the bounds; the slack is $s = \max(g - Gz, 1)$, which is positive whatever $z$ is, so the start need not be feasible; $\lambda = 1$ and $\nu = 0$. An interior-point method does not need a good start, and the solver ignores a warm start's quality beyond this projection. For the hand QP: $z^0 = 0$, $s^0 = 1$, $\lambda^0 = 1$, as the hero's first row shows.

## The residuals are the KKT conditions {#residuals}

::: code advanced_mpc_lmpc/qp.py:solve_qp
Read the loop body in order. The first three lines are the linear KKT conditions as residual vectors: `rd` is stationarity, $Hz + f + G^\top \lambda + E^\top \nu$; `rp` is primal feasibility of the inequalities with their slacks, $Gz + s - g$; `re` is the equalities, $Ez - e$. Then `mu`, the average complementarity $s^\top \lambda / m$, and `rc`, the residual of the perturbed complementarity $s_i \lambda_i = \sigma \mu$, written as $\sigma \mu - s_i \lambda_i$. The scalar `resid` is the largest entry of the three linear residuals and, with `mu`, decides the stopping test. If the caller passed a list as `trace`, the iterate is recorded here, which is how the hero's table and the numbers script see inside the loop.

The Newton system comes next. With $D = \lambda / s$ (elementwise) the full system in $(\Delta z, \Delta s, \Delta \lambda, \Delta \nu)$ is reduced by eliminating $\Delta s$ and $\Delta \lambda$: the matrix `M` is $\begin{pmatrix} H + G^\top D G & E^\top \\ E & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and the right-hand side carries the residuals, with the vector `t` collecting the complementarity and primal residuals divided by $s$. Part 4 derives it. `np.linalg.solve` does the work; if `M` is singular, which happens only for redundant equalities, a least-squares solve with a tiny regularisation stands in. The recovered `ds` and `dl` follow from the two eliminated equations.

The step is then shortened by the fraction-to-the-boundary rule, Part 5, and all four unknowns move together by the same `a`. A non-finite iterate, which an infeasible problem can produce, ends the loop early. After the loop comes the polish of Part 6, then the result: the solution, the multipliers split back into `lam` and `nu`, the largest constraint violation, the number of iterations, a `success` flag that means "the KKT residuals are below the tolerance and the point is feasible", and the final `kkt_residual` for the caller to inspect.
:::

## The Newton system, with $s$ and $\lambda$ eliminated {#newton}

Linearising the perturbed KKT system at the current iterate gives, for the step $(\Delta z, \Delta s, \Delta \lambda, \Delta \nu)$,

$$\begin{aligned} H \Delta z + G^\top \Delta \lambda + E^\top \Delta \nu &= -r_d, \\ G \Delta z + \Delta s &= -r_p, \\ E \Delta z &= -r_e, \\ \Lambda \Delta s + S \Delta \lambda &= r_c, \end{aligned}$$

with $S = \mathrm{diag}(s)$, $\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda)$ and $r_c = \sigma \mu \mathbf 1 - S \Lambda \mathbf 1$. The last two equations are cheap to solve for the eliminated variables: $\Delta s = -r_p - G \Delta z$ and $\Delta \lambda = S^{-1}(r_c - \Lambda \Delta s)$. Substituting both into the first,

$$\boxed{\begin{pmatrix} H + G^\top D G & E^\top \\ E & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \Delta z \\ \Delta \nu \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -r_d - G^\top t \\ -r_e \end{pmatrix}}, \qquad D = S^{-1} \Lambda, \quad t = S^{-1}(r_c + \Lambda r_p) .$$
%narrow \begin{pmatrix} H + G^\top D G & E^\top \\ E & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \Delta z \\ \Delta \nu \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -r_d - G^\top t \\ -r_e \end{pmatrix}

The reduced matrix has the size of $z$ plus the equalities, $n + p$, whatever the number of inequalities, and its top-left block is the Hessian plus a weighted Gram matrix of the inequality rows. The weights $D_i = \lambda_i / s_i$ are the whole story of an interior-point method: a row that is far from active has a large slack and a tiny multiplier, so its weight vanishes and the row is ignored; a row that is becoming active has $s_i \to 0$, its weight grows without bound, and the row is enforced ever more rigidly. The active set is never chosen; it emerges from the weights.

**Why the linear residuals vanish after one step.** For a QP the first three KKT equations are linear, so a full Newton step ($a = 1$) solves them exactly. The hero's table shows `resid` at $10^{-16}$ from iteration 1 on; the remaining iterations only reduce $\mu$. Whenever the step is cut short by the boundary rule, the linear residuals shrink by the factor $1 - a$ instead, and recover on the next full step.

## The step, and when to stop {#step}

**Fraction to the boundary.** The Newton direction may try to make some $s_i$ or $\lambda_i$ negative. The step length is

$$a = \min\Big(1,\ \min_{\Delta s_i < 0} \frac{-0.99\, s_i}{\Delta s_i},\ \min_{\Delta \lambda_i < 0} \frac{-0.99\, \lambda_i}{\Delta \lambda_i}\Big),$$

the largest step that keeps every slack and multiplier at least 1% of its current value. On the hand QP the first step is cut to $a = 0.95$: the full step would have put $s$ at zero, on the constraint, from where the next weight $D$ would be infinite. Staying strictly inside is what keeps the reduced system solvable.

**Centring.** With $\sigma = 0.1$ the complementarity target is a tenth of the current average, and the table shows $\mu$ falling by almost exactly that factor at each step: $5 \times 10^{-2}$, $7 \times 10^{-3}$, $7 \times 10^{-4}$, and so on. The numbers script runs the $N = 8$ MPC QP with $\sigma = 0.01, 0.1, 0.5$ and needs 12, 13 and 34 iterations: a small $\sigma$ aims at the solution, a large one at the central path. Production solvers choose $\sigma$ adaptively (Mehrotra's predictor-corrector, [[ref:1]]); the course keeps a constant because every problem here converges in about a dozen steps anyway.

**Stopping.** The loop ends when `resid < tol` and `mu < tol`, with `tol` $= 10^{-9}$ by default. Tightening it to $10^{-12}$ costs three more iterations on the MPC QP, loosening it to $10^{-6}$ saves three: each decade of accuracy is one Newton step, because $\mu$ falls geometrically. The iteration cap, 100, is reached only by problems that have no solution, Part 8.

## The polish: from $10^{-10}$ to round-off {#polish}

An interior-point iterate is never exactly on a constraint: at the end of the hand QP the slack is $10^{-10}$, not zero, and $\lambda = 1 + 10^{-10}$. For a course that quotes residuals to machine precision that is not good enough, and at a degenerate optimum, where more constraints are active than there are variables, the multipliers are not unique and the interior point drifts among them. The last block of `solve_qp` fixes both.

Once the primal residuals are small, the rows pressed against or touched, $\{i : \lambda_i > s_i \text{ or } s_i \approx 0\}$, are taken as the active set $\mathcal A$, and the equality-constrained QP on it is solved as one linear system,

$$\begin{pmatrix} H & G_{\mathcal A}^\top & E^\top \\ G_{\mathcal A} & 0 & 0 \\ E & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} z \\ \lambda_{\mathcal A} \\ \nu \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -f \\ g_{\mathcal A} \\ e \end{pmatrix},$$
%narrow \begin{pmatrix} H & G_{\mathcal A}^\top & E^\top \\ G_{\mathcal A} & 0 & 0 \\ E & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} z \\ \lambda_{\mathcal A} \\ \nu \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -f \\ g_{\mathcal A} \\ e \end{pmatrix}

by least squares, so that a redundant active set does no harm. The polished point is accepted only if it passes the KKT test exactly: every inequality satisfied, the multipliers nonnegative and stationarity at round-off; when the least-squares multipliers come out with a wrong sign, a non-negative least-squares fit of the gradient onto the active normals recovers a valid set. If anything fails, the interior iterate is kept. On the hand QP the active set is $\{1\}$ and the polished system returns $(0.7, 0.3)$ and $\lambda = 1$ exactly, which is why chapter [[a03]]'s residuals are 0 and not $10^{-10}$.

::: callout
**A degenerate example.** Minimise $\tfrac12 \lVert z \rVert^2 + (1, 2, 3)^\top z$ on the simplex $z \ge 0$, $z_1 + z_2 + z_3 = 1$, $z \le 1$. The answer is $(1, 0, 0)$ with the bound $z_2 \ge 0$ weakly active: its multiplier is exactly zero, so the interior point approaches it slowly, with $z_2 \approx 3 \times 10^{-5}$ at the tolerance. The polish returns $z = (1, 0, 0)$, $\nu = -2$ and the single nonzero multiplier $\lambda = 1$ on $z_3 \ge 0$, in 17 iterations.
:::

## Against SLSQP, and timings {#compare}

The package used SciPy's SLSQP before, a sequential quadratic programming method for general nonlinear problems. The numbers script runs both on four problems.

::: table num
| Problem | Interior point | SLSQP | Agreement |
|---|---|---|---|
| The hand QP, 2 variables, 1 row | 10 iterations, 0.5 ms, residual $10^{-15}$ | 4 iterations, 0.4 ms | $10^{-15}$ |
| MPC QP, $N = 8$, 8 variables, 48 rows + 16 bounds | 13 iterations, 0.8 ms, residual $2 \times 10^{-13}$ | 12 iterations, 0.8 ms | $4 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| MPC QP, $N = 20$, 20 variables, 120 rows + 40 bounds | 13 iterations, 0.9 ms, residual $3 \times 10^{-12}$ | 16 iterations, 4 ms | $7 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| Infeasible pair $z_1 \le -1$, $-z_1 \le -1$ | 7 iterations, fails, violation 1 | 97 iterations, fails, violation 1 | – |
:::

Three things to read. The interior-point iteration count does not grow with the problem: 13 steps at $N = 8$ and at $N = 20$, each step one linear solve, which is the known behaviour of the method. SLSQP's accuracy is its tolerance of $10^{-8}$ on the objective, so its inputs differ from the exact ones in the fifth decimal, and the old course pages spent paragraphs explaining residuals of that size away; the new solver returns KKT residuals of $10^{-12}$ and the paragraphs are gone. And both fail on an infeasible problem, with the interior point giving up after seven steps when its iterate becomes non-finite; neither failure is a proof. Timings are for one laptop and vary; the shape of the comparison does not.

**Scaling.** The reduced system is dense and $n + p$ square, so a solve costs $\mathcal O((n + p)^3)$: fine for the course's few dozen variables, hopeless for the thousands of a sparse long-horizon problem. That is the moment for a structure-exploiting solver (HPIPM, acados), a first-order method (OSQP) or an active-set method with warm starts (qpOASES), all referenced in chapter [[b06]]. The algorithm is the same; the linear algebra is not.

## Infeasibility, certified by an LP {#certificate}

A solver that fails has not proved that no solution exists; it may have stalled, or hit its cap. The course makes the distinction with an independent calculation.

::: code advanced_mpc_lmpc/qp.py:feasibility_margin
Add one variable $t \ge 0$ and relax every row by it: $Gz \le g + t\mathbf 1$ and $|Ez - e| \le t \mathbf 1$. Minimising $t$ is a linear program, solved here by SciPy's HiGHS, which shares no code with `solve_qp`. Its optimum is zero exactly when the original constraints can all be met, and a positive value is the smallest uniform relaxation that makes them compatible, a certificate that no $z$ satisfies them as they stand. The infeasible pair above has margin 1; the $N = 8$ MPC QP has margin 0; and the cart at $(3, 2)$ with a three-step horizon has margin $0.333$, because its next position $3 + 2 + u/2 \ge 4.5$ leaves the box whatever the input, a doomed state in the vocabulary of chapter [[a10]].
:::

Chapter [[b06]] uses this margin as the arbiter whenever a solver reports failure at a corner of the feasible set: a failure with margin zero is a solver artefact, a failure with a positive margin is infeasibility.

## Common pitfalls {#pitfalls}

1. **"The solver picks the active constraints."** It does not; the weights $\lambda_i / s_i$ do. The active set is read off at the end, for the polish and for the multipliers.
2. **"A warm start speeds up an interior-point method."** Barely: the start must be pushed back inside, and the iteration count is set by the decades of $\mu$ to be removed. Active-set methods are the ones that profit from warm starts, which is why they are preferred for MPC at high rates.
3. **"Success means optimal."** `success` means the KKT residuals are below the tolerance and the point is feasible, which for a convex QP is optimality. For a nonconvex problem, chapter [[a05]], the same flag means a local optimum at best.
4. **"Failure means infeasible."** Failure means the method did not converge. The feasibility LP decides; chapter [[b06]] shows a solver failing on a feasible, degenerate problem.
5. **"$\sigma$ is a tolerance."** It is the fraction of the current complementarity aimed at in the next step: a convergence-rate knob, not an accuracy knob.

## Check your understanding {#check}

:::: qa
Try to answer each question before opening it.

::: q 1. Which KKT condition is nonlinear, and how does the method handle it?
Complementary slackness, $s_i \lambda_i = 0$. The method replaces the zero by $\sigma \mu$, a positive target that shrinks every iteration, and takes Newton steps on the resulting smooth system.
:::
::: q 2. Why are $s$ and $\lambda$ eliminated from the Newton system?
Because their equations are diagonal: $\Delta s$ and $\Delta \lambda$ follow from $\Delta z$ by elementwise formulas, so the system to factorise has only $n + p$ unknowns however many inequalities there are.
:::
::: q 3. What do the weights $D_i = \lambda_i / s_i$ do?
They turn constraints on or off continuously: a slack row has a vanishing weight and is ignored, an active row has a huge weight and is enforced. The active set emerges from them.
:::
::: q 4. Why must the iterates stay strictly inside?
Because the weights $\lambda_i / s_i$ and the eliminated step $\Delta \lambda = S^{-1}(\dots)$ divide by $s$. A zero slack breaks the reduced system; the 99% rule prevents it.
:::
::: q 5. Why do the linear residuals vanish after one full step on a QP?
Stationarity and feasibility are linear in the unknowns, so one Newton step solves them exactly. Only the complementarity residual needs more steps.
:::
::: q 6. What does the polish add?
An exact solve on the detected active set: the solution and multipliers to round-off instead of to the tolerance, and a unique multiplier set at degenerate optima.
:::
::: q 7. Why does the course keep a separate feasibility LP?
Because a solver's failure is not a proof. The LP, from a different code base, returns zero for a feasible constraint set and a positive margin otherwise.
:::
::: q 8. When is this solver the wrong tool?
For large or sparse problems: its linear algebra is dense and cubic in the number of variables. Structure-exploiting and first-order solvers scale; the algorithm's idea is the same.
:::

```

:::: exercise A degenerate optimum
Solve $\min \tfrac12 \lVert z \rVert^2 + (1, 2, 3)^\top z$ subject to $z_1 + z_2 + z_3 = 1$, $0 \le z \le 1$. Which bounds are active, which multipliers are zero, and why is the polish needed?
::: hint
The answer is a vertex of the simplex; count the active constraints against the number of variables.
:::
::: solution
$z^\star = (1, 0, 0)$: three bounds are active ($z_1 \le 1$, $z_2 \ge 0$, $z_3 \ge 0$) plus the equality, four constraints for three variables, so the multipliers are not unique. Stationarity $z + f + \nu \mathbf 1 - \lambda_{\mathrm{lb}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{ub}} = 0$ with $\nu = -2$ gives $\lambda = 0$ on $z_2 \ge 0$ and $1$ on $z_3 \ge 0$, a weakly active bound. The interior point reaches $z_2 \approx 3 \times 10^{-5}$ at the tolerance; the polish returns the exact vertex.

```python
r = solve_qp(np.eye(3), np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0]), A_eq=[[1, 1, 1]], b_eq=[1], lb=[0, 0, 0], ub=[1, 1, 1])
print(r.x, r.nu, r.lam)      # [1. 0. 0.] [-2.] [0. 0. 0. 0. 0. 1.]
```
:::

:::: exercise Build it yourself
Write a twenty-line interior-point solver for problems with inequalities only, following Parts 3 to 5 without the polish, and compare its answer on the $N = 8$ MPC QP of the numbers script with the package's.
::: hint
You need `rd`, `rp`, `rc`, the matrix $H + G^\top D G$, the two elimination formulas and the step rule. Twelve iterations suffice at $\sigma = 0.1$.
:::
::: solution
```python
def ipm(H, f, G, g, sigma=0.1, tol=1e-9, iters=100):
    n, m = len(f), len(g); z = np.zeros(n); s = np.maximum(g - G @ z, 1.0); lam = np.ones(m)
    for _ in range(iters):
        rd = H @ z + f + G.T @ lam; rp = G @ z + s - g; mu = s @ lam / m; rc = sigma * mu - s * lam
        if max(abs(rd).max(), abs(rp).max()) < tol and mu < tol: break
        D = lam / s; t = (rc + lam * rp) / s
        dz = np.linalg.solve(H + G.T @ (D[:, None] * G), -rd - G.T @ t)
        ds = -rp - G @ dz; dl = (rc - lam * ds) / s
        a = min([1.0] + [-0.99 * s[i] / ds[i] for i in range(m) if ds[i] < 0] + [-0.99 * lam[i] / dl[i] for i in range(m) if dl[i] < 0])
        z, s, lam = z + a * dz, s + a * ds, lam + a * dl
    return z, lam

12, 13, 34 and over 100 iterations. ## The chapter on one page {#summary}

::: steps
- **The method**: Newton's method on the KKT system with $s_i \lambda_i = \sigma \mu$ instead of $0$, iterates kept strictly inside, $\mu$ driven to zero.
- **Stacking**: bounds become inequality rows; one matrix $G$, one slack vector $s$; the start is $z = 0$, $s = \max(g - Gz, 1)$, $\lambda = 1$.
- **Residuals**: `rd`, `rp`, `re` are the linear KKT conditions, `rc` the centred complementarity; they vanish after one full step on a QP except `rc`.
- **The reduced system**: $(H + G^\top D G)$ with $D = \lambda / s$, size $n + p$; the weights switch constraints on and off continuously.
- **The step**: at most 99% of the way to the boundary; stop when residuals and $\mu$ are below $10^{-9}$; one Newton step per decade.
- **The polish**: solve the equality-constrained QP on the detected active set, accept if KKT-exact; residuals go from $10^{-10}$ to 0.
- **Against SLSQP**: same answers to the SQP's tolerance, 13 iterations at $N = 8$ and $N = 20$, about a millisecond; both fail on infeasible problems.
- **Certificate**: `feasibility_margin`, an LP in a different code base, returns 0 for feasible constraints and the smallest repairing relaxation otherwise.
:::

### The vocabulary, in one table

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Slack $s$ | $g - Gz$, kept strictly positive during the iteration. |
| Complementarity $\mu$ | The average of $s_i \lambda_i$; zero at the solution. |
| Centring parameter $\sigma$ | The fraction of $\mu$ aimed at in the next step. |
| Central path | The curve of solutions of the perturbed system as $\mu \to 0$. |
| Reduced Newton system | The $(n + p)$-square system left after eliminating $\Delta s$ and $\Delta \lambda$. |
| Fraction to the boundary | The step rule that keeps $s$ and $\lambda$ positive. |
| Active-set polish | An exact solve on the detected active set, accepted if KKT-exact. |
| Feasibility margin | The smallest uniform relaxation making the constraints compatible; zero if feasible. |
| SLSQP | SciPy's sequential quadratic programming method, the package's previous solver. |

**Scope.** A dense primal-dual interior-point method with a constant centring parameter for small convex QPs, as implemented in the course package and its browser toolkit. Mehrotra's predictor-corrector, sparse factorisations, first-order methods and active-set methods with warm starts are named, not implemented; nonlinear programs are chapter [[a05]].

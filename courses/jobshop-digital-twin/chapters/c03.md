## A plan is a forecast; a controller re-forecasts {#idea}

Chapters [[c01]] and [[c02]] built a planner: given the orders in the shop, it returns the best plan it can find and, often, a proof that none is better. But a plan is made from what is known at one instant. Minutes later an order arrives that the plan never heard of, or a machine stops, and the plan is a stale forecast.

The remedy is the one a navigation app uses: **make the plan again whenever the world changes, and only ever act on its first steps**. Used this way, a planner becomes a *controller*: a rule that maps what the shop looks like now to what the machines do next.

**The paint machine with a surprise.** Take the paint machine of chapter [[a02]]: set up for blue, 10 minutes per colour change, four orders waiting at minute 0. Their best order is A B D C, with 40 minutes of total tardiness. Now a fifth order E arrives at minute 35: blue, 5 minutes of painting, due at minute 50. Three ways to run the machine:

- **Plan once.** Keep A B D C and put E after it. A runs 0–20, a changeover 20–30, B 30–40 (15 late), D 40–65 (5 late), a changeover 65–75, C 75–90 (20 late), E 90–95 (45 late): **85 minutes**.
- **Re-plan when E arrives.** At minute 35, B started its changeover at minute 20, so it is *frozen*: the machine is free at minute 40, set up for red. Of the six orders of C, D and E from there (Exercise 1), E C D is best: a changeover 40–50, E 50–55 (5 late), C 55–70, a changeover, D 80–105 (45 late). With B's 15: **65 minutes**.
- **Clairvoyant.** Knowing at minute 0 that E would come, the best of all 120 orders is A C E B D: A 0–20, C 20–35, E 35–40, a changeover, B 50–60 (35 late), D 60–85 (25 late): **60 minutes**. It keeps the machine blue for E and sacrifices B.

Re-planning recovers 20 of the 25 minutes that foresight would save. The other 5 are the price of *myopia*: B went on the machine before anyone knew about E. Make E red and re-planning gives A B E D C with 50 minutes, the clairvoyant optimum, because B was the right thing to be doing anyway. Move E's arrival earlier in the widget and the gap closes: less is frozen when the news arrives.

## The snapshot of a running shop {#snapshot}

To plan again, the controller needs the shop's state in the terms of the model of chapter [[c01]]. The repository calls it a snapshot. Look first at the one the hero opens on, minute 117, when order 5 arrives. Order 0 is on a cart to m0: its next machine is settled, and so is when it gets there. Order 4 has finished on m0 and waits for a cart to m1: the destination is settled, the arrival time is not. Order 2 waits in the queue of m4, but nothing stops the planner from sending it to m1 instead. Machine m3 is broken and expected back at minute 129; order 3 is queued in front of it. The snapshot writes all this down in a form the planner can use.

::: define What is a snapshot?
**In plain words:** a photograph of the shop at an event, in the planner's language: what each order still has to do, where it may go next and how soon it can be there, and when each machine is free.

**More precisely:** at time $t$, a snapshot $\sigma(t)$ lists, for each machine, the time $\phi_m \ge t$ it is expected to be free and the family $g_m$ it is set up for, and, for each order $j$ with a decision left, its first undecided operation $\kappa_j$ and a ready map $\alpha_j : \mathcal R_j \to \mathbb Z$. Here $\mathcal R_j \subseteq \mathcal M_{j \kappa_j}$ is the set of machines it may still start on and $\alpha_j(m)$ the earliest minute it can be at $m$ (the ready time $R_{jm}$ of chapter [[c01#precedence]]). The model of chapter [[c01]] is solved for the operations $(j, k)$, $k \ge \kappa_j$, with $(j, \kappa_j)$ restricted to $\mathcal R_j$ and arriving no earlier than $\alpha_j(m)$.

- Frozen :: no longer changeable by a new plan: an operation already running, the destination of an order on or waiting for a cart.
- Free to change :: everything else; an order queued at a machine may still be sent to another eligible machine.
- Expected free time :: $\phi_m$ uses the *mean* repair time for a broken machine; the real one is hidden.
:::

$\phi_m$ and $g_m$ are those of chapter [[c01]] (and of the [notation page](notation.html#model)). The symbols $u$, $w$, $\kappa$ and $\operatorname{st}$ are local to this chapter, and $\rho$, $F$ and $A$ keep their course meanings (load, flow time, availability). An order is in one of five states (`JobState` in `jobshop/shop.py`), and each gives its ready map a different form. Write $\operatorname{st}_j$ for the station where order $j$ is, $\operatorname{st}(m) = m + 1$ for the station of machine $m$ (the load station is 0), $\tau$ for travel times and $\delta = 2$ for the allowance for a cart to come.

::: table
| State | First operation to plan | Machines $\mathcal R_j$ | Ready time $\alpha_j(m)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| new, at the load station | $k$ | all of $\mathcal M_{jk}$ | $t + \delta + \tau(\operatorname{st}_j, \operatorname{st}(m))$ |
| queued at a machine | $k$ | all of $\mathcal M_{jk}$ | $t$ if $\operatorname{st}(m) = \operatorname{st}_j$, else $t + \delta + \tau(\operatorname{st}_j, \operatorname{st}(m))$ |
| waiting for a cart | $k$ | its destination only | $t + \delta + \tau(\operatorname{st}_j, \operatorname{st}(\text{dest}))$ |
| on a cart | $k$ | its destination only | the cart's arrival time |
| processing on $m'$ | $k + 1$, none if $k$ is the last | all of $\mathcal M_{j, k+1}$ | $\phi_{m'} + \text{lag}(m', m)$ |
:::

with $\text{lag}(m', m) = 0$ if $m = m'$ and $\delta + \tau(\operatorname{st}(m'), \operatorname{st}(m))$ otherwise. The operation in progress is not in the plan: it appears only through $\phi_{m'}$. On the machine side,

$$\phi_m = u_m + w_m, \qquad u_m = \begin{cases} t & \text{if } m \text{ works,} \\ \max\big(\operatorname{round}(t^{\text{down}}_m + \text{MTTR}),\ t + 1\big) & \text{if } m \text{ is down since } t^{\text{down}}_m, \end{cases}$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} \phi_m &= u_m + w_m, \\ u_m &= t \text{ if } m \text{ works, else} \\ u_m &= \max(\operatorname{round}(t^{\text{down}}_m + \text{MTTR}), t + 1) \end{aligned}

where $w_m$ is the changeover and processing still to do on the operation in progress. The $t + 1$ covers a repair that has already lasted longer than MTTR: its expected end has passed, so the planner assumes the machine is back in one minute.

**Read one by hand.** At minute 117 the table lists six orders. The travel times used are $\tau(1, 2) = \tau(4, 1) = \tau(4, 3) = \tau(0, 1) = 2$ and $\tau(5, 2) = 3$.

- Order 0 is on a cart to m0 for its last operation: frozen, $\alpha = 117$, the cart's arrival time.
- Order 4 is at m0 (station 1) and waits for a cart to m1 (station 2): frozen destination, $\alpha = 117 + 2 + \tau(1, 2) = 121$.
- Order 2 is queued at m4 (station 5): $\alpha(\text{m4}) = 117$ if it stays, $117 + 2 + \tau(5, 2) = 122$ on m1.
- Order 3 is queued at m3 (station 4): $\alpha = 117$ on m3, since it stands there, although m3 is down and expected back only at 129 (that is $\phi_3$, below); $117 + 2 + 2 = 121$ on m0 or m2.
- Order 1 is processed on m4, free at 120; its next operation can only run on m4: $\alpha = 120$.
- Order 5 is new at the load station; its first operation runs only on m0 (station 1): $\alpha = 117 + 2 + \tau(0, 1) = 121$.

The machines: $\phi = (117, 117, 136, 129, 120)$, since m2 broke at 116 and is expected at $116 + 20$, m3 at $109 + 20$. CP-SAT returns a plan with objective 1,194, not proven optimal within its budget, against 1,227 for the greedy warm start. It keeps order 3 on m3 (expected back at 129, a 4-minute changeover from family 0 to 2, start at 133) and plans orders 0 and 5 on m0.

## Re-planning at events, executing the beginning {#loop}

The repository's controller, optimiser re-planning (CP-SAT), does what the paint example did by hand.

::: define What is event-driven re-planning on a rolling horizon?
**In plain words:** every time something happens (an arrival, a breakdown, a repair), take a snapshot, make a new plan for all the orders in the shop, and follow it until the next event replaces it. Only the beginning of each plan is ever carried out.

**More precisely:** with event times $t_1 \le t_2 \le \dots$, the controller computes at $t_i$ a plan for the orders $\mathcal J_i$ (the shop's orders, at most the 25 most urgent, Part 5),

$$\pi_i \in \Pi(\sigma(t_i)), \qquad J(\pi_i) \le J(\pi_i^{\text{warm}}), \qquad J(\pi) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal J_i} \big(10\, T_j(\pi) + C_j(\pi)\big),$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} &\pi_i \in \Pi(\sigma(t_i)),\ J(\pi_i) \le J(\pi_i^{\text{warm}}), \\ &J(\pi) = \textstyle\sum_{j \in \mathcal J_i} (10 T_j + C_j) \end{aligned}

where $\Pi(\sigma)$ is the set of plans consistent with the snapshot and $\pi_i^{\text{warm}}$ the greedy warm start of Part 4: $\pi_i$ is the best plan CP-SAT finds within its budget. When CP-SAT proves it optimal, $\pi_i \in \arg\min_{\Pi(\sigma(t_i))} J$; that holds for 56 % of the benchmark's re-plans without breakdowns, 42 % with them, and 34 of the 37 non-empty plans of the hero's morning. The shop follows $\pi_i$ on $[t_i, t_{i+1})$. The controller is the map from the state to the first part of the plan computed from it.

- Receding horizon :: plan far, act near, plan again; the plan's window moves with time.
- Event-driven :: re-planning is triggered by events, not by a clock.
- Plan drift :: what happens differently from the plan before the next event.
:::

**The idea from control.** This is the receding-horizon principle of model predictive control, taught in [the MPC course](../mpc-lmpc-lab/a06-receding-horizon-from-a-plan-to-feedback.html): an open-loop optimiser becomes a feedback law because it is re-run on the measured state [[ref:3]]. Here the state is discrete; the trigger is an event, not a sampling period, since between events nothing changes that the plan did not predict, except the carts' timing (Part 6); and each plan runs every known order to completion, so the horizon grows with arrivals and shrinks as orders leave.

**Watch it in the hero.** At every event, the shaded band of the top chart is all of the plan that will ever be carried out; press *Next event* and the rest is thrown away. Step to event 20 (minute 109): m3 breaks down, and the new plan sends order 3 from the queue of m0 to m3, expected back at 129; at event 21 the table shows it queued at m3. That transfer is the only physical act of a re-plan. At event 22 (minute 117) order 5 arrives and 4 of the 15 operations shared with the previous plan change machine: tick the outline to see which. At event 24 (minute 128) m3 is repaired a minute early and no operation changes machine.

**How many re-plans a day?** One per arrival, breakdown and repair. Without breakdowns, exactly the 60 arrivals; the runs file shows 60 on every test day. With breakdowns, each machine alternates up periods of mean MTBF and repairs of mean MTTR: an alternating renewal process whose cycles last MTBF + MTTR = 140 minutes on average (chapter [[b01#breakdowns]]). The elementary renewal theorem says that the expected number of cycles begun by time $t$, divided by $t$, tends to $1/(\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR})$ [[ref:5]]. So over a day of length $C_{\max}$ a machine fails about $C_{\max}/140$ times, and each failure brings two events. With $C_{\max} = 1415$, the mean makespan of the CP-SAT runs with breakdowns in the runs file,

$$\mathbb E[\text{re-plans}] \approx n + 2 \cdot 5 \cdot \frac{C_{\max}}{\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR}} = 60 + 10 \cdot \frac{1415}{140} = 161.1 .$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[\text{re-plans}] &\approx n + 2 \cdot 5 \cdot \tfrac{C_{\max}}{\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR}} \\ &= 60 + 10 \cdot \tfrac{1415}{140} = 161.1 \end{aligned}

The runs file records 158.9 on average (from 137 to 177). The days differ by 11.4 re-plans (standard deviation); compared day by day with $60 + 10 \cdot C_{\max,i}/140$, the count falls short by 2.2 on average, with a standard error of 2.5: within one standard error, so noise explains the gap. The hero's morning has 43 events in 314 minutes, one every 7.3 minutes: 12 arrivals, 16 breakdowns, 15 repairs (Exercise 4 asks whether that many is surprising). The 6 before the first order (minute 51) give an empty plan; of the other 37, 34 are proven optimal and 3 stop at the budget. Simultaneous events re-plan one after the other: at minute 51 an order arrives, m3 breaks down and m2 is repaired.

**Executing the beginning: strict plan following.** Between events, the plan answers the simulator's two questions (chapter [[b01]]). *Where does this order go?* To its planned machine. *Which waiting order does this free machine start?* The next one in the plan's order on that machine, and if it is not there yet, the machine **waits for it**, even with other orders in its queue. The idle time is deliberate: the plan grouped families and timed the arrivals, and grabbing whatever is present breaks the grouping. The repository chose this on validation days, before the test: mean tardiness 8.2 against 9.0 minutes on six validation seeds with CP-SAT. With the greedy planner on the test days, as an illustration only: 3.07 against 4.90 minutes without breakdowns, 24.03 against 26.61 with them.

**Orders the plan does not know.** Orders left out by the cap (Part 5) are not in the plan. They are routed by the earliest-expected-finish rule of chapter [[b02#routing]], and a waiting machine may start one only if it *fits*: if its changeover and processing end by the planned start of the machine's next planned operation. The most urgent that fits goes first. The test is not a guarantee: it ignores the changeover from the unplanned order's family back to the planned one, and it assumes nothing breaks before that start, so an unplanned order can still delay the planned one, by up to one changeover (at most 10 minutes) when the planned order arrives on time and nothing breaks. On the benchmark the cap never binds, so this never arises there.

## Greedy list scheduling: warm start and ablation {#greedy}

A complete plan can be built without a solver, by **list scheduling**: place operations one at a time, each at the earliest moment it can start given what is already placed.

::: define What is greedy list scheduling?
**In plain words:** look at the next unplaced operation of every order, work out for each eligible machine when it would start and finish there, place the best by a simple preference, and repeat. No choice is revisited.

**More precisely:** with $\phi_m$ and $g_m$ from the snapshot, a candidate (operation $(j, k)$, machine $m$) gets

$$S = \max(\phi_m,\ \alpha_{jkm}) + s_m(g_m, f(j)), \qquad E = S + p_{jkm},$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} S &= \max(\phi_m, \alpha_{jkm}) + s_m(g_m, f(j)), \\ E &= S + p_{jkm} \end{aligned}

with $\alpha_{jkm} = \alpha_j(m)$ for the first operation and $\alpha_{jkm} = E_{j,k-1} + \text{lag}(m_{j,k-1}, m)$ afterwards. A preference key picks one candidate; it is placed, $\phi_m$ becomes $E$, $g_m$ becomes $f(j)$, and the order's next operation becomes a candidate.

- "finish" :: earliest end first.
- "start" :: earliest start first, then earliest due date.
- "edd" :: most urgent order first.
- "previous" :: the previous plan's machines and order, re-timed; new operations afterwards by earliest end.
:::

**By hand, on the minute-117 snapshot.** The first step has nine candidates: the next operation of each of the six orders on each machine of its ready map, with $\phi$, $\alpha$ and $g = (1, 0, \text{none}, 0, 3)$ from Part 2 and the changeovers of the workshop.

::: table num
| Order (operation) | Machine | $\phi_m$ | $\alpha$ | Changeover | $S$ | $p$ | $E$ | Due |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (3) | m0 | 117 | 117 | 6 | 123 | 8 | 131 | 207 |
| 1 (1) | m4 | 120 | 120 | 0 | 120 | 17 | 137 | 358 |
| 2 (0) | m1 | 117 | 122 | 5 | 127 | 26 | 153 | 294 |
| 2 (0) | m4 | 120 | 117 | 0 | 120 | 23 | 143 | 294 |
| 3 (0) | m0 | 117 | 121 | 9 | 130 | 16 | 146 | 321 |
| 3 (0) | m2 | 136 | 121 | 0 | 136 | 19 | 155 | 321 |
| 3 (0) | m3 | 129 | 117 | 4 | 133 | 16 | 149 | 321 |
| 4 (1) | m1 | 117 | 121 | 3 | 124 | 18 | 142 | 286 |
| 5 (0) | m0 | 117 | 121 | 0 | 121 | 9 | 130 | 327 |
:::

"finish" takes the earliest end: order 5 on m0, 121–130. "start" takes the earliest start, 120, and breaks the tie between orders 1 and 2 on m4 by due date: order 2, 120–143. "edd" takes the most urgent order, 0 (due 207), on m0, 123–131. After "finish" has placed order 5, m0 is free at 130, so order 0 there now starts at $130 + 6 = 136$, and order 5's next operation joins the candidates; "finish" then places order 1 on m4 (120–137) and order 4 on m1 (124–142). The keys disagree from the first step on and build four different plans: objectives 1,262 (finish), 1,227 (start), 1,362 (edd) and 1,239 (previous). The warm start is the cheapest, 1,227, and CP-SAT improves it to 1,194.

**The cost.** A plan has $N$ placement steps, $N$ the number of operations to plan (19 here); each scans the next operation of at most $n$ orders on at most three machines, $n$ the number of orders planned. So a plan costs $\mathcal O(3Nn)$ evaluations: 0.47 ms per re-plan without breakdowns and 0.51 ms with them, from the runs file. The repository uses it twice. **As the warm start**, `warm_start` builds three greedy plans (finish, start, edd), plus the re-timed previous plan when there is one, and hands the best to CP-SAT as a complete hint (chapter [[c02#warm]], [[ref:4]]), and keeps it as the fallback. On the hero's morning CP-SAT beat its warm start in 14 of the 37 plans, by 211 objective units in total and 33 at most; switch the hero to "Greedy warm start" to compare. **As the ablation**, quick re-planning (Greedy replan): an ablation is the same system with one component removed, here the solver. The controller with `use_solver=False` re-plans with the greedy plan alone, which separates what re-planning does from what the solver adds.

**Why carry the previous plan forward?** Because re-timing it cannot hurt when nothing went worse than predicted.

**Proposition.** Let $\pi$ be a plan made for snapshot $\sigma$, and $\sigma'$ a later snapshot that contains no order $\pi$ did not plan, in which every ready time and every machine's free time is no later than $\pi$ assumed and every machine is set up as $\pi$ expected. Re-time $\pi$ on $\sigma'$, keeping each operation's machine and each machine's order. Then no operation starts later than in $\pi$, and $J$ over the orders of $\sigma'$ does not increase.

*Proof.* Place the operations in increasing order of their start in $\pi$: this respects each machine's order and each order's sequence, and it is what the "previous" key does. Since $\pi$ is feasible in the model, every operation $o$ of order $j$ on machine $m$ satisfies $S_o \ge \max(E_{\text{pred}}, \alpha_o) + s_m(g, f(j))$, where $E_{\text{pred}}$ is the end of its predecessor on $m$ (or $\phi_m$ if $o$ is first), $\alpha_o$ its ready time, and $g$ the family of that predecessor (or $g_m$). Suppose all operations placed so far start and end no later than in $\pi$. Re-timing gives $o$ the start $S'_o = \max(E'_{\text{pred}}, \alpha'_o) + s_m(g, f(j))$. Its machine predecessor ends no later (or, if $o$ is first, $\phi'_m \le \phi_m$ by assumption); its job predecessor ends no later and the lag depends only on the two machines, so $\alpha'_o \le \alpha_o$; and the changeover $s_m(g, f(j))$ is unchanged because the preceding family is. Since $\max$ is nondecreasing in each argument, $S'_o \le \max(E_{\text{pred}}, \alpha_o) + s_m(g, f(j)) \le S_o$ and $E'_o \le E_o$. By induction every $C'_j \le C_j$, and $10 \max(0, C_j - d_j) + C_j$ is nondecreasing in $C_j$. $\square$

An objective nondecreasing in every completion time is called *regular* [[ref:2]]. Since `solve_snapshot` returns the better of the solver's plan and the warm start, and the warm start is no worse than the re-timed plan, **in the nominal case, with no new order, the new plan costs no more than the old one on the same orders**. With an arrival, the "previous" key still places all of $\pi$'s operations first, so $\pi$'s orders finish no later inside the re-timed plan; but the solver minimises the total, newcomer included, and may make old orders later, as D in Part 1 (5 minutes late in the first plan, 45 after re-planning).

What is checked? The test `test_previous_plan_is_reused_as_a_starting_point` and the numbers script's re-timing of each of the morning's 37 CP-SAT plans on its own snapshot (cost increased in none) cover only $\sigma' = \sigma$. The script also re-times each plan on the next snapshot whenever no order arrived in between. At the 7 repairs that came no later than MTTR, the cost of the same orders never increased; at 2 of the 6 later repairs it rose, by 6 and 13 units, and at 10 of the 12 breakdowns. A repair earlier than expected is the typical nominal event. A breakdown, a late repair or an arrival is not, and the new plan may then cost more, as it should.

## The 25-job cap {#cap}

The model of chapter [[c01]] grows faster than the shop. A fresh order brings an optional interval per eligible machine of each operation: 9, 7, 10 and 8 for families 0 to 3. Worse, the circuit on a machine with $c$ candidate operations has an arc from the start node to each candidate and back, a self-loop per candidate, every ordered pair, and the empty-machine loop:

$$1 + 3c + c(c - 1) = (c + 1)^2 \text{ arcs.}$$
%narrow 1 + 3c + c(c - 1) = (c + 1)^2 \text{ arcs}

Each arc is a Boolean literal; all but the $c$ self-loops are new ones (a self-loop reuses the literal "not on this machine"). Each arc leaving the start node carries two conditional constraints, and each arc between two operations one or two (the second only when the changeover is nonzero). With one fresh order per family the machines have $q = (6, 7, 8, 6, 7)$ candidates; with $n$ fresh orders in an even mix ($n$ a multiple of 4), $c_m = n\, q_m / 4$, and the circuits have exactly $\sum_m (c_m + 1)^2 = 14.625\, n^2 + 17 n + 5$ arcs, so about $14.625\, n^2$: 1,077 for 8 fresh orders, 8,837 for 24, 24,085 for 40. The solver's deterministic budget is fixed (chapter [[c02#warm]]), so a larger model gets less search per variable.

The controller therefore plans at most 25 orders, the most urgent by due date. **On the benchmark the cap never binds**: the runs file has no capped CP-SAT re-plan, and in the greedy runs the shop never held more than 16 orders at an event without breakdowns and 21 with them. The cap counts every order in the shop, including those on their last operation, which have nothing left to plan; counts of snapshot orders, as in chapter [[c02]], are smaller. What a tighter cap would cost, with the greedy planner on the test days (an illustration, not a choice of setting):

::: table num
| Greedy re-planning, cap | 25 (or none) | 10 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| No breakdowns, mean tardiness (min) | 3.07 | 2.86 | 8.07 |
| Frequent breakdowns, mean tardiness (min) | 24.03 | 22.89 | 41.95 |
| Changeover time per day, no breakdowns / frequent (min) | 633 / 618 | 643 / 635 | 772 / 770 |
:::

A cap of 10 was even slightly lower on these days: against a cap of 25, −0.21 [−1.12, 0.74] minutes without breakdowns and −1.14 [−4.35, 2.15] with them (paired differences on the same days, 95 % bootstrap intervals). Both intervals contain zero, so this is noise, and the table illustrates rather than tunes. A cap of 5 costs +5.00 [+2.69, +8.09] and +17.92 [+13.26, +23.42] minutes and adds 139 and 152 minutes of changeovers a day, probably because a plan of five orders is too short to group families.

## What a plan cannot see {#blind}

Re-planning corrects a plan after the fact; it cannot make the plan see what its model leaves out.

**Myopia.** $J$ sums over the orders in the shop. An order arriving in ten minutes counts for nothing, so a plan may leave a machine set up for the wrong family, as with E in Part 1: the plan has no terminal cost for the state it leaves behind. Exercise 5 measures the opposite: what is lost by re-planning *less* often.

**Certainty equivalence.** A broken machine is expected back after exactly MTTR = 20 minutes; a real repair lasts from 10 to 30. Start with two values: a repair takes 10 or 30 minutes with probability 1/2 each, mean 20, and an order is planned to finish exactly at its due date behind it. With 10 minutes it is early, tardiness 0; with 30 it is 10 minutes late. The expected tardiness is 5, against a planned 0: the early case cannot make up for the late one, because tardiness stops at 0. Replacing a random quantity by its mean inside an optimisation is called *certainty equivalence*, and for tardiness it is optimistic.

The general statement is **Jensen's inequality**: for a convex function $\varphi$ and a random variable $X$, $\mathbb E[\varphi(X)] \ge \varphi(\mathbb E[X])$. For a variable with two values it is the definition of convexity, the chord lies above the curve; the general case is a standard result that we cite [[ref:6]]. Why is tardiness convex in a repair duration $D$? In the planner's model (fixed machine sequences, fixed transport lags), every start and end is computed from free times, ready times and durations by $+$ and $\max$ only. Unfolded, a completion time is the largest, over chains of operations, of a starting time plus the durations along the chain. $D$ enters only through the broken machine's free time $\phi_m = t^{\text{down}}_m + D + w_m$, and a chain starts there or not, so it contains $D$ once with coefficient 1 or not at all. Hence $C_j(D) = \max(c_1, c_2 + D)$ for two constants $c_1$, $c_2$: convex and nondecreasing. Then $T_j = \max(0, C_j - d_j)$ is a maximum of such functions, convex too, and

$$\mathbb E\big[T_j(D)\big] \ \ge\ T_j\big(\mathbb E[D]\big) .$$
%narrow \mathbb E[T_j(D)] \ge T_j(\mathbb E[D])

A plan's tardiness is a lower bound on what that plan should expect. In the simulator the order of events can itself change with $D$ (carts come first come, first served; the fit test of Part 3 depends on timing), so the bound holds for the plan, not exactly for the shop. With $D$ uniform on $[10, 30]$, the order planned to finish exactly at its due date expects

$$\mathbb E[\max(0, D - 20)] = \int_{20}^{30} \frac{x - 20}{20}\, dx = \frac{10^2}{40} = 2.5 \text{ minutes},$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[\max(0, D - 20)] &= \textstyle\int_{20}^{30} \tfrac{x - 20}{20}\, dx \\ &= \tfrac{10^2}{40} = 2.5 \text{ min} \end{aligned}

and $(10 - \beta)^2 / 40$ with $\beta$ minutes of slack, 0.625 at $\beta = 5$ (Exercise 3).

**The carts are a fixed lag.** The model has no carts (AGVs): a move is a lag $\tau(a, b) + \delta$, as if a cart always came 2 minutes after being called. In the simulator they are real resources, first come, first served. Over the 20 test days with breakdowns (greedy re-planning, 5,000 loaded moves) the wait for a cart has median 2 minutes, which is where $\delta$ comes from, but mean 2.91 and 90th percentile 7: 24 % of moves do not wait at all and 48 % wait more than 2 minutes.

**Plan drift.** Compare each operation's actual start with its start in the last plan made before it. On the hero's morning, of the 36 operations started before minute 314, 28 started exactly on time, 7 early and 1 late, never by more than 2 minutes (mean −0.31). Drift is reset at the next event, which replaces the plan by one built on the real state: 7.3 minutes later on average on this morning, 8.9 over a day with breakdowns (1,415 minutes for 158.9 events) and 22.4 without. Between two events drift can build up along a chain of cart moves; on this morning it stayed within 2 minutes. That is what feedback buys, and why the fixed-lag approximation costs little here.

## Nervousness {#nervous}

Step the hero from event 21 to event 22 (minute 117, order 5 arrives) with the outline on: of the 15 operations in both plans, 4 change machine, and planned starts move by 4.2 minutes on average. A controller that re-plans 158.9 times a day on average may change its mind as often. In a real workshop that costs something the simulator does not charge: operators prepare the next order, material is staged. Production planners call a schedule that changes too often *nervous*, its opposite *stable* [[ref:1]].

::: define How is nervousness measured?
**In plain words:** compare each new plan with the one it replaces, on the operations in both: how many moved to another machine, how far their planned start moved, and on how many machines the next job changed.

**More precisely:** for consecutive plans $\pi_{i-1}$, $\pi_i$ with common operations $\mathcal O_i$,

$$\begin{aligned} \nu^{\text{mach}}_i &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal O_i|} \sum_{o \in \mathcal O_i} \mathbb 1\big[m_i(o) \ne m_{i-1}(o)\big], \\ \nu^{\text{start}}_i &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal O_i|} \sum_{o \in \mathcal O_i} \big|S_i(o) - S_{i-1}(o)\big|, \end{aligned}$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} \nu^{\text{mach}}_i &= \tfrac{1}{|\mathcal O_i|} \textstyle\sum_{o} \mathbb 1[m_i(o) \ne m_{i-1}(o)], \\ \nu^{\text{start}}_i &= \tfrac{1}{|\mathcal O_i|} \textstyle\sum_o |S_i(o) - S_{i-1}(o)| \end{aligned}

pooled over a day with weights $|\mathcal O_i|$, together with the share of machines whose first planned operation after $t_i$ changed.
:::

Neither controller pays for changing its mind, so these numbers describe, they are not minimised. ## The code behind it {#code}

The snapshot is a direct transcription of the table of Part 2.

::: code jobshop/cpsat.py:take_snapshot
`machine_free` is $\phi_m$, from `shop.expected_free`: work left on the current operation plus the expected end of a repair. `machine_family` is $g_m$. A `PROCESSING` order on its last operation is skipped; otherwise its next operation is planned from its machine's free time plus the lag. `IN_TRANSIT` keeps the destination at the cart's arrival `jr.eta`, `WAIT_TRANSPORT` the destination after allowance and travel. The last branch, `QUEUED` or `NEW`, is where rerouting lives: every eligible machine, at `shop.now` if the order is standing there.
:::

The controller re-plans in `on_event`, called at every arrival, breakdown and repair.

::: code jobshop/cpsat.py:CPSATController.on_event
Orders are sorted by due date and cut to `max_jobs`. `solve_snapshot` receives the previous plan, which feeds the "previous" greedy rule. The new plan replaces the old one, and `sequence_of` stores each machine's planned operations by start: the order `sequence` will follow. The last loop is the only physical action of a re-plan: a queued order the new plan wants elsewhere is sent there with `shop.reroute`. `shop.kick` then lets idle machines ask again.
:::

Executing the beginning of the plan is `sequence`, called whenever a free machine has a queue.

::: code jobshop/cpsat.py:CPSATController.sequence
Entries that have started are discarded first. With `strict`, only the head of the machine's planned order counts: start it if it is in the queue; otherwise start the most urgent unplanned order that fits before the head's planned start, or return `None` and wait. The `fits` test adds the unplanned order's changeover and processing to `shop.now`, but not the changeover back to the head's family: that is the gap noted in Part 3. The non-strict branch takes the earliest planned order present, else the most urgent. `route`, just above in the file, sends an order to its planned machine, or to `route_earliest_finish` if it is unplanned.
:::

The greedy planner, warm start and ablation:

::: code jobshop/cpsat.py:greedy_plan
`free`, `family` and `next_op` are $\phi_m$, $g_m$ and each order's next operation. A first operation may only go to a machine of its ready map, which is how frozen destinations are respected; later ones arrive after `transport_lag`. The start is `max(free[k], arrive)` plus the changeover, the formula of Part 4. The "previous" key `(0, old start, ...)` puts first every operation that keeps its old machine, in the order of its old start, which is the order of the proof. `warm_start`, below it in the file, keeps the cheapest of three greedy plans, plus the re-timed previous plan when there is one.
:::

## Common pitfalls {#pitfalls}

1. **"The executed schedule is the last plan."** No plan is executed in full; the executed schedule is stitched from the beginnings of 60 to 177 plans a day, and its cost is not the cost of any of them.
2. **"Re-planning more often is always better."** In the nominal case a new plan cannot be worse *on the snapshot*, but the snapshot is optimistic, so it can be worse in reality. At minute 109 (event 20 of the hero) m3 breaks down, and the plan sends order 3 from the queue of m0 to m3, expected back at 129, with a planned start at 133. The repair took 19 minutes, so it worked; a 30-minute repair would have left order 3, a cart trip later, waiting at a broken machine until minute 139. And every change disturbs the shop. What helps is re-planning when information changes, which is what events are.
3. **"The plan's tardiness is what to expect."** It is a lower bound for that plan, by Jensen's inequality.
4. **"Frozen means fixed in time."** A frozen destination fixes the machine, not the start.
5. **"The solver is where the gain comes from."** About two thirds of it come from re-planning with a greedy plan; the solver adds the last third.

## Check your understanding {#check}

:::: qa
Try to answer each question before opening it.

::: q 1. What turns a planner into a controller?
Re-running it on the measured state whenever the state changes in a way the plan did not predict, and applying only the beginning of each plan.
:::
::: q 2. Why is an order on a cart treated differently from an order in a queue?
The cart is already driving to its destination, so its ready map keeps that one machine at the known arrival time. A queued order can still be put on a cart to any eligible machine, at the cost of allowance and travel.
:::
::: q 3. Why does an operation in progress not appear in the plan?
Nothing about it can be decided any more. It enters the snapshot only through its machine's free time $\phi_m$ and the ready time of its order's next operation.
:::
::: q 4. Why re-plan at events and not every minute?
Between events nothing happens that the plan did not predict, except the carts' timing, so a re-plan would essentially reproduce the plan. Events are when information arrives.
:::
::: q 5. Why does strict plan following let a machine stand idle with orders in its queue?
The plan wants another order next, typically of the same family or more urgent and about to arrive; starting a queued order would spend a changeover and break the grouping. Unplanned orders may fill the gap if they finish in time.
:::
::: q 6. What does the "previous" greedy rule guarantee, and when?
Re-timing the previous plan does not increase the objective on the same orders if no order is new, every ready time and free time is no later than the plan assumed, and every machine is set up as expected: with a regular objective, earlier completions cannot cost more.
:::
::: q 7. Why is the planned tardiness optimistic?
The planner replaces random repair times by their mean. In its model a completion time has the form $\max(c_1, c_2 + D)$ in a repair duration $D$, so tardiness is convex in $D$, and by Jensen's inequality its expectation is at least its value at the mean.
:::
::: q 8. How much of CP-SAT's gain over the best rule comes from the solver?
About a third: −1.7 of −5.7 minutes without breakdowns, −4.7 of −14.8 with them. The rest comes from re-planning with a greedy plan, at about a three-thousandth of the computing time.
:::

:::: exercise When re-timing hurts

(a) On one machine with a 5-minute changeover between families 0 and 1 in either direction, find two orders and a plan whose re-timed version costs more although the machine is free *earlier* than planned. :::: exercise The price of betting on the mean
A repair lasts $D$ uniform on $[10, 30]$ minutes and the planner assumes 20. An order is planned to finish $\beta$ minutes before its due date, $0 \le \beta \le 10$, and each minute of repair delays it by a minute. Show that its expected tardiness is $(10 - \beta)^2 / 40$.

:::: exercise Count the re-plans of one morning

::: solution
By the elementary renewal theorem each machine fails about $314/140 = 2.24$ times in the window, so the prediction is $12 + 10 \cdot 314/140 = 34.4$ events, 22.4 of them breakdowns and repairs. Test day 0 has 43: 16 breakdowns and 15 repairs, 31 downtime events. Over 2,000 simulated mornings the count of downtime events before minute 314 has mean 21.7 (slightly below 22.4, since a repair under way at minute 314 has not ended yet) and standard deviation 5.4, and 31 or more happen on 5.8 % of mornings: the hero's morning is an unusually broken one, a realisation from the tail, not a failure of the formula.                    for s in range(2000)])
print(counts.mean(), counts.std(ddof=1), (counts >= 31).mean())     # 21.7 5.4 0.058

rp = np.array([float(r["replans"]) for r in rows]); mk = np.array([float(r["makespan"]) for r in rows])
gap = rp - (60 + 10 * mk / 140)
print(rp.mean(), gap.mean(), gap.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(gap)))  # 158.9 -2.2 2.5

:::: exercise Re-plan only at arrivals
Write a controller that re-plans with the greedy planner at arrivals only, ignoring breakdowns and repairs, and run it on the 20 test days with frequent breakdowns. Compare with greedy re-planning at every event, 24.03 minutes.
::: hint
Subclass `CPSATController`, call the parent's `on_event` only when `kind == "arrival"`, and use `use_solver=False` so the 20 runs take seconds.
:::
::: solution
```python
import numpy as np
from jobshop.cpsat import CPSATController
from jobshop.metrics import run_metrics
from jobshop.scenario import LEVELS, make_scenario, make_workshop
from jobshop.shop import Shop

class ArrivalsOnly(CPSATController):
    def on_event(self, shop, kind, **info):
        if kind == "arrival":
            super().on_event(shop, kind, **info)

ws = make_workshop(); tard = []
for seed in range(20):
    sc = make_scenario(ws, seed, mtbf=LEVELS["frequent breakdowns"])
    log = Shop(ws, sc, ArrivalsOnly(use_solver=False)).run()
    tard.append(run_metrics(ws, sc, log)["mean_tardiness"])
print(np.mean(tard))     # 28.80
```
The mean tardiness rises from 24.03 to 28.80 minutes: between arrivals, orders wait for a broken machine the plan still counts on, and a repaired machine waits for the next arrival to be used. Re-planning on information, not only on new work, is worth almost 5 minutes here.
:::

## The chapter on one page {#summary}

::: steps
- **A controller re-plans**: at every event, snapshot, plan all known orders, execute only the beginning; the receding horizon, driven by events. Each plan is the best CP-SAT finds within its budget, proven optimal in 56 % and 42 % of the benchmark's re-plans.
- **The snapshot** freezes running operations and the destinations of orders on or waiting for a cart; queued orders may be rerouted; a broken machine is expected back after the mean repair time.
- **Strict plan following** makes a machine wait for its next planned order; unplanned orders only fill gaps they fit into, and may still delay the planned one by a changeover.
- **Greedy list scheduling** builds a plan in under a millisecond: the warm start and, alone, the ablation. Re-timing the previous plan cannot increase a regular objective in the nominal case.
- **The 25-job cap** bounds a model whose circuit has $(c + 1)^2$ arcs per machine; it never binds on the benchmark.
- **What a plan cannot see**: future orders (myopia), the spread of repair times (certainty equivalence, optimistic by Jensen), the carts (median wait 2 minutes, mean 2.91). On one morning, drift stayed within 2 minutes.
- **Nervousness**: consecutive CP-SAT plans move 14.2 % of their common operations and shift starts by 3.7 minutes; nothing limits it.
- **Results**: re-planning earns 69 % and 68 % of CP-SAT's gain over the best rule, with fewer changeovers among its visible effects; the solver adds −1.7 and −4.7 minutes.
:::

### The vocabulary, in one table

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Snapshot | The running shop in the planner's terms: free times, families, each order's first open operation and ready map. |
| Frozen | Not changeable by a new plan: running operations, destinations of orders on or waiting for a cart. |
| Event-driven, receding horizon | A new plan at every event; act on its beginning, plan again from the new state. |
| Strict plan following | Machines run their planned order and wait for the next planned job. |
| List scheduling | Placing operations one by one at their earliest feasible start. |
| Ablation | The same system with one component removed, to measure what that component adds. |
| Regular objective | A cost nondecreasing in every completion time. |
| Myopia, certainty equivalence | Planning without future orders; planning with mean durations. |
| Plan drift, nervousness | Reality against the plan; a plan against the previous one. |

## A plan is a forecast; a controller re-forecasts {#idea}

Chapters [[c01]] and [[c02]] built a planner: given the orders in the shop, it returns the best plan it can find. But a plan is made from what is known at one instant; minutes later an order arrives, or a machine stops, and the plan is a stale forecast.

The remedy is the one a navigation app uses: **make the plan again whenever the world changes, and only ever act on its first steps**. Used this way, a planner becomes a *controller*: a rule that maps what the shop looks like now to what the machines do next.

**The paint machine with a surprise.** The paint machine of chapter [[a02]] is set up for blue, needs 10 minutes per colour change, and has four orders at minute 0; their best order is A B D C, 40 minutes of total tardiness. A fifth order E arrives at minute 35: blue, 5 minutes of painting, due at 50. Three ways to run the machine:

- **Plan once.** Keep A B D C and append E: A 0–20, a changeover, B 30–40 (15 late), D 40–65 (5 late), a changeover, C 75–90 (20 late), E 90–95 (45 late): **85 minutes**.
- **Re-plan when E arrives.** B started its changeover at minute 20, so it is *frozen*: the machine is free at 40, set up for red. The best of the six orders of C, D and E (Exercise 1) is E C D: E 50–55 (5 late), C 55–70, D 80–105 (45 late). With B's 15: **65 minutes**.
- **Clairvoyant.** Knowing E from the start, the best of all 120 orders is A C E B D: A 0–20, C 20–35, E 35–40, B 50–60 (35 late), D 60–85 (25 late): **60 minutes**. It keeps the machine blue for E and sacrifices B.

Re-planning recovers 20 of the 25 minutes that foresight would save. The other 5 are the price of *myopia*: B went on the machine before anyone knew about E. Make E red and re-planning reaches the clairvoyant optimum, A B E D C with 50 minutes. Move E's arrival earlier and the gap closes: less is frozen when the news arrives.

## The snapshot of a running shop {#snapshot}

To plan again, the controller needs the shop's state in the terms of chapter [[c01]]. At minute 117, where the hero opens, order 0 is on a cart to m0: where and when it arrives are settled. Order 2 waits at m4, but may still be sent to m1.

::: define What is a snapshot?
**In plain words:** a photograph of the shop at an event, in the planner's language: what each order still has to do, where it may go next and how soon it can be there, and when each machine is free.

**More precisely:** the snapshot $\Sigma_t$ at time $t$ lists, for each machine, the time $\phi_m \ge t$ it is expected to be free and the family $g_m$ it is set up for, and, for each order $j$ with a decision left, its first undecided operation $\kappa_j$, the machines $\mathcal R_j \subseteq \mathcal M_{j \kappa_j}$ it may still start on and, for each $m \in \mathcal R_j$, the ready time $R_{jm}$, the earliest minute it can be at $m$. The model of chapter [[c01]] is solved for the operations $(j, k)$, $k \ge \kappa_j$, with $(j, \kappa_j)$ restricted to $\mathcal R_j$ and arriving no earlier than $R_{jm}$ ([[c01#precedence]]).

- Frozen :: no longer changeable by a new plan: an operation already running, the destination of an order on or waiting for a cart.
- Free to change :: everything else; an order queued at a machine may still be sent to another eligible machine.
- Expected free time :: $\phi_m$ uses the *mean* repair time for a broken machine; the real one is hidden.
:::

Each of the five states of an order (`JobState` in `jobshop/shop.py`) gives its ready times a different form. Write $\operatorname{st}_j$ for the station of order $j$, $\operatorname{st}(m) = m + 1$ for that of machine $m$ (the load station is 0), $\delta = 2$ for the cart allowance, and $\ell(m', m)$ for the transport lag of chapter [[c01#precedence]]: 0 if $m = m'$, else $\delta + \tau(\operatorname{st}(m'), \operatorname{st}(m))$.

::: table
| State | First operation to plan | Machines $\mathcal R_j$ | Ready time $R_{jm}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| new, at the load station | $k$ | all of $\mathcal M_{jk}$ | $t + \delta + \tau(\operatorname{st}_j, \operatorname{st}(m))$ |
| queued at a machine | $k$ | all of $\mathcal M_{jk}$ | $t$ if $\operatorname{st}(m) = \operatorname{st}_j$, else $t + \delta + \tau(\operatorname{st}_j, \operatorname{st}(m))$ |
| waiting for a cart | $k$ | its destination only | $t + \delta + \tau(\operatorname{st}_j, \operatorname{st}(\text{dest}))$ |
| on a cart | $k$ | its destination only | the cart's arrival time |
| processing on $m'$ | $k + 1$, none if $k$ is the last | all of $\mathcal M_{j, k+1}$ | $\phi_{m'} + \ell(m', m)$ |
:::

The operation in progress is not in the plan: it appears only through $\phi_{m'}$. On the machine side,

$$\phi_m = \hat t^{\,\text{up}}_m + \mathrm{rem}_m, \qquad \hat t^{\,\text{up}}_m = \begin{cases} t & \text{if } m \text{ works,} \\ \max\big(\operatorname{round}(t^{\text{down}}_m + \text{MTTR}),\ t + 1\big) & \text{if } m \text{ is down since } t^{\text{down}}_m, \end{cases}$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} \phi_m &= \hat t^{\,\text{up}}_m + \mathrm{rem}_m, \\ \hat t^{\,\text{up}}_m &= t \text{ if } m \text{ works, else} \\ \hat t^{\,\text{up}}_m &= \max(\operatorname{round}(t^{\text{down}}_m + \text{MTTR}), t + 1) \end{aligned}

the expected free time of chapter [[b02#routing]], with $\mathrm{rem}_m$ what remains of the operation in progress, its changeover included (0 if idle); $t + 1$ covers a repair that has outlasted MTTR.

**Read one by hand.** The minute-117 snapshot has fewer orders on purpose than snapshot 101 of chapters [[c01]] and [[c02]] (minute 891, 8 orders), though the same 19 operations to place: its 6 orders can be read line by line. The travel times used are $\tau(1, 2) = \tau(4, 1) = \tau(4, 3) = \tau(0, 1) = 2$ and $\tau(5, 2) = 3$.

- Order 0, on a cart to m0 for its last operation: frozen, $R_{0,0} = 117$, the cart's arrival.
- Order 4, at m0 (station 1), waiting for a cart to m1: frozen destination, $R_{4,1} = 117 + 2 + \tau(1, 2) = 121$.
- Order 2, queued at m4 (station 5): $R_{2,4} = 117$ if it stays, $R_{2,1} = 117 + 2 + \tau(5, 2) = 122$.
- Order 3, queued at m3 (station 4), which is down: $R_{3,3} = 117$, since it stands there; $117 + 2 + 2 = 121$ on m0 or m2.
- Order 1, processed on m4 until 120; its next operation runs only on m4: $R_{1,4} = 120$.
- Order 5, new; its first operation runs only on m0: $R_{5,0} = 117 + 2 + \tau(0, 1) = 121$.

The machines: $\phi = (117, 117, 136, 129, 120)$, since m2 broke at 116 and is expected at $116 + 20$, m3 at $109 + 20$. CP-SAT returns the plan $\Pi_{117}$ with objective 1,194 (not proven optimal), against 1,227 for the greedy warm start; it keeps order 3 on m3 (a 4-minute changeover, start at 133).

## Re-planning at events, executing the beginning {#loop}

::: define What is event-driven re-planning on a rolling horizon?
**In plain words:** every time something happens (an arrival, a breakdown, a repair), take a snapshot, make a new plan for all the orders in the shop, and follow it until the next event replaces it. Only the beginning of each plan is ever carried out.

**More precisely:** at each event time $t_1 \le t_2 \le \dots$ the controller takes the snapshot $\Sigma_{t_i}$ of the orders $\mathcal J_i$ (at most the 25 most urgent, Part 5) and computes a plan

$$\Pi_{t_i} \in \mathcal P(\Sigma_{t_i}), \qquad J(\Pi_{t_i}) \le J(\Pi^{\text{warm}}_{t_i}), \qquad J(\Pi) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal J_i} \big(10\, T_j(\Pi) + C_j(\Pi)\big),$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} &\Pi_{t_i} \in \mathcal P(\Sigma_{t_i}),\ J(\Pi_{t_i}) \le J(\Pi^{\text{warm}}_{t_i}), \\ &J(\Pi) = \textstyle\sum_{j \in \mathcal J_i} (10 T_j + C_j) \end{aligned}

with $\mathcal P(\Sigma)$ the plans consistent with the snapshot and $\Pi^{\text{warm}}_{t_i}$ the greedy warm start of Part 4. $\Pi_{t_i}$ is the best plan CP-SAT finds within its budget, a minimiser of $J$ over $\mathcal P(\Sigma_{t_i})$ when proven optimal (chapter [[c02#gap]] counts how often on the benchmark; on the hero's morning, 34 of the 37 non-empty plans). The shop follows $\Pi_{t_i}$ on $[t_i, t_{i+1})$.

- Receding horizon :: plan far, act near, plan again; the plan's window moves with time.
- Event-driven :: re-planning is triggered by events, not by a clock.
- Plan drift :: what happens differently from the plan before the next event.
:::

**The idea from control.** This is the receding-horizon principle of model predictive control, taught in [the MPC course](../mpc-lmpc-lab/a06-receding-horizon-from-a-plan-to-feedback.html): an open-loop optimiser becomes a feedback law because it is re-run on the measured state [[ref:2]]. Here the state is discrete; the trigger is an event, since between events nothing changes that the plan did not predict except the carts' timing (Part 6); and each plan runs every known order to completion.

**How many re-plans a day?** One per arrival, breakdown and repair: without breakdowns, exactly the 60 arrivals, on every test day. With breakdowns, each machine fails about once per MTBF + MTTR = 140 minutes in the long run (chapter [[b01#breakdowns]] counts the cycles), and each failure brings two events. Over a day of length $C_{\max} = 1415$, the mean makespan of the CP-SAT runs with breakdowns,

$$\mathbb E[\text{re-plans}] \approx n + 2 \cdot 5 \cdot \frac{C_{\max}}{\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR}} = 60 + 10 \cdot \frac{1415}{140} = 161.1 ,$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[\text{re-plans}] &\approx n + 2 \cdot 5 \cdot \tfrac{C_{\max}}{\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR}} \\ &= 60 + 10 \cdot \tfrac{1415}{140} = 161.1 \end{aligned}

against 158.9 recorded on average (from 137 to 177). The hero's morning has 43 events in 314 minutes, one every 7.3 minutes: 12 arrivals, 16 breakdowns, 15 repairs, more than the formula predicts (Exercise 4); the 6 before the first order (minute 51) give empty plans.

<details class="sol"><summary>Going further: is the shortfall of 2.2 re-plans real?</summary>

The days differ by 11.4 re-plans (standard deviation). Compared day by day with its own prediction $60 + 10 \cdot C_{\max,i}/140$, the count falls short by 2.2 on average, with a standard error of 2.5: within one standard error, so noise explains the gap.

</details>

**Executing the beginning: strict plan following.** Between events, the plan answers the simulator's two questions (chapter [[b01]]): an order goes to its planned machine, and a free machine starts the next order of its planned sequence; if that order is not there yet, the machine **waits for it**, even with others in its queue, so as not to break the plan's grouping of families. The repository chose this on six validation seeds with CP-SAT, before the test: mean tardiness 8.2 against 9.0 minutes.

**Orders the plan does not know**, left out by the cap (Part 5), are routed by earliest expected finish (chapter [[b02#routing]]); a waiting machine starts one only if it *fits*, ending by the planned start of the next planned operation. The test ignores the changeover back, up to 10 minutes of possible delay. On the benchmark the cap never binds.

## Greedy list scheduling: warm start and ablation {#greedy}

::: define What is greedy list scheduling?
**In plain words:** look at the next unplaced operation of every order, work out for each eligible machine when it would start and finish there, place the best by a simple preference, and repeat. No choice is revisited.

**More precisely:** with $\phi_m$ and $g_m$ from the snapshot, a candidate (operation $(j, k)$, machine $m$) gets

$$S = \max(\phi_m,\ \alpha_{jk}) + s_m(g_m, f(j)), \qquad E = S + p_{jkm},$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} S &= \max(\phi_m, \alpha_{jk}) + s_m(g_m, f(j)), \\ E &= S + p_{jkm} \end{aligned}

with the arrival $\alpha_{jk} = R_{jm}$ for the first operation and $\alpha_{jk} = E_{j,k-1} + \ell(\mu(j, k-1), m)$ afterwards, $\mu(j, k-1)$ the machine already chosen for the previous operation. A preference key picks one candidate; it is placed, $\phi_m$ becomes $E$, $g_m$ becomes $f(j)$, and the order's next operation becomes a candidate.

- "finish" :: earliest end first.
- "start" :: earliest start first, then earliest due date.
- "edd" :: most urgent order first.
- "previous" :: the previous plan's machines and order, re-timed; new operations afterwards by earliest end.
:::

**By hand, on the minute-117 snapshot.** The first step has nine candidates, with $\phi$, $R$ and $g = (1, 0, \text{none}, 0, 3)$ from Part 2.

::: table num
| Order (operation) | Machine | $\phi_m$ | $\alpha$ | Changeover | $S$ | $p$ | $E$ | Due |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (3) | m0 | 117 | 117 | 6 | 123 | 8 | 131 | 207 |
| 1 (1) | m4 | 120 | 120 | 0 | 120 | 17 | 137 | 358 |
| 2 (0) | m1 | 117 | 122 | 5 | 127 | 26 | 153 | 294 |
| 2 (0) | m4 | 120 | 117 | 0 | 120 | 23 | 143 | 294 |
| 3 (0) | m0 | 117 | 121 | 9 | 130 | 16 | 146 | 321 |
| 3 (0) | m2 | 136 | 121 | 0 | 136 | 19 | 155 | 321 |
| 3 (0) | m3 | 129 | 117 | 4 | 133 | 16 | 149 | 321 |
| 4 (1) | m1 | 117 | 121 | 3 | 124 | 18 | 142 | 286 |
| 5 (0) | m0 | 117 | 121 | 0 | 121 | 9 | 130 | 327 |
:::

"finish" takes the earliest end: order 5 on m0, 121–130; m0 is then free at 130, and "finish" next places order 1 on m4 (120–137). "start" takes the earliest start, 120, and breaks the tie on m4 by due date: order 2. "edd" takes the most urgent order, 0, on m0. The four keys end with objectives 1,262 (finish), 1,227 (start), 1,362 (edd) and 1,239 (previous); CP-SAT improves the cheapest, 1,227, to 1,194.

**The cost.** $N$ placement steps ($N$ operations to plan, 19 here), each scanning at most $n$ orders on at most three machines: $\mathcal O(3Nn)$, 0.47 ms per re-plan without breakdowns and 0.51 ms with them. **As the warm start**, `warm_start` hands the best of the greedy plans to CP-SAT as a complete hint (chapter [[c02#warm]], [[ref:3]]) and keeps it as the fallback; on the hero's morning CP-SAT beat it in 14 of the 37 plans, by 211 objective units in total and 33 at most. **As the ablation**, quick re-planning (Greedy replan) is the same controller with the solver removed (`use_solver=False`), which separates what re-planning does from what the solver adds.

**Why carry the previous plan forward?** Because re-timing it cannot hurt when nothing went worse than predicted.

**Proposition.** Let $\Pi$ be a plan made for snapshot $\Sigma$, and $\Sigma'$ a later snapshot that contains no order $\Pi$ did not plan, in which every ready time and every machine's free time is no later than $\Pi$ assumed and every machine is set up as $\Pi$ expected. Re-time $\Pi$ on $\Sigma'$, keeping each operation's machine and each machine's order. Then no operation starts later than in $\Pi$, and $J$ over the orders of $\Sigma'$ does not increase.

*Proof.* With machines and machine orders fixed, the constraints of $\Sigma'$ have the two forms of the left-shift theorem ([[a01#left-shift]]): bounds $b'_o$ built from $\phi'_m$, $R'_{jm}$ and changeovers, and predecessor arcs whose constants are lags and changeovers. By assumption $b'_o \le b_o$; the constants are those of $\Sigma$, since a lag depends only on the two machines and a changeover only on the family before, which is unchanged. So $\Pi$'s timetable meets every constraint of $\Sigma'$. The "previous" key places the operations in increasing order of their start in $\Pi$, which respects each machine's order and each order's sequence, each at $\max(\phi_m, \alpha_{jk}) + s_m(g_m, f(j))$, its tightest bound: it computes the earliest-start timetable (LS) of $\Pi$'s machines and orders. By the theorem no operation starts or ends later than in $\Pi$, so every $C'_j \le C_j$, and $J$ is a regular measure ([[a01#regular]]). $\square$

Since `solve_snapshot` returns a plan no worse than the warm start, itself no worse than the re-timed plan, **in the nominal case the new plan costs no more than the old one**. With an arrival, the solver minimises the total, newcomer included, and may make old orders later, as D in Part 1.

Checked by the numbers script: re-timed on the next snapshot with no new order, the plan never cost more at the 7 repairs no later than MTTR; it did at 2 of the 6 later repairs (by 6 and 13 units) and at 10 of the 12 breakdowns, outside the nominal case.

## The 25-job cap {#cap}

The model of chapter [[c01]] grows faster than the shop. A fresh order brings an optional interval per eligible machine of each operation: 9, 7, 10 and 8 for families 0 to 3. Worse, a machine with $c$ candidate operations has $(c + 1)^2$ arcs in its circuit (chapter [[c01#circuit]] counts them), one Boolean literal each. One fresh order per family gives the machines $q = (6, 7, 8, 6, 7)$ candidates; $n$ fresh orders in an even mix ($n$ a multiple of 4) give $c_m = n\, q_m / 4$ and exactly $\sum_m (c_m + 1)^2 = 14.625\, n^2 + 17 n + 5$ arcs: 1,077 for 8 orders, 8,837 for 24, 24,085 for 40. With a fixed deterministic budget (chapter [[c02#warm]]), a larger model gets less search per variable.

The controller therefore plans at most the 25 most urgent orders. **On the benchmark the cap never binds**: no CP-SAT re-plan is capped, and the greedy runs never held more than 16 orders at an event without breakdowns, 21 with them. A tighter cap, with the greedy planner on the test days (an illustration):

::: table num
| Greedy re-planning, cap | 25 (or none) | 10 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| No breakdowns, mean tardiness (min) | 3.07 | 2.86 | 8.07 |
| Frequent breakdowns, mean tardiness (min) | 24.03 | 22.89 | 41.95 |
| Changeover time per day, no breakdowns / frequent (min) | 633 / 618 | 643 / 635 | 772 / 770 |
:::

A cap of 10 against 25: −0.21 [−1.12, 0.74] and −1.14 [−4.35, 2.15] minutes (paired, 95 % bootstrap intervals), both containing zero. A cap of 5 costs +5.00 [+2.69, +8.09] and +17.92 [+13.26, +23.42] minutes, probably because a plan of five orders is too short to group families.

## What a plan cannot see {#blind}

**Myopia.** $J$ sums over the orders in the shop; an order arriving in ten minutes counts for nothing, so a plan may leave a machine set up for the wrong family, as with E in Part 1. Exercise 5 measures the opposite: what is lost by re-planning *less* often.

**Certainty equivalence.** A broken machine is expected back after exactly MTTR = 20 minutes; a real repair lasts from 10 to 30. Say a repair takes 10 or 30 minutes with probability 1/2 each, and an order is planned to finish exactly at its due date behind it: its tardiness is 0 or 10, expected 5, against a planned 0. The early case cannot make up for the late one, because tardiness stops at 0. Replacing a random quantity by its mean inside an optimisation is *certainty equivalence*; for tardiness it is optimistic.

The general statement is **Jensen's inequality**: for a convex function $\varphi$ and a random variable $X$, $\mathbb E[\varphi(X)] \ge \varphi(\mathbb E[X])$. For two values it is the definition of convexity, the chord above the curve; the general case we cite [[ref:4]]. Why is tardiness convex in a repair duration $D$? In the planner's model (fixed machine sequences, fixed lags), every start and end is computed from free times, ready times and durations by $+$ and $\max$ only. Unfolded, a completion time is the largest, over chains of operations, of a starting time plus the durations along the chain. $D$ enters only through the broken machine's free time $\phi_m = t^{\text{down}}_m + D + \mathrm{rem}_m$, and a chain starts there or not, so it contains $D$ once with coefficient 1 or not at all. Hence $C_j(D) = \max(c_1, c_2 + D)$ for two constants: convex and nondecreasing. Then $T_j = \max(0, C_j - d_j)$ is convex too, and

$$\mathbb E\big[T_j(D)\big] \ \ge\ T_j\big(\mathbb E[D]\big) .$$
%narrow \mathbb E[T_j(D)] \ge T_j(\mathbb E[D])

A plan's tardiness is a lower bound on what that plan should expect (for the plan, not exactly for the shop, where the order of events can itself change with $D$). With $D$ uniform on $[10, 30]$, the order planned to finish exactly at its due date expects

$$\mathbb E[\max(0, D - 20)] = \int_{20}^{30} \frac{x - 20}{20}\, dx = \frac{10^2}{40} = 2.5 \text{ minutes},$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[\max(0, D - 20)] &= \textstyle\int_{20}^{30} \tfrac{x - 20}{20}\, dx \\ &= \tfrac{10^2}{40} = 2.5 \text{ min} \end{aligned}

and $(10 - \eta)^2 / 40$ with a margin of $\eta$ minutes (Exercise 3).

**The carts are a fixed lag.** The model has no carts (AGVs): a move is a lag $\tau(a, b) + \delta$, as if a cart always came 2 minutes after being called. In the simulator they are real resources. Over the 20 test days with breakdowns (greedy re-planning, 5,000 loaded moves) the wait for a cart has median 2 minutes, where $\delta$ comes from, but mean 2.91 and 90th percentile 7: 24 % of moves do not wait and 48 % wait more than 2 minutes.

<details class="sol"><summary>Going further: how far does the shop drift from the plan?</summary>

Compare each operation's actual start with its start in the last plan made before it. On the hero's morning, of the 36 operations started before minute 314, 28 started exactly on time, 7 early and 1 late, never by more than 2 minutes (mean −0.31). Drift is reset at the next event: 7.3 minutes later on average on this morning, 8.9 over a day with breakdowns (1,415 minutes for 158.9 events) and 22.4 without. So the fixed-lag approximation costs little here.

</details>

## Nervousness {#nervous}

Step the hero from event 21 to event 22 (minute 117, order 5 arrives) with the outline on: of the 15 operations in both plans, 4 change machine, and planned starts move by 4.2 minutes on average. In a real workshop each change costs something the simulator does not charge: operators prepare the next order. Planners call a schedule that changes too often *nervous*, its opposite *stable* [[ref:1]].

::: define How is nervousness measured?
**In plain words:** compare each new plan with the one it replaces, on the operations in both: how many moved to another machine, how far their planned start moved, and on how many machines the next job changed.

**More precisely:** for consecutive plans $\Pi_{i-1}$, $\Pi_i$ (short for $\Pi_{t_{i-1}}$, $\Pi_{t_i}$) with common operations $\mathcal O_i$, machines $m_i(o)$ and starts $S_i(o)$,

$$\begin{aligned} \nu^{\text{mach}}_i &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal O_i|} \sum_{o \in \mathcal O_i} \mathbb 1\big[m_i(o) \ne m_{i-1}(o)\big], \\ \nu^{\text{start}}_i &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal O_i|} \sum_{o \in \mathcal O_i} \big|S_i(o) - S_{i-1}(o)\big|, \end{aligned}$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} \nu^{\text{mach}}_i &= \tfrac{1}{|\mathcal O_i|} \textstyle\sum_{o} \mathbb 1[m_i(o) \ne m_{i-1}(o)], \\ \nu^{\text{start}}_i &= \tfrac{1}{|\mathcal O_i|} \textstyle\sum_o |S_i(o) - S_{i-1}(o)| \end{aligned}

pooled over a day with weights $|\mathcal O_i|$, together with the share of machines whose first planned operation after $t_i$ changed.
:::

## Results: where the gain comes from {#results}

On the 20 test days, mean tardiness in minutes from `results/runs.csv`: the best rule 7.0 (most urgent first, EDD) without breakdowns and 34.1 (shortest setup first, SST) with them, quick re-planning 3.1 and 24.0, optimiser re-planning 1.3 and 19.3. Methods are compared day by day on paired differences $\Delta_i$, with 95 % bootstrap intervals (chapter [[d02#bootstrap]]). On each day the total gain splits exactly in two,

$$\underbrace{\bar T^{\text{CP-SAT}}_i - \bar T^{\text{rule}}_i}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{\big(\bar T^{\text{greedy}}_i - \bar T^{\text{rule}}_i\big)}_{\text{re-planning}} + \underbrace{\big(\bar T^{\text{CP-SAT}}_i - \bar T^{\text{greedy}}_i\big)}_{\text{the solver}} ,$$

## The code behind it {#code}

The snapshot is a direct transcription of the table of Part 2.

::: code jobshop/cpsat.py:take_snapshot
`machine_free` is $\phi_m$, `machine_family` is $g_m$, `ready` holds the $R_{jm}$. `IN_TRANSIT` keeps the destination at the cart's arrival `jr.eta`; the last branch, `QUEUED` or `NEW`, is where rerouting lives: every eligible machine, at `shop.now` if the order stands there.
:::

The controller re-plans in `on_event`, called at every arrival, breakdown and repair.

::: code jobshop/cpsat.py:CPSATController.on_event
Orders are sorted by due date and cut to `max_jobs`; `solve_snapshot` receives the previous plan for the "previous" greedy key; `sequence_of` stores each machine's planned operations by start. The last loop is the only physical action of a re-plan: `shop.reroute` sends a queued order where the new plan wants it.
:::

Executing the beginning of the plan is `sequence`, called whenever a free machine has a queue.

::: code jobshop/cpsat.py:CPSATController.sequence
With `strict`, only the head of the machine's planned order counts: start it if it is in the queue, else the most urgent unplanned order that `fits` before the head's planned start, else `None`: wait. `fits` omits the changeover back, the gap of Part 3.
:::

The loop of `greedy_plan`, warm start and ablation:

::: code jobshop/cpsat.py:85-122
`free`, `family` and `next_op` are $\phi_m$, $g_m$ and each order's next operation. A first operation may only go to a machine of its `ready` map, which is how frozen destinations are respected. The "previous" key `(0, old start, ...)` puts first every operation that keeps its old machine, in the order of its old start: the order of the proof.
:::

## Common pitfalls {#pitfalls}

1. **"The executed schedule is the last plan."** No plan is executed in full; the executed schedule is stitched from the beginnings of 60 to 177 plans a day.
2. **"Re-planning more often is always better."** In the nominal case a new plan cannot be worse *on the snapshot*, but the snapshot is optimistic. At minute 109 (event 20) the plan sends order 3 to m3, expected back at 129. The repair took 19 minutes; a 30-minute one would have kept m3 down until 139 with order 3 waiting in front of it.
3. **"The plan's tardiness is what to expect."** It is a lower bound for that plan, by Jensen's inequality.
4. **"Frozen means fixed in time."** A frozen destination fixes the machine, not the start.
5. **"The solver is where the gain comes from."** About two thirds of it come from re-planning with a greedy plan.

## Check your understanding {#check}

:::: qa
Try to answer each question before opening it.

::: q 1. What turns a planner into a controller?
Re-running it on the measured state at every event, and applying only the beginning of each plan.
:::
::: q 2. Why is an order on a cart treated differently from an order in a queue?
The cart is already driving, so the snapshot keeps its destination, with the cart's arrival as $R_{jm}$. A queued order can still go to any eligible machine.
:::
::: q 3. Why does an operation in progress not appear in the plan?
Nothing about it is left to decide; it enters only through $\phi_m$ and the ready times of its order's next operation.
:::
::: q 4. Why re-plan at events and not every minute?
Between events nothing happens that the plan did not predict, except the carts' timing.
:::
::: q 5. Why does strict plan following let a machine stand idle with orders in its queue?
The plan wants another order next, typically of the same family or more urgent; starting a queued order would spend a changeover and break the grouping.
:::
::: q 6. What does the "previous" greedy rule guarantee, and when?
Re-timing it does not increase the objective if no order is new, no ready or free time is later than assumed, and every machine is set up as expected.
:::
::: q 7. Why is the planned tardiness optimistic?
The planner replaces random repair times by their mean; tardiness is convex in a repair duration, so by Jensen's inequality its expectation is at least its value at the mean.
:::
::: q 8. How much of CP-SAT's gain over the best rule comes from the solver?
About a third: −1.7 of −5.7 minutes without breakdowns, −4.7 of −14.8 with them, at about 3,000 times the computing time.
:::

The proposition of Part 4 assumes that every machine is set up as the plan expected and free no later than planned. (a) On one machine with a 5-minute changeover between families 0 and 1, find two orders and a plan whose re-timed version costs more although the machine is free *earlier* than planned. (b) Find a two-order example where the machine is free later and the cost increases. (c) Which events of the workshop produce each situation?

:::: exercise The price of betting on the mean
A repair lasts $D$ uniform on $[10, 30]$ minutes and the planner assumes 20. An order is planned to finish $\eta$ minutes before its due date, $0 \le \eta \le 10$, and each minute of repair delays it by a minute. Show that its expected tardiness is $(10 - \eta)^2 / 40$.
::: hint
It is late by $\max(0, D - 20 - \eta)$; integrate against the density $1/20$.
:::
::: solution
$\mathbb E[\max(0, D - 20 - \eta)] = \int_{20 + \eta}^{30} \frac{x - 20 - \eta}{20}\, dx = \frac{(10 - \eta)^2}{40}$: 2.5 minutes at $\eta = 0$, 0.625 at $\eta = 5$, against a planned 0. That is Jensen's inequality for the convex function $\max(0, \cdot)$.

Predict the number of events of the hero's morning (12 orders, minutes 0 to 314, 5 machines with frequent breakdowns) as Part 3 does for a full day, and compare with the 43 observed. Is 43 surprising? Answer with a simulation of the breakdowns of many mornings.
::: hint
One failure per 140 minutes and machine in the long run (chapter [[b01#breakdowns]]). `make_scenario(ws, seed, n_jobs=1, mtbf=120)` gives a day's breakdowns in `downtime[m]`, a list of (failure, repair) minutes; count those before 314.

Each machine fails about $314/140 = 2.24$ times, so the prediction is $12 + 10 \cdot 314/140 = 34.4$ events, 22.4 of them downtime events. Test day 0 has 31 (16 breakdowns, 15 repairs). Write a controller that re-plans with the greedy planner at arrivals only, ignoring breakdowns and repairs, and run it on the 20 test days with frequent breakdowns. Compare with greedy re-planning at every event, 24.03 minutes.
::: hint
Subclass `CPSATController`, call the parent's `on_event` only when `kind == "arrival"`, and use `use_solver=False` so the 20 runs take seconds.

```python
import numpy as np
from jobshop.cpsat import CPSATController
from jobshop.metrics import run_metrics
from jobshop.scenario import LEVELS, make_scenario, make_workshop
from jobshop.shop import Shop

class ArrivalsOnly(CPSATController):
    def on_event(self, shop, kind, **info):
        if kind == "arrival":
            super().on_event(shop, kind, **info)

ws = make_workshop(); tard = []

    sc = make_scenario(ws, seed, mtbf=LEVELS["frequent breakdowns"])
    log = Shop(ws, sc, ArrivalsOnly(use_solver=False)).run()

print(np.mean(tard))     # 28.80
```
The mean tardiness rises from 24.03 to 28.80 minutes: between arrivals, orders wait for a broken machine the plan still counts on, and a repaired machine waits for the next arrival to be used.

## The chapter on one page {#summary}

::: steps
- **A controller re-plans**: at every event, snapshot $\Sigma_t$, plan $\Pi_t$, execute only its beginning.
- **The snapshot** freezes running operations and cart destinations; ready times $R_{jm}$ say how soon each order can be at each machine.
- **Strict plan following** makes a machine wait for its next planned order.
- **Greedy list scheduling**: the warm start and, alone, the ablation. Re-timing the previous plan cannot increase a regular objective ([[a01#regular]]) in the nominal case.
- **The 25-job cap** bounds a model whose circuits grow quadratically; it never binds on the benchmark.
- **What a plan cannot see**: future orders (myopia), the spread of repair times (optimistic by Jensen), the carts.
- **Nervousness**: consecutive CP-SAT plans move 14.2 % of their common operations; nothing limits it.
- **Results**: re-planning earns 69 % and 68 % of CP-SAT's gain over the best rule.
:::

### The vocabulary, in one table

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Snapshot | The running shop in the planner's terms. |
| Frozen | Not changeable by a new plan. |
| Event-driven, receding horizon | A new plan at every event; act on its beginning, plan again. |
| Strict plan following | Machines run their planned order and wait for the next planned job. |
| List scheduling | Placing operations one by one at their earliest feasible start. |
| Ablation | The same system with one component removed. |
| Myopia, certainty equivalence | Planning without future orders; planning with mean durations. |
| Plan drift, nervousness | Reality against the plan; a plan against the previous one. |

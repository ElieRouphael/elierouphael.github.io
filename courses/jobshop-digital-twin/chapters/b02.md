## The question a free machine asks {#idea}

**In plain words.** Machine m2 finishes a job; three jobs of different families, lengths and due dates wait in its buffer, and the controller's `sequence` method ([[b01]]) says which starts. A **dispatching rule** answers locally: it scores each job in this queue and starts the best, with no plan, in microseconds (*completely reactive* scheduling, in the survey of Ouelhadj and Petrovic [[ref:7]]). Rules are simple, robust and free; they can be beaten because a local look cannot see what is coming (Part 6).

**How often does the choice matter?** A simulated day has 60 jobs of 4 operations, so 240 decisions: each operation starts after one call to `sequence`. Under most urgent first only 24.1 % of the decisions of a calm day have a real choice, two or more jobs waiting (mean queue 1.30 jobs); with frequent breakdowns, 42.2 % (mean queue 1.60). Rules differ through a minority of decisions, more of them when machines fail.

::: define What is a dispatching rule?
**In plain words:** a recipe a machine applies each time it becomes free: score every waiting job from what is known now, start the best.

**More precisely:** at a decision epoch $t$ (a minute at which machine $m$ is idle and up and its queue $\mathcal Q_m(t)$ is not empty), the rule gives each $j \in \mathcal Q_m(t)$ a priority index $\pi_j(t)$ and starts

$$j^\star = \arg\min_{j \in \mathcal Q_m(t)} \pi_j(t),$$

ties broken by a second index and finally by the job number. The index may use the job's data, the time and the state of machine $m$, not the other machines' queues: the rule is *local*.

- Priority index :: The score $\pi_j(t)$; smallest first here, except for ATCS (largest first).
- Non-delay :: A free machine with a waiting job starts one; every rule of the course is non-delay.
- Policy :: A map from the shop's state to a decision, the notion chapter [[d01]] uses for the learning agent.
:::

## Four rules as priority indices {#indices}

The four rules of `jobshop/rules.py`. Job $j$ waits for operation $k$ on machine $m$; $q_j$ is when it entered *this* queue; $l$ is the family $m$ is set up for (that of the last job it ran); $s_m(l, f(j))$ is the changeover of [[a03]].

::: table num
| Rule | Index $\pi_j(t)$, smallest first | Ties | Depends on $t$ or $l$? |
|---|---|---|---|
| First come, first served (FIFO) | $q_j$ | job number | no: static |
| Shortest job first (SPT) | $p_{jkm}$ | job number | no: static |
| Most urgent first (EDD) | $d_j$ | job number | no: static |
| Shortest setup first (SST) | $s_m(l, f(j))$ | due date, then job number | yes: dynamic |
:::

FIFO uses the entry into this queue, not the release time $r_j$; EDD uses the job's due date for every operation; SST breaks ties by due date, so among jobs needing no changeover it is EDD.

**The paint machine, by hand.** One machine set up for blue, colour changes of 10 minutes, four orders: A blue, 20 min, due 30; B red, 10 min, due 25; C blue, 15 min, due 70; D red, 25 min, due 60. The indices at $t = 0$ (FIFO: the arrival rank, all four having waited since before minute 0; SST cells read (changeover, due date), compared left to right):

::: table num
| Rule | A | B | C | D | Order | Total tardiness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FIFO | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | A B C D | 55 |
| SPT | 20 | 10 | 15 | 25 | B C A D | 75 |
| EDD | 30 | 25 | 70 | 60 | B A D C | 85 |
| SST (set up for blue) | 0 · 30 | 10 · 25 | 0 · 70 | 10 · 60 | A C B D | 50 |
:::

FIFO, SPT and EDD just sort the row. SST recomputes: set up for blue, A and C need no change and A is due first; then C; then B and D both need 10 minutes and B is due first. None finds the best of the 24 orders, A B D C with 40 minutes ([[a02]]). The hero shows every index.

::: define What separates a static index from a dynamic one?
**In plain words:** a static index is a label on the job, so two waiting jobs never swap ranks while they wait; a dynamic index also depends on the clock or the machine, and ranks can change between decisions.

**More precisely:** $\pi_j$ is static if $\pi_j(t)$ depends on neither $t$ nor the machine's state while $j$ waits. Then, on one machine without arrivals, dispatching produces the jobs sorted by index: at every decision the smallest remaining index is the next element of the sorted list. A dynamic index must be recomputed at every decision, and its sequence is not a sort.
:::

**The theorems of A2, here.** On one machine, all jobs present at the start and no changeovers, most urgent first minimises the maximum lateness (Jackson [[ref:4]], proved in [[a02#jackson]]). The theorem does not survive the shop, but it leaves a trace: most urgent first has the smallest maximum tardiness of the four rules at both levels (Part 4).

## Earliest expected finish, and rerouting {#routing}

**In plain words.** When a job arrives or finishes an operation, the controller picks which eligible machine does its next one: **routing**. The repository does what a shopper does at the checkouts: estimate when the job would be done at each machine, go where that is earliest. The four rules and the agent of [[d01]] share this routing, so Part 4 compares sequencing only. When a machine fails, the jobs in its queue are routed again: **rerouting**.

**A real reroute.** Test day 0, frequent breakdowns, shortest setup first: machine m4 fails at minute 124 with job 0 (family 0, operation 2, due at 207) in its queue. The operation can run on m4 (7 min) or m1 (10 min).

- Stay: the job is there, so it could start at 124; m4 is expected back at $124 + 20 = 144$ (the mean repair time), then 13 minutes of the interrupted operation and 17 committed to job 2, on its way: m4 is free for job 0 at 174 and job 0 is done at $\max(124, 174) + 7 = 181$.
- Move: the cart needs 3 minutes, so the job could start at 127; m1 is up and busy for 22 more minutes, free at 146: done at $\max(127, 146) + 10 = 156$.

The job moves to the slower machine and expects to finish 25 minutes earlier. The repair really ends at minute 137, but even then staying would give $\max(124, 137 + 13 + 17) + 7 = 174$. Job 2, not yet at m4, is counted ahead of job 0, already waiting. The sliders show when the decision flips.

::: define What is earliest-expected-finish routing?
**In plain words:** send each job to the machine where, by a rough estimate made now, it would be finished first.

**More precisely:** minimise the expected finish $E_m$ below over the eligible machines, ties by the shorter processing time, then the lower machine number. It assumes all committed work runs before $j$, counts no changeovers for $j$, ignores the wait for a cart, and replaces the repair by MTTR $= 20$ min (the real one lasts uniform $[10, 30]$ min).
:::

**The formula, one symbol at a time.** Job $j$ needs operation $k$ at time $t$ and stands at station $b_j$ (the station of the machine it is at, or the load station 0). For each eligible machine $m \in \mathcal M_{jk}$, `route_earliest_finish` computes:

- $a_m = t + \tau(b_j, m+1)$: when the job could be at $m$, after the cart's travel (zero if it is already there).
- $U_m$: when $m$ is expected up: $t$ if it is up; if it is down, $U_m = \max\big(\operatorname{round}(t^{\text{down}}_m + \text{MTTR}),\ t+1\big)$, with $t^{\text{down}}_m$ the minute $m$ failed. The real repair time is hidden ([[b01#breakdowns]]), so the controller assumes the mean; if the repair is already overdue by that estimate, it assumes the machine is back no earlier than the next minute.
- $R_m$: what remains of the operation in progress on $m$, its changeover included; 0 if $m$ is idle.
- $W_m = \sum_{i \in \mathcal Q_m \cup \mathcal I_m,\ i \ne j} p_{i k_i m}$: the processing committed to $m$ by the jobs in its queue $\mathcal Q_m$ or on their way to it $\mathcal I_m$, each at its own next operation $k_i$, $j$ excluded.
- $F_m = U_m + R_m + W_m$: when $m$ is expected free for $j$.
- $E_m = \max(a_m, F_m) + p_{jkm}$: when $j$ is expected done on $m$.

The job goes to $\arg\min_m (E_m, p_{jkm}, m)$, compared lexicographically. In the example, for m4: $a = 124$, $U = 144$, $R = 13$, $W = 17$, $F = 174$, $E = 181$; for m1: $a = 127$, $U = 124$, $R = 22$, $W = 0$, $F = 146$, $E = 156$.

**Rerouting, and what it is worth.** When machine $m$ fails, each job waiting in its queue is routed again and moved by cart if another machine now gives an earlier finish; jobs on a cart or waiting for one keep their destination, and nothing happens at a repair. With frequent breakdowns shortest setup first moves 16.9 jobs a day out of 34.5 it re-examines. Switching rerouting off raises its mean tardiness from 34.1 to 37.5 min, and that of most urgent first from 37.6 to 39.1 (exercise 5; short simulations that first re-create `results/runs.csv` exactly).

## Why shortest setup first gains under breakdowns {#breakdowns}

**In plain words.** A changeover is machine time that produces nothing. Avoiding one makes no particular job earlier but gives capacity back to every job behind, and what that is worth depends on the load. In a calm shop capacity is cheap, queues are short and urgency matters more: most urgent first wins. When machines fail 14 % of the time, each saved minute shortens many waits, and shortest setup first catches up, as avoiding colour changes (50 min) beat most urgent first (85 min) on the paint machine of [[a02]].

**Measured.** Shortest setup first spends 107 fewer changeover minutes a day than most urgent first without breakdowns, 195 fewer with them, because it has more to choose from: its decisions with two or more jobs waiting rise from 24.0 % to 40.4 %.

**A rough load calculation.** One job arrives every 20.83 minutes on average and there are 5 machines, so the shop offers $5 \times 20.83 = 104.15$ machine-minutes per arriving job. The course's load $\rho = 0.60$ ([[b01#load]]) counts only fastest-route work: $62.5 / 104.15 = 0.60$. The real shop does more: 67.1 minutes of processing per job without breakdowns and 67.4 with them (more than the 62.5 of the fastest routes: routing balances machines), plus changeovers: 17.1 minutes per job under most urgent first and 15.3 under shortest setup first in the calm shop, 16.9 and 13.7 with breakdowns. With breakdowns only a share $A = 0.857$ of machine time is available. The **effective load** is work over available capacity:

$$\rho_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\bar p + \bar s}{5 / \lambda} \cdot \frac{1}{A}: \qquad \begin{array}{lcc} & \text{EDD} & \text{SST} \\ \text{no breakdowns} & 0.808 & 0.791 \\ \text{frequent breakdowns} & 0.944 & 0.908 \end{array}$$
%narrow \rho_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\bar p + \bar s}{5/\lambda}\,\frac1A: \ 0.808 \text{ vs } 0.791 \text{ (calm)}, \ 0.944 \text{ vs } 0.908 \text{ (breakdowns)}

with $A = 1$ in the calm shop: 0.60 becomes about 0.8 with real routes and changeovers, and above 0.9 with 14 % of machine time lost. For one server with Poisson arrivals and exponential service (M/M/1), the mean wait is the mean service time times $\rho / (1 - \rho)$ (Law [[ref:6]], cited, not derived). With $\rho_{\text{eff}}$ in its place, that factor is 4.22 against 3.78 in the calm shop, a ratio of 1.12, and 16.87 against 9.85 with breakdowns, a ratio of 1.71: the same few points of load cost little at 0.808 and a great deal at 0.944.

::: callout warm
**A direction, not a model.** The workshop is not an M/M/1 queue and these numbers predict no tardiness. What carries over is the shape: waiting grows like $1/(1-\rho)$, so capacity saved near saturation is worth far more than capacity saved with slack.
:::

**The worst job tells the other half.** Shortest setup first keeps a machine on its family, so a job of another family can wait through several runs: with breakdowns its maximum tardiness exceeds that of most urgent first by $+58\ [+24, +96]$ min day by day, even though its mean is lower.

## What a rule cannot see {#blind}

A rule decides with the queue in front of it, now. Three things are outside its view.

**Other machines' queues.** The index uses only $\mathcal Q_m(t)$ and machine $m$: it cannot prefer the job whose next machine is idle to one whose next machine is the bottleneck.

**Future arrivals.** Every rule here is non-delay. Take the paint machine set up for blue, with only B (red, 10 min, due 25) waiting at minute 0 and A (blue, 20 min, due 30) arriving at minute 1. Any rule starts B: change, B done at 20, change back, A done at 50, total tardiness 20. Waiting one minute: A done at 21, change, B done at 41, total 16. For a regular objective such as total tardiness, in shops without sequence-dependent changeovers, an optimal schedule always exists among the *active* schedules (no operation can start earlier without delaying another), which may hold a machine idle, but not always among the non-delay ones (Pinedo [[ref:1]], chapter 2, cited); our example has a changeover, which that result does not cover, but the lesson holds: a non-delay rule can lose.

**Grouping families across a horizon.** Shortest setup first avoids the next changeover, one greedy step at a time; avoiding them over a sequence is the travelling-salesman structure of [[a02#setups]]. Yet the fewest changeovers is not the goal. On the six orders of m2 in the hero, shortest setup first spends 20 minutes on changeovers and is 109 minutes late in total; most urgent first spends 46 and is 135 late; the best of all 720 orders, J0 J3 J4 J1 J5 J2, spends 29, more than shortest setup first, and is 101 late.

A plan over every machine's next operations sees all three: chapters [[c01]] and [[c03]] build it, and with frequent breakdowns mean tardiness falls from 34.1 min (the best rule) to 19.3, changeovers from 820 to 633 minutes a day. Inside the rule, one thing can still be done: weigh several criteria at once.

## The composite index ATCS, term by term {#atcs}

**In plain words.** A composite rule multiplies three factors: favour jobs short for their importance (they free the machine soon), favour jobs about to be late, penalise jobs that need a changeover. The apparent tardiness cost with setups rule, **ATCS**, of Lee, Bhaskaran and Pinedo [[ref:2]] extends the apparent tardiness cost rule (ATC) of Vepsalainen and Morton [[ref:5]] with the changeover factor; "apparent" because the index is an estimate, made from the slack now, of the tardiness cost a job will incur if it waits. With weights $w_j$ (all 1 in this course), when the machine is free at $t$ and set up for family $l$:

$$\boxed{I_j(t, l) = \frac{w_j}{p_j}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{\max(d_j - p_j - t,\ 0)}{k_1 \bar p}\right) \exp\!\left(-\frac{s_{lj}}{k_2 \bar s}\right)}$$
%narrow \boxed{I_j = \frac{w_j}{p_j}\, e^{-\max(d_j - p_j - t, 0)/(k_1 \bar p)}\, e^{-s_{lj}/(k_2 \bar s)}}

and the **largest** index starts. $s_{lj}$ is the changeover from $l$ to the family of $j$, $\bar p$ the average processing time, $\bar s$ the average changeover, $k_1, k_2 > 0$ two scaling parameters.

**First factor, $w_j / p_j$: Smith's ratio.** If every waiting job is certain to be late, $T_j = C_j - d_j$ and $\sum_j w_j T_j = \sum_j w_j C_j - \sum_j w_j d_j$, the last sum independent of the order: weighted tardiness becomes weighted completion time, which Smith's rule [[ref:3]] minimises on one machine by ordering on decreasing $w_j/p_j$. Swapping adjacent jobs $i$ and $j$ changes only their two terms, by $w_j p_i - w_i p_j$ ("$i$ first" minus "$j$ first"), negative exactly when $w_i/p_i > w_j/p_j$; exercise 1 completes the proof. This is weighted shortest processing time (WSPT), the right rule *for jobs already lost*.

**Second factor: urgency through the slack.** The slack $\sigma_j(t) = d_j - p_j - t$ is how long $j$ can still wait and finish on time. If $\sigma_j \le 0$ the job cannot be early (with $\sigma_j < 0$ it is late whatever happens): the factor is 1 and ATCS is WSPT among such jobs, as the first factor says it should be. If $\sigma_j > 0$ the job can wait, and its priority is discounted by $\exp(-\sigma_j / (k_1 \bar p))$: the slack counted in average jobs, divided by $k_1$. A slack of $k_1$ average jobs keeps $e^{-1} = 0.3679$ of the priority.

**Third factor: the changeover penalty.** $\exp(-s_{lj} / (k_2 \bar s))$ is 1 for a job of the current family and shrinks with the changeover, measured in average changeovers; $k_2$ sets how hard.

**The parameters are scales, and their limits are simple rules** (proved in exercise 2). As $k_1, k_2 \to \infty$ both exponentials tend to 1: WSPT. As $k_1 \to 0$, among jobs of positive slack the smallest slack wins (the **minimum slack** rule, close to most urgent first when processing times are similar), and any job of non-positive slack beats them all. As $k_2 \to 0$, the smallest changeover wins: shortest setup first, with ATC breaking ties. Dividing by $\bar p$ and $\bar s$ makes $k_1$, $k_2$ dimensionless; Lee, Bhaskaran and Pinedo give rules of thumb for them [[ref:2]], which we replace by a scan. On one machine we take $\bar p$ as the mean processing time of the orders and $\bar s$ as the mean changeover over ordered pairs of distinct orders: for the paint machine $\bar p = 17.5$ and $\bar s = 6.6667$, since 8 of the 12 pairs need a 10-minute change.

**By hand on the paint machine, $k_1 = k_2 = 1$.** Slack is counted in units of $k_1 \bar p = 17.5$ min, changeovers in units of $k_2 \bar s = 6.667$ min, and a colour change costs the factor $e^{-10/6.667} = 0.2231$.

- $t = 0$, blue. A: slack 10, $I = \frac{1}{20} \times e^{-10/17.5} = \frac{1}{20} \times 0.5647 = 0.02824$. B: slack 15 and a change, $\frac{1}{10} \times 0.4244 \times 0.2231 = 0.00947$. C: slack 55, $\frac{1}{15} \times 0.0432 = 0.00288$. D: slack 35 and a change, $\frac{1}{25} \times 0.1353 \times 0.2231 = 0.00121$. **A** starts.
- $t = 20$, blue. B's slack is now negative: $\frac{1}{10} \times 0.2231 = 0.02231$, against 0.00902 for C and 0.00379 for D. **B**.
- $t = 40$, red. D's slack is negative and it needs no change: $\frac{1}{25} = 0.04000$, against 0.00631 for C. **D**, then C.

A B D C completes at 20, 40, 65 and 90, tardiness 0, 15, 5 and 20: **40 minutes, the best of all 24 orders**, found without search: at minute 20 the index saw that B was lost and short, and C could wait.

**Over the slider grid.** On the paint machine a band of parameters reaches the optimum, from any $k_2$ at $k_1 = 0.1$ to $k_2 = 2$ alone at $k_1 = 5$; too small a $k_2$ gives A C B D (50), large $k_1$ and $k_2$ give B C A D (75). On the six orders of m2 a band from $(k_1, k_2) = (0.25, 0.1)$ to $(3, 1.5)$ reaches 101, the best of 720 orders, against 109 for the best single rule. The band depends on the instance.

## The code behind it {#code}

ATCS is not in the repository; its README names it as the missing stronger baseline. The four indices and the choice:

::: code jobshop/rules.py:16-39
Each rule returns a tuple and `pick` takes the `min` over the queue; Python compares tuples lexicographically, so the later entries are the tie-breaks. `_fifo` returns `queued_at`; `_edd` uses `j.due` for every operation; `_sst` asks for the changeover from `m.last_family`, which is `None` on a machine that has run nothing (then every changeover is 0 and the due date decides). `Shop._try_start` calls `sequence` whenever a machine is free, up and has a queue, and a `RuleController` always returns a job: non-delay.
:::

The routing formula of Part 3:

::: code jobshop/rules.py:43-58
`workload` is $W_m$: processing times on this machine of the jobs queued at it or travelling to it (`m.queue + m.inbound`), with the condition `if j is not skip` leaving out the job being routed. In `route_earliest_finish`, `arrival` is $a_m$, `free` is $F_m$, and the key `(max(arrival, free) + proc, proc, machine)` is $(E_m, p_{jkm}, m)$, compared lexicographically; `expected_free` (in `shop.py`) is $U_m + R_m$. No changeover appears in the key.
:::

::: code jobshop/rules.py:82-91
`RuleController.route` and `.sequence`, just above in the file, answer the simulator's two questions with `route_earliest_finish` and `pick`. `on_event` is the rerouting: it ignores arrivals and repairs; on a breakdown it re-routes every job in a copy of the failed machine's queue (`reroute` removes jobs from it) and moves a job only when the target changes.
:::

## Common pitfalls {#pitfalls}

1. **"EDD uses the operation's due date."** It uses the job's $d_j$ for every operation.
2. **"The rule decides every time a machine frees."** Only when two or more jobs wait (Part 1).
3. **"Routing accounts for changeovers."** $E_m$ is an estimate from processing minutes, not a schedule.
4. **"A lower mean means better on every day."** Shortest setup first has the lower mean with breakdowns, wins 11 days of 20, and its paired interval contains zero ([[d02#bootstrap]]).
5. **"Fewer changeovers is always better."** Changeovers cost capacity; they are not the objective (Part 6).

## Check your understanding {#check}

:::: qa
Try to answer each question before opening it.

::: q 1. What makes an index static, and why does it matter?
It depends only on the job's data. On one machine without arrivals the dispatch order is then a sort; a dynamic index must be recomputed at every decision.
:::
::: q 2. Why is shortest setup first dynamic?
Its index is the changeover from the machine's current family, which changes with every job started.
:::
::: q 3. Write the routing key, and say what it leaves out.
$E_m = \max(t + \tau(b_j, m+1),\ U_m + R_m + W_m) + p_{jkm}$, ties by $p_{jkm}$ then $m$. It leaves out changeovers, the real service order of the queue, the wait for a cart and the real repair time.
:::
::: q 4. When a machine fails, which jobs can move?
Only those waiting in its queue. Jobs on a cart or waiting for one keep their destination; nothing is rerouted at a repair.
:::
::: q 5. Why does most urgent first have the smallest maximum tardiness?
On one machine, all jobs present at the start and no changeovers, earliest due date minimises the maximum lateness (Jackson [[ref:4]]); in the shop the bias remains.
:::
::: q 6. Give two reasons why shortest setup first gains on most urgent first under breakdowns.
Capacity is scarcer, so a saved changeover minute shortens more waits; and queues are longer (40.4 % of its decisions with a choice, not 24.0 %), so it saves more: 195 minutes a day, not 107.
:::
::: q 7. In ATCS, what happens to a job with negative slack?
Its urgency factor is 1: among such jobs ATCS ranks by $w_j/p_j$ times the changeover factor, Smith's rule for jobs already lost.
:::
::: q 8. What do $k_1 \to 0$ and $k_2 \to 0$ turn ATCS into?
$k_1 \to 0$: minimum slack, jobs of non-positive slack first. $k_2 \to 0$: shortest setup first, with ATC breaking ties.
:::

## Exercises {#exercises}

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. The coding exercises use the repository and its `.venv`; they do not modify it.

::::: exercises
:::: exercise Smith's rule by exchange
Prove that on one machine, all jobs available at 0 and no changeovers, ordering by decreasing $w_j / p_j$ minimises $\sum_j w_j C_j$; then that if every job is late in every order, the same order minimises $\sum_j w_j T_j$.
::: hint
Swap two adjacent jobs and compare the two terms that change. For the second part write $T_j = C_j - d_j$.
:::
::: solution
For adjacent $i$, $j$ starting at $u$, the other completion times do not change under a swap. With $i$ first the two terms are $w_i (u + p_i) + w_j (u + p_i + p_j)$; with $j$ first, $w_j (u + p_j) + w_i (u + p_j + p_i)$; the difference is $w_j p_i - w_i p_j$, negative iff $w_i/p_i > w_j/p_j$. Any order can be turned into the sorted one by such swaps of adjacent out-of-order pairs (bubble sort), none of which increases the cost, so the sorted order is optimal. If every job is late in every order, $\sum w_j T_j = \sum w_j C_j - \sum w_j d_j$ with a constant last sum: the same order is optimal.
:::
::::
:::: exercise The limits of ATCS
Prove: (a) as $k_1 \to 0$, among jobs of positive slack the smallest slack wins, and a job of non-positive slack beats any job of positive slack; (b) as $k_2 \to 0$, a strictly smaller changeover wins; (c) as $k_1, k_2 \to \infty$, ATCS becomes WSPT.
::: hint
Write the ratio $I_a / I_b$ and see which factor goes to $0$ or $\infty$ while the others stay bounded.
:::
::: solution
With $\sigma^+ = \max(\sigma, 0)$, $I_a / I_b = \frac{w_a p_b}{w_b p_a} \exp\!\big(-\frac{\sigma_a^+ - \sigma_b^+}{k_1 \bar p}\big) \exp\!\big(-\frac{s_{la} - s_{lb}}{k_2 \bar s}\big)$. (a) For fixed $k_2$ the outer factors are positive constants; if $\sigma_a^+ < \sigma_b^+$ the middle one tends to $+\infty$ as $k_1 \to 0$, which covers both $0 < \sigma_a < \sigma_b$ and $\sigma_a \le 0 < \sigma_b$. (b) For fixed $k_1$, if $s_{la} < s_{lb}$ the last factor tends to $+\infty$ as $k_2 \to 0$. (c) Both exponentials tend to 1, and $I_a/I_b \to (w_a/p_a)/(w_b/p_b)$.
:::
::::
:::: exercise ATCS by hand with another parameter
Compute ATCS on the paint machine with $k_1 = 2$, $k_2 = 1$. Which order results, and why does C overtake B at minute 20?
::: hint
Now $k_1 \bar p = 35$; the changeover scale is still 6.667 min. At minute 20 B's slack is negative and C's is 35.
:::
::: solution
At $t = 0$: A $\frac{1}{20} \times e^{-10/35} = \frac{1}{20} \times 0.7515 = 0.03757$, B 0.01454, C 0.01385, D 0.00328: A. At $t = 20$, still blue: B $\frac{1}{10} \times 0.2231 = 0.02231$; C's slack is exactly one urgency scale, $\frac{1}{15} \times e^{-35/35} = \frac{1}{15} \times 0.3679 = 0.02453$, and it needs no change. C overtakes B: the doubled $k_1$ makes C's slack look small, and B pays the colour change. At $t = 35$, still blue: B 0.02231 against D 0.00893, so B, then D. The order is A C B D, 50 minutes, shortest setup first's order.
:::

:::: exercise Implement ATCS in the shop (coding)
Write an `ATCSController` with the routing and rerouting of `RuleController` and sequencing by ATCS. For an operation that is not the last, subtract the fastest remaining work after it from the due date. Choose $(k_1, k_2)$ on the validation days 5000–5009 only, then run the 20 test days at both levels, check every schedule with `check_schedule`, and compare with the best rule day by day.
::: hint
Subclass `RuleController` and override `sequence`. Use `shop.ws.min_remaining_work(j.family, j.op + 1)`. Take $\bar p$ over the queue and $\bar s$ as the mean off-diagonal changeover of the machine: in the shop the queue changes at every decision, and a fixed per-machine $\bar s$ keeps the changeover scale stable. Score $(k_1, k_2)$ by the mean tardiness over both levels on the validation days.
:::
::: solution
A sketch; the full version, with the grid search, is in `code/b02_numbers.py`.

```python
import math, numpy as np
from jobshop.rules import RuleController
from jobshop.scenario import make_workshop

WS = make_workshop()
SBAR = [np.mean([WS.setup[m][f][g] for f in range(4) for g in range(4) if f != g]) for m in range(5)]

class ATCSController(RuleController):
    def __init__(self, k1, k2):
        super().__init__("SST"); self.name, self.k1, self.k2 = "ATCS", k1, k2
    def sequence(self, shop, m):
        if len(m.queue) == 1:
            return m.queue[0]
        p = {j.idx: shop.ws.proc(j.family, j.op, m.idx) for j in m.queue}
        pbar = np.mean(list(p.values()))
        def index(j):
            slack = j.due - shop.ws.min_remaining_work(j.family, j.op + 1) - p[j.idx] - shop.now
            s = shop.ws.setup_time(m.idx, m.last_family, j.family)
            return math.exp(-max(slack, 0) / (self.k1 * pbar)) * math.exp(-s / (self.k2 * SBAR[m.idx])) / p[j.idx]
        return max(m.queue, key=lambda j: (index(j), -j.idx))
```

Scanning $k_1 \in \{0.5, 1, 2, 3, 5\}$ and $k_2 \in \{0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 5\}$ on validation gives scores from 22.06 to 26.53, best at $k_1 = 3$, $k_2 = 1$. On the test days every schedule passes the checker. Without breakdowns ATCS gives 5.6 min against 7.0 for most urgent first, $-1.5\ [-4.0, +0.4]$ day by day (computed before rounding), better on 9 of 20; with breakdowns 33.1 against 34.1 for shortest setup first, $-1.0\ [-4.0, +2.0]$, better on 10 of 20. Neither interval excludes zero, and optimiser re-planning is at 1.3 and 19.3. A better rule moves the baseline a little; the gap comes from what no local rule can see (Part 6).
:::

Count, over the 20 test days, the share of calls to `sequence` with two or more jobs waiting, per rule and level; then switch rerouting off and measure what it was worth.

```python
class Counting(RuleController):
    def __init__(self, rule, reroute=True):
        super().__init__(rule); self.q = []; self.reroute_on = reroute
    def sequence(self, shop, m):
        self.q.append(len(m.queue)); return super().sequence(shop, m)
    def on_event(self, shop, kind, **info):
        if self.reroute_on:
            super().on_event(shop, kind, **info)
```

## The chapter on one page {#summary}

::: steps
- **A rule is an index** $\pi_j(t)$, computed when a machine frees: local, non-delay. FIFO, SPT, EDD are static; SST is dynamic.
- **Routing**: $E_m = \max(t + \tau(b_j, m+1),\ U_m + R_m + W_m) + p_{jkm}$, with MTTR for the repair and no changeovers; rerouting after a failure.
- **Results**: EDD best without breakdowns; with them SST has the lower mean, from changeovers saved near saturation, but an uncertain lead; EDD keeps the smallest worst case.
- **Blind spots**: other queues, future arrivals, grouping across a horizon.
- **ATCS**: Smith's ratio, urgency by slack, changeover penalty; limits WSPT, minimum slack, SST; not significantly better than the best rule in the shop.
:::

### The vocabulary, in one table

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Dispatching rule | A local recipe picking the next job of a free machine from its queue. |
| Priority index $\pi_j(t)$ | A job's score at a decision; smallest first (ATCS: largest). |
| Static, dynamic index | Fixed while the job waits; or changing with $t$ or the set-up. |
| Non-delay | A free machine with a waiting job never stays idle. |
| Active schedule | No operation can start earlier without delaying another. |
| Earliest expected finish $E_m$ | $\max(a_m, F_m) + p_{jkm}$, minimised over the eligible machines. |
| Rerouting | Routing again the queue of a machine that fails. |
| Slack $\sigma_j(t)$ | $d_j - p_j - t$: how long the job can wait and be on time. |
| WSPT, minimum slack | Largest $w_j/p_j$ first; smallest slack first. |
| ATC, ATCS | Apparent tardiness cost (with setups); $k_1$, $k_2$ its urgency and changeover scales. |

**Scope.** Local, non-delay rules with the repository's routing, on one synthetic workshop at one load; rules that look at the next machine's queue are not covered, and learning which rule to use is chapter [[d01]].

## The same days for every method {#crn}

**The question.** Under frequent breakdowns, CP-SAT re-planning ([[c03]]) has a mean tardiness of 19.3 minutes over the 20 test days and the best dispatching rule, shortest setup first (SST, [[b02]]), 34.1. Orders arrive and machines break at random times. Is the 14.8-minute gap a property of the methods, or the luck of these 20 days?

**Look at one day first.** Seed 7 is a bad day for everybody: in the calm shop first come, first served (FIFO) averages 97.4 minutes of lateness per order and CP-SAT 12.4; under breakdowns 169.9 and 63.6. Had CP-SAT been tested on days 0 to 19 and SST on days 20 to 39, a few heavy days on one side would decide the comparison.

**The remedy: give every method the same days.** A test day *is* a seed: `make_scenario(ws, seed)` draws the 60 arrivals, families and due dates and every machine's breakdown calendar *before* any method runs. The file `results/runs.csv` holds 480 runs, $(7 + 5) \times 20 \times 2$: the seven methods and the five trained DQN agents of Part 4, on 20 days, at two levels.

::: define What are common random numbers?
**In plain words:** test every method on the same random days, so that a difference in results can only come from a difference in decisions.

**More precisely:** an experiment uses *common random numbers* (CRN) when the random inputs of replication $i$, here the arrivals, due dates and breakdown calendars of seed $i$, are identical for every method compared. The results $X_i$ and $Y_i$ of two methods on replication $i$ are then dependent, usually positively, and the analysis works with the paired differences $\Delta_i = X_i - Y_i$.

- Replication :: one independent run of the simulated system; here one test day, one seed.
- Paired difference :: $\Delta_i = \bar T^{X}_i - \bar T^{Y}_i$; negative means $X$ was less late on day $i$.
- Synchronisation :: each random number is used for the same purpose in every run, whatever the controller decides.
:::

**Synchronisation is the hard part.** With one shared generator, if failures were drawn only while a machine works, a method that leaves a machine idle would use fewer random numbers, and every later arrival would differ. The repository avoids this by construction:

::: code jobshop/scenario.py:make_scenario
The arrivals have their own generator, `default_rng([seed, 1])`, each machine its own, `default_rng([seed, 2, m])`. Breakdowns are in *calendar* time, drawn far beyond any run into `scenario.downtime`: a machine fails at the same minute whether or not it is working. Law [[ref:2]] treats this synchronisation as what makes CRN work.
:::

Pick FIFO and CP-SAT in the hero: they rise and fall together; their differences are far more regular. The readouts quote 95 % intervals:

::: define What does a 95 % interval say?
**In plain words:** a range computed from the 20 days that very likely contains the true mean difference over all the days the shop could have.

**More precisely:** a *procedure* that, applied to many independent sets of 20 days, would contain the true mean difference $\mu_\Delta$ in about 95 % of them; for a roughly normal mean, close to the mean $\pm 1.96$ standard errors. It *excludes zero* when it lies wholly on one side of 0, settling the sign of the difference.

- Standard error :: the standard deviation of a mean, $\sigma/\sqrt n$ for $n$ independent days of spread $\sigma$.
:::

## The variance of a paired difference {#variance}

**In plain words.** When both methods do badly on the same days, subtracting their results cancels the shared part. Three days with $X = (10, 20, 60)$ and $Y = (14, 26, 68)$ minutes: each method spreads from tens to sixty, but the differences $(-4, -6, -8)$ barely move. *Covariance* measures how much two results go up and down together; *correlation* is covariance rescaled to lie between $-1$ and $1$ (here $\rho$ is a correlation, not the load of [[b01#load]]). By hand: the deviations from the means 30 and 36 are $(-20, -10, 30)$ and $(-22, -10, 32)$, so the sample variances are $s_X^2 = (400 + 100 + 900)/2 = 700$ and $s_Y^2 = (484 + 100 + 1024)/2 = 804$, the sample covariance $\big((-20)(-22) + (-10)(-10) + (30)(32)\big)/2 = 750$, the correlation $750/\sqrt{700 \times 804} = 0.9997$. Then $s_X^2 + s_Y^2 - 2\,\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}} = 700 + 804 - 1500 = 4$, exactly the variance of $(-4, -6, -8)$: $(4 + 0 + 4)/2 = 4$.

**The identity.** Let $X$ and $Y$ be the two methods' results on one random day, with variances $\sigma_X^2$, $\sigma_Y^2$ and covariance $\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb E[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)]$. Expanding the square,

$$\mathrm{Var}(X - Y) = \mathbb E\big[\big((X - \mu_X) - (Y - \mu_Y)\big)^2\big] = \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 - 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y),$$

that is, $\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 - 2 \rho \sigma_X \sigma_Y$ with the correlation $\rho = \mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) / (\sigma_X \sigma_Y)$.

**Averaged over $n$ days.** The test days are independent (each has its own seed and starts from an empty shop), so the $n$ differences $\Delta_i$ are independent copies of $X - Y$ and their mean $\bar\Delta$ has variance $\mathrm{Var}(X - Y)/n$. Two designs with $n$ days per method:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{common days:} \quad \mathrm{Var}(\bar\Delta) &= \frac{\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 - 2\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y}{n}, \\ \text{independent days:} \quad \mathrm{Var}(\bar X - \bar Y') &= \frac{\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2}{n}, \end{aligned}$$

where $\bar Y'$ is the mean of $Y$ over other days, independent of $\bar X$. Their ratio is

$$\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\bar\Delta)}{\mathrm{Var}(\bar X - \bar Y')} = 1 - \rho\, c, \qquad c = \frac{2\sigma_X\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2} \in (0, 1],$$

with $c = 1$ when the two spreads are equal ($c \le 1$ because $(\sigma_X - \sigma_Y)^2 \ge 0$). So pairing helps exactly when $\rho > 0$, and for $\rho \ge 0$ the variance shrinks at most to the share $1 - \rho$, reached when the methods spread equally.

**The numbers, for CP-SAT minus SST under breakdowns.** $s_X = 17.55$ minutes for CP-SAT, $s_Y = 24.02$ for SST, covariance 411.8, correlation $r = 0.977$. Then

$$s_X^2 + s_Y^2 - 2\,\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}} = 308.0 + 576.9 - 823.6 = 61.3,$$

a standard deviation of 7.83 minutes for the daily difference, against $\sqrt{884.9}$ for unrelated days. Here $c = 0.953$ and $1 - rc = 0.069$: pairing divides the variance by about 14. The standard error of the mean difference is $7.83/\sqrt{20} = 1.75$ minutes paired, 6.65 unpaired.

**Correlations are high everywhere.** The 21 correlations between pairs of the seven methods run from 0.82 to 0.99 in the calm shop (median 0.96) and from 0.88 to 0.98 under breakdowns (median 0.95), the lowest involving quick re-planning (Greedy replan): a burst of arrivals or a long repair is hard for every controller.

**What it changes.** The README compares CP-SAT, Greedy replan and the self-learning agent (DQN, [[d01]]) with the best rule (most urgent first, EDD, calm; SST, breakdowns), and CP-SAT with Greedy replan. Beside each paired interval, the unpaired one of Part 3:

## The bootstrap {#bootstrap}

**The problem.** We have 20 numbers $\Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_{20}$ with mean $\bar\Delta = -14.8$ and want an interval for the true mean difference $\mu_\Delta$. The textbook interval $\bar\Delta \pm t_{19}\, s/\sqrt n$ ($t_{19} = 2.093$, the 97.5 % quantile of Student's $t$ with 19 degrees of freedom) assumes $\bar\Delta$ roughly normal, while tardiness is *skewed*, with a long tail of bad days such as day 7. The bootstrap [[ref:1]] reads the distribution of $\bar\Delta$ from the data instead. Here the two nearly agree, $[-18.4, -11.7]$ and $[-18.5, -11.2]$; the bootstrap needs no formula and works for any statistic.

::: define What is the percentile bootstrap?
**In plain words:** we cannot draw new days from the shop, but we have 20 days that came from it. Pretend they *are* the distribution, draw new sets of 20 from them with replacement, many times, and watch how much the mean moves from set to set; read an interval from that spread.

**More precisely:** let $\hat F_n$ be the empirical distribution of the sample, mass $1/n$ on each $\Delta_i$. Draw $B$ bootstrap samples $\Delta^{*b}_1, \dots, \Delta^{*b}_n$ from $\hat F_n$ ($n$ days chosen uniformly with replacement) and compute each mean $\bar\Delta^{*b}$. The 95 % percentile interval is $[\hat q_{0.025}, \hat q_{0.975}]$, the 2.5 % and 97.5 % quantiles of the $B$ resample means. The course uses $B = 10{,}000$.

- Plug-in principle :: estimate a property $t(F)$ of the unknown distribution by the same property of the empirical one, $t(\hat F_n)$.
- Paired resampling :: resample whole days, keeping day $i$'s two results together; resampling $\Delta_i$ does exactly that.
- Unpaired resampling :: resample $X$'s and $Y$'s days independently and subtract the two resample means, ignoring the pairing.
:::

**Why it works: the plug-in principle.** We need the distribution of $\bar\Delta - \mu_\Delta$, which depends on the unknown distribution $F$ of a day. The plug-in principle replaces $F$ by $\hat F_n$: under $\hat F_n$ the "true mean" is $\bar\Delta$ itself and a sample of $n$ days is a resample, so $\bar\Delta^* - \bar\Delta$ stands for $\bar\Delta - \mu_\Delta$. Taken literally, with $\hat q$ the quantiles of $\bar\Delta^*$,

$$\begin{aligned} &P\big(\hat q_{0.025} - \bar\Delta \le \bar\Delta - \mu_\Delta \le \hat q_{0.975} - \bar\Delta\big) \approx 0.95 \\ \iff\ &\mu_\Delta \in \big[2\bar\Delta - \hat q_{0.975},\ 2\bar\Delta - \hat q_{0.025}\big], \end{aligned}$$
%narrow \mu_\Delta \in \big[2\bar\Delta - \hat q_{0.975},\ 2\bar\Delta - \hat q_{0.025}\big]

the *basic* bootstrap interval, $[-17.9, -11.3]$ for CP-SAT − SST, good when $n$ is large enough for $\hat F_n$ to be close to $F$.

**The percentile interval rests on a different argument** (Efron and Tibshirani [[ref:1]], chapter 13). Suppose some increasing function $g$ makes $g(\bar\Delta) - g(\mu_\Delta)$ normal with mean 0 and some variance $\omega^2$, in the real and the bootstrap world alike. The exact 95 % interval for $g(\mu_\Delta)$ is then $g(\bar\Delta) \pm 1.96\omega$. In the bootstrap world $g(\bar\Delta^*)$ has quantiles $g(\bar\Delta) \pm 1.96\omega$ too, and since $g$ is increasing, the quantiles of $\bar\Delta^*$ are $\hat q = g^{-1}\big(g(\bar\Delta) \pm 1.96\omega\big)$: exactly the ends of that interval mapped back. So the percentile interval is right without our knowing $g$. The two intervals agree when the bootstrap distribution is symmetric about $\bar\Delta$; here the percentile ends lie 3.57 below and 3.09 above the mean.

**One thing can be computed exactly.** A single draw $\Delta^*$ from $\hat F_n$ has $\mathbb E^*[\Delta^*] = \frac1n \sum_i \Delta_i = \bar\Delta$ and $\mathrm{Var}^*(\Delta^*) = \frac1n \sum_i (\Delta_i - \bar\Delta)^2 = \hat\sigma^2$, the plug-in variance. The $n$ draws of a resample are independent, so

$$\mathrm{Var}^*(\bar\Delta^*) = \frac{\hat\sigma^2}{n} = \frac{n - 1}{n} \cdot \frac{s^2}{n},$$

the squared standard error, slightly shrunk ($\hat\sigma^2$ divides by $n$, $s^2$ by $n - 1$). For CP-SAT − SST, $\hat\sigma = 7.63$ and the bootstrap standard error is $7.63/\sqrt{20} = 1.71$, against $s/\sqrt n = 1.75$.

**A hand-sized example.** Three days with differences $-3$, $-6$ and $-15$, mean $-8$. There are $3^3 = 27$ ordered resamples, all equally likely, with means

::: table num
| Resample mean | −15 | −12 | −11 | −9 | −8 | −7 | −6 | −5 | −4 | −3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Resamples | 1 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 6 | 3 | 1 | 3 | 3 | 1 |
:::

(two $-15$ and one $-6$ give $-12$, in 3 orders; one of each gives $-8$, in 6 orders; and so on). The plug-in variance is $\hat\sigma^2 = (25 + 4 + 49)/3 = 26$, and the variance of the 27 means is exactly $26/3 = 8.6667$, a standard error of 2.94 where $s/\sqrt n$ gives 3.61. But the percentile interval is useless here: the smallest mean has probability $1/27 = 0.037$, more than 2.5 %, so the 95 % interval is the whole range $[-15, -3]$, as `bootstrap_ci` returns. With 20 days there are $20^{20} \approx 1.05 \times 10^{26}$ ordered resamples; $B = 10{,}000$ random ones approximate their distribution.

**More days.** Since $\mathrm{Var}(\bar\Delta) = \sigma_\Delta^2/n$, the half-width is about $1.96\, \sigma_\Delta/\sqrt n$: four times the days for half the width, on average. With the hero's slider at 5, 10, 15 and 20 days, the paired interval of CP-SAT − SST is 10.4, 12.0, 8.4 and 6.7 minutes wide, not monotone because the heavy days 6 and 7 (differences $-29.6$ and $-35.4$) raise the standard deviation from 6.27 over 6 days to 10.53 over 8. DQN − SST under breakdowns, $+1.3$ $[-2.1, +5.1]$, has a daily standard deviation of 8.50; a half-width of 1.3 would need $n \approx (1.96 \times 8.50/1.3)^2 = 164$ days.

## Training, validation and test days {#separation}

A method adjusted while looking at the test days has, in part, learned those days: its test score measures how well it fits them, not how it will do tomorrow.

::: define What are training, validation and test sets?
**In plain words:** three disjoint sets of days, one to learn on, one to choose with, and one to report, used once.

**More precisely:** *training* days are what a method learns from (the agent's episodes); *validation* days serve every development choice (hyper-parameters, checkpoints, settings); *test* days are run once, after every choice is frozen, and only they are reported.
:::

In the repository they are seed ranges:

::: table
| Set | Seeds | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| Training | 100001 to 105500: 100000 + 1000 × training seed + episode, episodes 1 to 1500; consecutive agents share 500 days, so the 7,500 episodes use 5,500 distinct days | the DQN agent's learning episodes |
| Validation | 5000 to 5009, both levels | the agent's checkpoints (every 25 episodes) and its training seed; the CP-SAT settings, load and due dates |
| Test | 0 to 19 | `scripts/evaluate.py`, run once with everything fixed |
:::

**What choosing on the test would have reported.** `scripts/train_dqn.py` trains five agents from five training seeds and keeps the best on validation; all five were evaluated on the test days:

::: table num
| Training seed | Validation score | Test, calm | Test, breakdowns |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 23.27 | 7.2 | 33.6 |
| 1 | 23.23 | 7.0 | 36.3 |
| 2 | 24.81 | 7.8 | 37.7 |
| 3 (kept) | 21.98 | 7.4 | 35.4 |
| 4 | 24.02 | 8.1 | 37.0 |
:::

The agent kept on validation scores 7.4 and 35.4. Picking "the best agent" by its test score would report 7.0 in the calm shop (seed 1) and 33.6 under breakdowns (seed 0), the second "beating" SST's 34.1. That claim would not survive: agent 0 minus SST on the same days is $-0.55$ $[-3.4, +2.1]$. The agents may differ a little: 2 of their 20 paired comparisons exclude zero.

## The winner's curse {#winners}

**In plain words.** Run several equally good methods on the same 20 days and report the best. Its result is low partly because the days happened to suit it, luck that does not repeat on fresh days. (In auctions, the winning bid tends to be the one that overestimated the most.)

**The mathematics.** Take $K$ methods with the same true mean $\mu$ and daily spread $\sigma$, any two correlated $\rho$ on a common day. A convenient model writes a day's result for method $k$ as $\mu + \sigma(\sqrt\rho\, Z_i + \sqrt{1 - \rho}\, \varepsilon_{ki})$: a shared day effect $Z_i$ and a method-specific part $\varepsilon_{ki}$, all independent standard normals. Averaged over $n$ days,

$$\bar X_k = \mu + \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\left(\sqrt\rho\, G_0 + \sqrt{1 - \rho}\, G_k\right), \qquad G_0 = \sqrt n\, \bar Z,\ \ G_k = \sqrt n\, \bar\varepsilon_k,$$
%narrow \bar X_k = \mu + \tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\big(\sqrt\rho\, G_0 + \sqrt{1 - \rho}\, G_k\big)

where $\bar Z$ and $\bar\varepsilon_k$ are the day averages of $Z_i$ and $\varepsilon_{ki}$, so that $G_0, G_1, \dots, G_K$ are independent standard normals. The shared term is the same for every $k$, so it does not affect which method wins:

$$\min_k \bar X_k = \mu + \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\left(\sqrt\rho\, G_0 + \sqrt{1 - \rho}\, \min_k G_k\right).$$
%narrow \min_k \bar X_k = \mu + \tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\big(\sqrt\rho\, G_0 + \sqrt{1 - \rho}\, \min_k G_k\big)

Take expectations, with $\mathbb E[G_0] = 0$ and $\mathbb E[\min_k G_k] = -e_K$:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb E\Big[\min_k \bar X_k\Big] - \mu &= -\,\sigma\sqrt{1 - \rho}\;\frac{e_K}{\sqrt n}, \\ e_K = \mathbb E\Big[\max_{k \le K} (-G_k)\Big] &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} w\, K \varphi(w)\, \Phi(w)^{K-1}\, dw, \end{aligned}$$

since $\min_k G_k = -\max_k(-G_k)$, the $-G_k$ are independent standard normals too, and $K\varphi(w)\Phi(w)^{K-1}$ is the density of their maximum, the derivative of $P(\max_k (-G_k) \le w) = \Phi(w)^K$. Numerically, $e_2 = 0.564$ (exactly $1/\sqrt\pi$, exercise 3), $e_3 = 0.846$, $e_4 = 1.029$, $e_5 = 1.163$, $e_{10} = 1.539$ and $e_{20} = 1.867$: the curse grows with the number of candidates, slowly, asymptotically like $\sqrt{2 \ln K}$ (a standard result, cited, not proved; at $K = 20$ it still gives 2.45).

**Read the formula.** The optimism shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$, grows with $K$, and common random numbers reduce it by $\sqrt{1 - \rho}$: only method-specific luck can make one method win. With $\sigma = 24.0$ minutes (SST's spread under breakdowns), $n = 20$ and $K = 4$, independent days give an expected optimism of 5.53 minutes, common days with $\rho = 0.90$ only 1.75; with $K = 10$, 8.26 and 2.61; with $K = 4$, $\rho = 0.90$ and 5 days, 3.50. ## What a fair report contains {#reporting}

A fair comparison shows the size of the difference, its uncertainty and how it was produced. For CP-SAT against SST under breakdowns:

::: steps
- **The protocol.** The same 20 test days (seeds 0 to 19) for every method, run once after all choices were made on other seeds; all 480 runs of `results/runs.csv` passed the checker.
- **Each method, with its interval.** CP-SAT 19.3 $[12.3, 27.1]$, SST 34.1 $[24.8, 44.9]$ minutes of mean tardiness.
- **The paired difference, with its interval.** $-14.8$ $[-18.4, -11.7]$ minutes, far from zero although the two own intervals overlap.
- **Days better, equal and worse.** 20, 0 and 0; the gain on a day ranges from 4.3 to 35.4 minutes, median 11.9.
- **Effect sizes.** Absolute: 14.8 minutes per order. Relative: $1 - 19.3/34.1 = 43\ \%$. Standardised: $d_z = \bar\Delta / s_\Delta = -1.89$, the mean difference in units of its daily spread.
:::

**Counts of days are a test of their own.** If neither method were better, each independent day would favour CP-SAT with probability $\tfrac12$, and all 20 on one side would have probability $2 \times 0.5^{20} = 1.9 \times 10^{-6}$: the sign test, crude but distribution-free. DQN − SST gives $+1.3$ $[-2.1, +5.1]$, $d_z = 0.15$, better on 11 days of 20: no detectable difference.

**Relative sizes can mislead.** In the calm shop CP-SAT cuts EDD's tardiness by 81 %, but the gain is 5.7 minutes and $d_z = -0.71$: there is little tardiness to remove. Report the absolute difference first.

**Many comparisons, a few false alarms.** Seven methods make 21 pairs per level; if none differed, about $0.05 \times 21 = 1.05$ intervals per level would still exclude zero. `scripts/evaluate.py` fixes its comparisons in code, and the README reports all of them.

## The checker as executable invariants {#checker}

Statistics cannot rescue a wrong simulator. If the simulation of [[b01#simpy]] let a method break a rule of the shop, that method would look better on every day, with a tight paired interval.

**A small instance first.** On the paint machine of [[a01]], set up for blue, a log that paints A (blue, minutes 0 to 20) then B (red) from 20 to 30, skipping the 10-minute colour change, finishes B at 30 instead of 40. One line of code, "before each job, the changeover from the previous colour", catches it.

::: define What is an executable invariant?
**In plain words:** a rule of the shop written as code that inspects a finished schedule and complains if the rule was broken, run on every schedule, not trusted.

**More precisely:** a property every valid executed schedule must satisfy. `check_schedule(ws, scenario, log)` takes only the workshop data, the scenario and the executed log (machine work segments and the legs of the automated carts, AGVs) and returns the list of violated invariants, empty for a valid schedule. It reads nothing from the simulator or a solver model, so a bug that breaks a rule of the shop cannot hide from it; bugs that keep every invariant (a controller peeking at future breakdowns, a wrong due date) need other tests.
:::

**What it checks.** Four groups of invariants, re-derived from the log alone:

1. *Every operation, once, correctly*: on one eligible machine $m \in \mathcal M_{jk}$, for exactly $p_{jkm}$, with no changeover work after processing began and no work for an operation that does not exist.
2. *Machines*: no overlap; no work during a breakdown $[t_{\text{down}}, t_{\text{up}})$; no interleaving; before each operation, exactly the changeover $s_m(f, g)$ from the previous family.
3. *Carts*: every leg takes the travel time $\tau(a, b)$, starts where the previous one ended, and never overlaps another leg of the same cart.
4. *Jobs move physically*: operation $k+1$ starts after operation $k$ ends, and the job is carried to the right station before processing starts.

**A bug the checker catches.** The numbers script builds a simulator with a plausible bug: it reads every changeover as zero (`dataclasses.replace(ws, setup=...)` with all entries 0). Under breakdowns, the four rules then report a mean tardiness of 6.2 (FIFO), 4.1 (shortest job first, SPT), 3.3 (EDD) and 3.3 (SST) minutes, against 60.8, 45.4, 37.6 and 34.1 with the real simulator; SST falls back on its tie-break, the due date, and becomes EDD. The checker, given the true workshop, rejects 20 of 20 runs of every rule, with 2,998, 2,907, 2,934 and 2,934 violations such as "job 7 op 0 got 0 min of setup, needs 6".

**Testing the tester.** A checker that accepts everything passes every run, so `tests/test_checker.py` takes a valid EDD log (seed 42, 40 jobs, frequent breakdowns: 277 work segments, 248 cart legs, 0 violations) and breaks it one way per test: an overlap, an operation started too early, a changeover or processing time cut short, work during a breakdown, an ineligible machine, a deleted operation, a cart in two places, teleporting or too fast, a job moved without a cart. All 14 are caught, and `scripts/evaluate.py` refuses to write results if any run fails the checker.

## The code behind it {#code}

Every interval of the course comes from one short function.

::: code jobshop/metrics.py:bootstrap_ci
`rng.choice(values, size=(resamples, len(values)), replace=True)` draws every resample at once: row $b$ is $n$ days drawn with replacement from $\hat F_n$. `samples.mean(axis=1)` gives the $B$ means $\bar\Delta^{*b}$, and `np.quantile` reads the percentile interval; NumPy interpolates between neighbouring order statistics where the browser demo takes the lower one, a source of last-digit differences. The fixed `seed=0` makes every table reproducible.
:::

The paired comparison is built in `level_tables`. Its first statement sorts each method's runs by seed:

::: code scripts/evaluate.py:102-104
After this sort, position $i$ of every list `by[m]` is test day $i$: the sorting *is* the pairing, and where a careless script would go wrong.
:::

A few lines further, `best = min(rules, ...)` picks the best rule on the test means (a small use of the test that the pool study of Part 5 shows is harmless for four well-separated rules), `ref` holds its 20 daily values, and each other method is compared with it:

::: code scripts/evaluate.py:140-146
`d` is the vector of $\Delta_i$; `cell(d, ...)` prints its mean with `bootstrap_ci(d)`, which resamples whole days, and the three sums count the days better, equal and worse.
:::

And the checker's group 2, the one the planted bug trips:

::: code jobshop/checker.py:62-90
Segments are sorted, neighbours compared for overlap, each intersected with `scenario.downtime[machine]`. The list `order` recovers the sequence of operations the machine ran (a repeated entry means interleaving), and the last loop compares the logged setup minutes with `ws.setup_time(machine, previous_family, family)`: here "got 0 min of setup, needs 6" comes from.
:::

## Common pitfalls {#pitfalls}

1. **"The two intervals overlap, so the methods are not different."** CP-SAT's $[12.3, 27.1]$ and SST's $[24.8, 44.9]$ overlap, yet the paired difference is $[-18.4, -11.7]$.
2. **"Same seed, same day."** Only if the random numbers are synchronised.
3. **Tuning on the test.** Every look at the test that changes a choice (a hyper-parameter, a checkpoint, which run to report) makes the reported score optimistic.
4. **"Not significant" read as "equal".** DQN − SST is $[-2.1, +5.1]$: the agent could be 2 minutes better or 5 worse.
5. **Trusting the percentile interval at its word.** With 20 skewed days it covered 90.8 to 93.3 % of the time; with 3 days it returns the data range.
6. **Trusting the simulator's own numbers.** Check every schedule with code that shares nothing with the simulator, and test that code by breaking schedules on purpose.

## Check your understanding {#check}

:::: qa
Try to answer each question before opening it.

::: q 1. Why does running every method on the same seeds make the comparison more precise?
A day's difficulty affects every method alike, so the results are positively correlated and $\mathrm{Var}(X - Y) = \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 - 2\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y$ is much smaller than $\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2$: 61.3 instead of 884.9 for CP-SAT − SST.
:::
::: q 2. What does the repository do so that a seed gives the same day to every method?
It draws the arrivals and each machine's breakdowns from separate generators, `[seed, 1]` and `[seed, 2, m]`, all before the run, and puts the failures in calendar time. No decision can change which random number is used for what.
:::
::: q 3. When can pairing hurt?
When the results are negatively correlated, $\rho < 0$: then $-2\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y > 0$. Here every correlation is between 0.82 and 0.99.
:::
::: q 4. Why must the bootstrap resample days, not each method's results separately?
Resampling days keeps each day's two results, and so their correlation, together; resampling separately throws the correlation away and gives the unpaired interval, 3.8 times wider for CP-SAT − SST.
:::
::: q 5. Why does picking the best of five agents on the test overstate its performance, and by how much here?
The lowest of five noisy scores is low partly by luck that will not repeat: 33.6 under breakdowns, against 35.4 for the agent kept on validation. In the equal-means model the expected optimism is $\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho}\,e_K/\sqrt n$.
:::
::: q 6. What does the checker look at, and why must it not look at anything else?
The workshop data, the scenario and the executed log only. If it used the simulator's state or the solver's model, it would share their bugs.
:::

Under breakdowns the 20 test days give, for DQN ($X$) and SST ($Y$), $s_X = 26.01$ and $s_Y = 24.02$ minutes, and $r = 0.945$. (a) Compute the variance of the daily difference, and the standard error of the mean difference over 20 days, paired and unpaired. (b) How many independent days per method would match the precision of 20 paired days? (c) What would $r = -0.5$ do?

:::: exercise An exact bootstrap interval for four days
Four days have differences $(-2, -4, -6, -20)$. (a) Show that the exact bootstrap variance of the resample mean is 12.5. (b) Enumerate the $4^4 = 256$ ordered resamples and find the exact percentile interval: the smallest $v$ with $P^*(\bar x^* \le v) \ge 0.025$, and with $\ge 0.975$. (c) Is it the data range?
::: hint
$\mathrm{Var}^*(\bar x^*) = \hat\sigma^2/n$. For (b), sort the 256 means and count from each end; $1/256 = 0.0039 < 0.025$.
:::
::: solution
(a) $\bar x = -8$, deviations $6, 4, 2, -12$, so $\hat\sigma^2 = (36 + 16 + 4 + 144)/4 = 50$ and $\mathrm{Var}^* = 50/4 = 12.5$.

```python
import itertools, numpy as np
h = [-2, -4, -6, -20]
means = np.sort([sum(c) / 4 for c in itertools.product(h, repeat=4)])   # the 256 ordered resamples
v, c = np.unique(means, return_counts=True); cum = np.cumsum(c) / 256
print(means.var(), v[np.argmax(cum >= 0.025)], v[np.argmax(cum >= 0.975)])   # 12.5 -16.0 -3.0
```
(b) The lowest means are $-20$ (1 resample, cumulative 0.0039), $-16.5$ (4, 0.0195) and $-16$ (4, 0.0352), so the lower end is $-16$; from the top, $-2$ (1), $-2.5$ (4) and $-3$ (10) take the cumulative from 0.9805 upwards, so the upper end is $-3$: $[-16, -3]$, as `bootstrap_ci` returns. (c) No: each extreme resample has probability $1/256 < 0.025$, so the quantiles move inside $[-20, -2]$; the bad day still makes the interval skewed, $8$ below the mean and $5$ above.
:::

:::: exercise Two candidates: optimism and the wrong winner
Two methods have $n$-day means $\bar X_1$, $\bar X_2$, jointly normal, each with standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt n$, correlation $\rho$. (a) With equal true means $\mu$, use [[d01]] exercise 3 to show $\mathbb E[\min(\bar X_1, \bar X_2)] = \mu - \sigma\sqrt{1 - \rho}/\sqrt{\pi n}$, hence $e_2 = 1/\sqrt\pi$. (b) Now method 2 is worse by $\delta > 0$, and the daily difference (2 minus 1) has standard deviation $s_\Delta$. Show that method 2 wins on $n$ common days with probability about $\Phi(-\delta\sqrt n/s_\Delta)$. Evaluate it for EDD against SST on the pool under breakdowns: $\delta = 3.97$, $s_\Delta = 9.29$, daily spreads 31.02 and 25.91, $n = 20$, common and independent days.
::: hint
(a) Split off Part 5's shared day effect. (b) Method 2 wins when $\bar\Delta < 0$, with $\bar\Delta$ about $\mathcal N(\delta, s_\Delta^2/n)$; independent days replace $s_\Delta^2$ by the sum of the two variances.

::: solution
(a) In the model of Part 5, $\bar X_k = \mu + c + Y_k$, with $c = \sigma\sqrt{\rho/n}\,G_0$ shared and $Y_1, Y_2$ independent $\mathcal N(0, s^2)$, $s = \sigma\sqrt{(1-\rho)/n}$. (b) Standardise: $P(\bar\Delta < 0) = \Phi(-\delta\sqrt n/s_\Delta)$. Common days: $3.97\sqrt{20}/9.29 = 1.91$, probability 0.028. Independent days: $\sqrt{31.02^2 + 25.91^2} = 40.41$, $3.97\sqrt{20}/40.41 = 0.44$, probability 0.330. On 10,000 random 20-day sets of the pool, EDD wins 2.1 % and 33.6 % of the time: pairing makes the wrong winner rare.

:::: exercise Write a new invariant
Every job needs at least its fastest-route work: $C_j \ge r_j + W_{f(j)}$, with $W_f$ the fastest-route work of family $f$ (`ws.min_remaining_work(f, 0)`). Write a check of it, verify that a valid EDD run passes and that shifting one job's segments 1,000 minutes earlier fails. Is it implied by the checker's invariants?
::: hint
A job's completion is the largest `end` among its segments. The shift puts the job's work before its arrival.

::: solution
```python
from dataclasses import replace
from jobshop.rules import RuleController
from jobshop.scenario import make_scenario, make_workshop
from jobshop.shop import simulate
ws = make_workshop(); sc = make_scenario(ws, 3, mtbf=120.0)

def check_min_flow(ws, sc, log):
    end = {}
    for s in log.segments:                                   # a job's completion: its latest segment end
        end[s.job] = max(end.get(s.job, s.end), s.end)
    return [f"job {j.idx} done at {end[j.idx]}, needs at least {j.arrival + ws.min_remaining_work(j.family, 0)}"
            for j in sc.jobs if end[j.idx] < j.arrival + ws.min_remaining_work(j.family, 0)]

log = simulate(ws, sc, RuleController("EDD"))
print(check_min_flow(ws, sc, log))                           # []
log.segments[:] = [replace(s, start=s.start - 1000, end=s.end - 1000) if s.job == 10 else s for s in log.segments]
print(check_min_flow(ws, sc, log)[:1])                       # ['job 10 done at -636, needs at least 250']
```
It is implied: groups 1 and 4 already force every operation, in order, to start after the job is available and to last $p_{jkm} \ge \min_m p_{jkm}$. A redundant invariant is still a cheap second line of defence.

## The chapter on one page {#summary}

::: steps
- **Common random numbers**: the same, synchronised seeds for every method.
- **The paired variance**: $\mathrm{Var}(X - Y) = \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 - 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)$; for CP-SAT − SST an interval 6.7 instead of 25.2 minutes wide.
- **The bootstrap**: the plug-in principle yields the basic interval, a normalising transformation the percentile one.
- **Separation**: train from seed 100001, choose on 5000 to 5009, report 0 to 19 once.
- **The winner's curse**: the best of $K$ equal candidates is optimistic by $\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho}\,e_K/\sqrt n$.
- **The report**: intervals on means and paired differences, day counts, effect sizes.
- **The checker**: invariants checked on every schedule from the log alone.
:::

### The vocabulary, in one table

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Replication | One independent run of the simulated system: one test day, one seed. |
| Common random numbers (CRN) | The same random inputs for every method on a replication. |
| Synchronisation | Each random number serves the same purpose in every run. |
| Paired difference $\Delta_i$ | The two methods' results on the same day, subtracted. |
| Covariance, correlation | How two results move together across days. |
| Plug-in principle | Estimate $t(F)$ by $t(\hat F_n)$. |
| Percentile interval | The 2.5 % and 97.5 % quantiles of the resample statistics. |
| Training, validation, test | Days to learn on, to choose with, to report once. |
| Winner's curse | The optimism of the best of several noisy scores. |
| Effect size | The size of a difference: absolute, relative or standardised ($d_z$). |
| Invariant | A property every valid schedule satisfies, checked by code. |

**Scope.** The workshop is synthetic, its days independent by construction; a real plant would need a block bootstrap and a checker fed by real event logs.

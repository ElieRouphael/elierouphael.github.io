## A workshop you can experiment on {#why}

You run the workshop of chapter [[a03]]: five machines, two carts, orders of four families arriving all day, changeovers, breakdowns. Does "most urgent first" beat "shortest setup first"? Trying each rule for weeks on the real shop is impossible: the orders change, and the losing rule costs real late deliveries.

A **simulation** replaces the workshop by a program that follows the same causes and effects: orders appear, wait, are carried, set up, processed, delayed by breakdowns and leave. On it you can replay the same day under every rule, so that the method is the only difference between two runs (chapter [[d02#crn]]). One simulated day of 1,348 minutes (22.5 hours) under a rule takes a median of 5.8 ms: $1.4 \times 10^7$ times faster than real time.

::: define What is a discrete-event simulation?
**In plain words:** a program that keeps a list of what is going to happen next (an order arrives at 10:42, machine 3 finishes at 10:47), jumps straight to the soonest item, updates the shop, writes the new consequences onto the list, and repeats.

**More precisely:** the state of the model changes only at a countable set of instants, the **events**; between two events nothing changes and nothing is computed. The simulator keeps a **simulation clock** $t$ and an **event list** of pending events keyed by their time. One iteration removes the earliest event, sets $t$ to its time and runs its **event routine**, which changes the state and may schedule or cancel future events.

- State :: what fixes the future given the random inputs: queues, what each machine does and has left, which machines are down, where the carts are.
- Event :: an instant at which the state changes: an arrival, the end of a changeover or of an operation, a failure, a repair, a cart reaching a station.
- Random inputs :: arrivals, families, due dates and breakdowns, drawn with a seed so that a run can be replayed.
:::

**The course's digital twin.** A *digital twin* usually means a model kept in step with a physical plant by its live data. In this course it means less: the simulator of this chapter together with its controller interface (Part 8), into which any decision method can be plugged. It is synthetic, invented rather than measured, and synchronised with no plant: a fair test bench for decision methods, not a forecast of a factory.

## Time steps or events {#events}

**Time-stepped.** Choose a step $\Delta$, say one minute, and loop: $t \leftarrow t + \Delta$, then ask every machine, cart and order whether anything happened. Simple, but most steps are empty, events are rounded to the grid, and two events in one step are ordered arbitrarily.

**Event-driven.** Keep the list of future events and jump from one event time to the next: no empty steps, no rounding. The price is bookkeeping: every routine must write its consequences onto the list, and strike off those that will no longer happen.

::: define What is the event list?
**In plain words:** the simulator's diary of the future, sorted by time. Its top line says what happens next and when.

**More precisely:** a priority queue of records (time, tie-breaker, event), with two operations, *insert* and *remove the minimum*. A binary heap does each in about $\log_2 n$ comparisons for $n$ pending events. SimPy keeps exactly this: a heap of (time, priority, sequence number, event), where the sequence number breaks ties in scheduling order, so that a run is deterministic.

- Clock :: the time of the event being executed; it jumps and never moves backwards.
- Scheduling, cancelling :: inserting a future event ("B's changeover will be done at minute 30"); striking off one that will not happen (a breakdown at minute 25 cancels it).
:::

**The paint machine, traced by hand.** Take the four orders of chapter [[a02]], blue A (20 min), red B (10), blue C (15) and red D (25), but let them arrive at minutes 0, 5, 12 and 40. The machine is set up for blue, a colour change takes 10 minutes, the rule is first come, first served (FIFO), and the machine is down from minute 25 to 33. An interrupted operation, changeover included, keeps the work done and resumes after the repair. Only the *next* arrival is on the list: each arrival schedules the one after it. The hero replays this trace.

With carts that take no time, the repository's simulator produces the same log, B's changeover in two pieces (20 to 25, 33 to 38); exercise 5 writes one that does too.

On test day 0 under "most urgent first" (EDD) without breakdowns, SimPy executes 1,680 events, bookkeeping included, on only 613 of the 1,384 minutes: a one-minute loop would be idle in 56 % of its steps.

## Random arrivals: the Poisson process {#poisson}

::: define What is a Poisson process?
**In plain words:** arrivals at a constant average rate but without rhythm: clumps and droughts happen by chance, and when the last order came tells you nothing about when the next comes.

**More precisely:** a counting process $N_{\mathrm{arr}}(t)$, the number of arrivals in $[0, t]$, with $N_{\mathrm{arr}}(0) = 0$, is a Poisson process of rate $\lambda > 0$ when its increments over disjoint intervals are independent, and an interval of length $h$ gains one arrival with probability $\lambda h + o(h)$ and two or more with probability $o(h)$, where $o(h)$ vanishes faster than $h$. Equivalently, the gaps $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are independent exponentials of rate $\lambda$; the equivalence is a standard theorem, which we only cite [[ref:3]].

- Exponential distribution :: $P(X > x) = e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x \ge 0$, density $\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$.
- Count in a window :: $N_{\mathrm{arr}}(t+u) - N_{\mathrm{arr}}(t)$ is Poisson with mean $\lambda u$: $P(k \text{ arrivals}) = e^{-\lambda u} (\lambda u)^k / k!$.
- In the workshop :: $1/\lambda = 20.83$ min, $\lambda = 0.0480$ orders per minute, 2.88 per hour.
:::

**Mean and variance, derived.** Integrate by parts; the boundary terms vanish because $x^k e^{-\lambda x} \to 0$:

$$\begin{aligned} E[X] &= \int_0^\infty x\, \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\, dx = \Big[-x e^{-\lambda x}\Big]_0^\infty + \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda x}\, dx = \frac1\lambda, \\ E[X^2] &= \int_0^\infty x^2 \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\, dx = \Big[-x^2 e^{-\lambda x}\Big]_0^\infty + \frac2\lambda \int_0^\infty x\, \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\, dx = \frac2\lambda E[X] = \frac2{\lambda^2}, \\ \operatorname{Var} X &= E[X^2] - E[X]^2 = \frac2{\lambda^2} - \frac1{\lambda^2} = \frac1{\lambda^2}. \end{aligned}$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} E[X] &= \int_0^\infty x \lambda e^{-\lambda x} dx \\ &= 0 + \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda x} dx = \tfrac1\lambda \\ E[X^2] &= \int_0^\infty x^2 \lambda e^{-\lambda x} dx \\ &= 0 + \tfrac2\lambda \int_0^\infty x \lambda e^{-\lambda x} dx \\ &= \tfrac2\lambda E[X] = \tfrac2{\lambda^2} \\ \operatorname{Var} X &= E[X^2] - E[X]^2 = \tfrac1{\lambda^2} \end{aligned}

The standard deviation equals the mean, 20.83 minutes for the workshop (a variance of 434 min²): a coefficient of variation of 1, where a timetable has 0.

**The memoryless property, proved.** For $s, t \ge 0$,

$$P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = \frac{P(X > s + t)}{P(X > s)} = \frac{e^{-\lambda (s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} = e^{-\lambda t} = P(X > t).$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} P(X > s+t \mid X > s) &= \tfrac{P(X > s+t)}{P(X > s)} \\ &= \tfrac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} \\ &= e^{-\lambda t} = P(X > t) \end{aligned}

After $s$ minutes without an order, the remaining wait has the distribution of a fresh gap. The exponential is the only continuous distribution with this property (exercise 2), which is why "a constant chance per minute" and "exponential gaps" are the same model. In the widget, uniform gaps of the same mean remember: waiting makes the next arrival come sooner.

**The real stream.** The 1,200 gaps of the 20 test days have mean 20.14 min; the standard error of a mean of 1,200 draws is $20.83/\sqrt{1200} = 0.60$, so 20.14 is 1.14 standard errors from 20.83: ordinary chance.

<details class="sol"><summary>Going further: the test-day streams against the Poisson model</summary>

The 392 full hours hold 2.93 arrivals on average with variance 3.12, against a Poisson mean and variance of 2.88; an hour without orders happens 6.6 % of the time, against $e^{-2.88} = 0.056$. Of the 442 gaps longer than 20 min, 0.344 last beyond 40, where memorylessness predicts $e^{-20/20.83} = 0.383$: 1.7 standard errors (0.023) away, within chance.

</details>

**Merging (superposition).** For independent Poisson streams of rates $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, the time to the next event of either is $\min(X_1, X_2)$, and

$$P(\min(X_1, X_2) > t) = P(X_1 > t)\, P(X_2 > t) = e^{-(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2) t}:$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} P(\min(X_1, X_2) > t) &= P(X_1 > t) P(X_2 > t) \\ &= e^{-(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2) t} \end{aligned}

the first gap is exponential with rate $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2$. The merged stream is Poisson by the definition: the sum of two independent processes with independent increments has independent increments, and in an interval of length $h$ it gains one event with probability $(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2) h + o(h)$ and two or more with probability $o(h)$, since both streams firing has probability $\lambda_1 \lambda_2 h^2 + o(h^2)$. With all five machines up, each failing at rate $1/120$ per minute, the next failure *somewhere in the shop* comes after a mean $120/5 = 24$ minutes.

**Splitting (thinning).** If each arrival of a Poisson stream of rate $\lambda$ is independently labelled family $f$ with probability $p$, the family-$f$ arrivals form a Poisson process of rate $p\lambda$. No family-$f$ order comes in the first $t$ minutes exactly when each of the $k$ arrivals in that window, $k$ Poisson with mean $\lambda t$, is labelled not-$f$, which happens with probability $(1-p)^k$. So the wait $X^{(f)}$ for the first family-$f$ order has

$$P(X^{(f)} > t) = \sum_{k \ge 0} e^{-\lambda t} \frac{(\lambda t)^k}{k!} (1-p)^k = e^{-\lambda t}\, e^{(1-p)\lambda t} = e^{-p\lambda t},$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} P(X^{(f)} > t) &= \sum_{k \ge 0} e^{-\lambda t} \tfrac{(\lambda t)^k}{k!} (1-p)^k \\ &= e^{-\lambda t} e^{(1-p)\lambda t} = e^{-p\lambda t} \end{aligned}

an exponential of rate $p\lambda$ (the sum is the series of $e^x$ at $x = (1-p)\lambda t$). Each later family-$f$ gap is built in the same way from its own disjoint block of gaps and labels, so all are independent copies of $X^{(f)}$: a Poisson process. That the families are also independent of one another needs a further argument, which Ross gives [[ref:3]]. The workshop draws families uniformly, $p = 1/4$: each family arrives with mean gap $4 \times 20.83 = 83.33$ min.

## Load, by hand, and what it leaves out {#load}

Five machines offer five machine-minutes every minute; orders bring (orders per minute) × (minutes of work per order). The **load** is the ratio.

::: define What is the load ρ?
**In plain words:** how busy the machines would have to be, on average, to keep up with the orders, counting only processing on the fastest routes.

**More precisely:** with arrival rate $\lambda$, expected fastest-route work per job $E[W_{f(j)}]$ and $m$ machines, $\rho = \lambda\, E[W_{f(j)}] / m$. If $\rho \ge 1$, work arrives faster than the machines could process it even on their fastest routes, and the queue grows without bound. $\rho < 1$ is necessary, not sufficient: a machine that alone can do some operations can be overloaded alone. With the work actually done, $\omega_j$, in place of $W_{f(j)}$, the ratio is the busy fraction (Part 6).

- Fastest-route work :: $W_f$ in [[a03#workshop]]: each operation of family $f$ on its quickest eligible machine.
:::

**By hand, for the workshop.** The fastest routes of the four families take 46, 61, 70 and 73 minutes; families are equally likely, so

$$E[W_{f(j)}] = \frac{46 + 61 + 70 + 73}{4} = 62.5 \text{ min}, \qquad \frac1\lambda = \frac{E[W_{f(j)}]}{\rho\, m} = \frac{62.5}{0.60 \times 5} = 20.83 \text{ min}.$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} E[W_{f(j)}] &= \tfrac{46+61+70+73}{4} = 62.5 \text{ min} \\ \tfrac1\lambda &= \tfrac{E[W_{f(j)}]}{\rho m} = \tfrac{62.5}{0.60 \times 5} = 20.83 \text{ min} \end{aligned}

The repository fixes $\rho = 0.60$ and derives the gap, which is what `mean_interarrival` computes.

**What $\rho$ ignores**, each item computed from the workshop data:

- *Non-fastest routes.* If every operation took the average time of its eligible machines, a job would bring 68.00 min and $\rho = 0.65$.
- *Changeovers.* The mean changeover between two different families is 6.95 min (from 3 to 10). If the family changed with probability $3/4$ (random order, a rough estimate), that adds about 5.21 min per operation, 20.9 per job: $\rho = 0.80$. They depend on the order of the jobs, the decision being studied, so the load leaves them out.
- *Breakdowns.* A machine is up a fraction $A = 0.857$ of the time (Part 5): per *available* machine, $0.60 / 0.857 = 0.70$.
- *Transport.* A job on a cart, 2 or 3 minutes per move, is in the shop but not processed.

**Measured.** Over the 20 test days without breakdowns, first come, first served (FIFO) spends 67.3 min of processing and 18.1 min of changeover per job: $\lambda E[\omega]/m = 0.82$ with both counted. Shortest setup first (SST) cuts the changeovers to 15.3 min per job (0.79), optimiser re-planning (CP-SAT) to 10.6 (0.74). How busy the machines are depends on the decisions.

## Breakdowns and availability {#breakdowns}

Each machine works, fails, is repaired, works again. The repository draws the working stretch from an exponential of mean MTBF = 120 min, in calendar time (busy or idle), and the repair from a uniform distribution on $[0.5, 1.5] \times \text{MTTR} = [10, 30]$ min, mean 20 and standard deviation $20/\sqrt{12} = 5.77$, both rounded to whole minutes.

::: define What is an alternating renewal process?
**In plain words:** a machine that alternates between "up" and "down", every up-and-down cycle a fresh, independent copy of the same random pair.

**More precisely:** up times $X^{\mathrm{up}}_1, X^{\mathrm{up}}_2, \dots$ and down times $X^{\mathrm{down}}_1, X^{\mathrm{down}}_2, \dots$ (like the gap $X$ of Part 3), the pairs $(X^{\mathrm{up}}_i, X^{\mathrm{down}}_i)$ independent and identically distributed; cycle $i$ lasts $X^{\mathrm{up}}_i + X^{\mathrm{down}}_i$ minutes, of mean $\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR}$.

- MTBF :: here $E[X^{\mathrm{up}}]$, the mean time from a repair to the next failure, 120 min; some texts mean the whole cycle.
- MTTR :: $E[X^{\mathrm{down}}]$, the mean repair time, 20 min.
- Availability :: $A$, the long-run fraction of time a machine is up.
:::

**Availability from expected cycle lengths.** Over the first $n$ cycles the machine is up $\sum_{i \le n} X^{\mathrm{up}}_i$ out of $\sum_{i \le n} (X^{\mathrm{up}}_i + X^{\mathrm{down}}_i)$. Divide both by $n$ and apply the strong law of large numbers to each:

$$\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n X^{\mathrm{up}}_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n (X^{\mathrm{up}}_i + X^{\mathrm{down}}_i)} = \frac{\frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n X^{\mathrm{up}}_i}{\frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n (X^{\mathrm{up}}_i + X^{\mathrm{down}}_i)} \;\xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{}\; \frac{E[X^{\mathrm{up}}]}{E[X^{\mathrm{up}}] + E[X^{\mathrm{down}}]} = A,$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} \frac{\sum_{i \le n} X^{\mathrm{up}}_i}{\sum_{i \le n} (X^{\mathrm{up}}_i + X^{\mathrm{down}}_i)} &= \frac{\frac1n \sum_{i \le n} X^{\mathrm{up}}_i}{\frac1n \sum_{i \le n} (X^{\mathrm{up}}_i + X^{\mathrm{down}}_i)} \\ &\to \frac{E[X^{\mathrm{up}}]}{E[X^{\mathrm{up}}] + E[X^{\mathrm{down}}]} = A \end{aligned}

that is, $A = \text{MTBF}/(\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR})$, the simplest case of the renewal-reward theorem [[ref:3]]. To pass from $n$ cycles to a time $T$, let $B(T)$ be the number of cycles started by $T$; $T$ lies between the ends of cycles $B(T)-1$ and $B(T)$, both of which grow like $B(T)\,(\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR})$, so $T/B(T) \to \text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR}$. The up time in $[0, T]$ lies between $\sum_{i < B(T)} X^{\mathrm{up}}_i$ and $\sum_{i \le B(T)} X^{\mathrm{up}}_i$; divided by $T = B(T) \cdot (T/B(T))$, both bounds tend to $\text{MTBF}/(\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR})$, so the up fraction of $[0, T]$ tends to $A$ too. For the workshop,

$$A = \frac{120}{120 + 20} = \frac{120}{140} = 0.857, \qquad 1 - A = 0.1429:$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} A &= \tfrac{120}{120 + 20} = \tfrac{120}{140} = 0.857 \\ 1 - A &= 0.1429 \end{aligned}

each machine is down about 14.3 % of the time. Only the means enter $A$, not the distributions. By the same argument a machine has one breakdown per $\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR} = 140$ minutes: over $T = 1{,}400$ minutes, about a simulated day with breakdowns, $1400/140 = 10.0$ per machine.

**On the test days.** The 7,130 generated cycles have mean up time 119.9 min and mean repair 19.98 min: availability 0.857. Over the first 1,400 minutes of the 100 machine-days, the machines are down 13.9 % of the time with 9.78 breakdowns on average, but single machine-days range from 2.9 % to 23.9 % and from 2 to 16 breakdowns: one day is a noisy sample.

**What a breakdown does to an operation.** A failure suspends the operation in progress; the work done is kept and the rest resumes after the repair, the "preempt-resume" assumption [[ref:2]]. ## Little's law {#little}

In plain words: if orders come in at 3 an hour and each stays 2 hours, about 6 orders are inside on average. Little's law says no more, and it holds for any rule, any routing and any breakdowns.

::: define What is Little's law?
**In plain words:** the average number of jobs inside equals the rate at which they come in times the average time each spends inside.

**More precisely:** observe a system on $[0, T]$; let $N(t)$ be the number of jobs inside, $L_T = \frac1T \int_0^T N(t)\, dt$ its time average, $n$ the number of arrivals, $\lambda_T = n/T$ and $\bar F_T$ their mean time inside. If the system is empty at $0$ and at $T$, then $L_T = \lambda_T \bar F_T$ exactly. In the long run $L = \lambda \bar F$, usually written $L = \lambda W$ (this course keeps $W$ for the fastest-route work $W_f$). Little proved it for stationary systems [[ref:4]]; the sample-path version is below.

- Flow time, work in progress :: $F_j = C_j - r_j$ ([[a01]]), of mean $\bar F$; $L$, the average number of jobs in the shop.
:::

**The proof is an area counted twice.** Draw for each job a horizontal bar from its arrival $r_j$ to its completion $C_j$. At any instant, $N(t)$ is the number of bars crossing $t$, so the area under $N$ is the total length of the bars:

$$\int_0^T N(t)\, dt = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (C_j - r_j) = n\, \bar F_T \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac1T \int_0^T N(t)\, dt = \frac nT \cdot \bar F_T .$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} \int_0^T N(t) dt &= \sum_{j=1}^{n} (C_j - r_j) = n \bar F_T \\ \Longrightarrow \tfrac1T \int_0^T N(t) dt &= \tfrac nT \cdot \bar F_T \end{aligned}

If jobs are still inside at $T$, their bars are cut and the two sides differ by the cut-off parts; divided by $T$, the difference tends to 0 whenever $\lambda$ and $\bar F$ exist and are finite, the sample-path form of Little's theorem [[ref:6]].

**Applied to the machines: the busy fraction.** Let the "system" be the machines at work: a job counts as inside only while a machine works on it, changeover included. Job $j$ then stays inside for $\omega_j$ minutes, its work actually done, possibly in pieces cut by breakdowns. At most one machine works on a job, so the number inside is the number of busy machines, $N_s(t)$. Little's law with $\omega_j$ in place of $F_j$, divided by the $m$ machines, gives the long-run busy fraction $u$ of a machine:

$$\underbrace{\lim_{T \to \infty} \frac1T \int_0^T N_s(t)\, dt}_{\text{mean number of busy machines}} = \lambda\, E[\omega] \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad u = \frac{\lambda\, E[\omega]}{m}.$$
%narrow \begin{aligned} \lim_{T \to \infty} \tfrac1T \int_0^T N_s(t) dt &= \lambda E[\omega] \\ \Longrightarrow\ u &= \tfrac{\lambda E[\omega]}{m} \end{aligned}

This is the promise of Part 4: $W_{f(j)} \le \omega_j$, so $\rho = 0.60$ is a lower bound on $u$, and FIFO's 0.82 is $u$ for an endless stream of orders. A machine works only while up, so a stable shop needs $u < A$: in the hero, where half the orders need a colour change, $u$ is the slider times $(17.5 + 0.5 \times 10)/17.5 = 1.29$, and $u < 0.857$ means a slider below 0.67.

**On the worked example.** $N(t)$ is 1 on $[0, 5)$, 2 on $[5, 12)$, 3 on $[12, 20)$, 2 on $[20, 40)$, 3 on $[40, 48)$, 2 on $[48, 73)$ and 1 on $[73, 108)$: an area of $5 + 14 + 24 + 40 + 24 + 50 + 35 = 192$ job-minutes, and the flow times 20, 43, 61 and 68 sum to 192 too. So $L = 192/108 = 1.778 = (4/108) \times 48 = \lambda_T \bar F_T$. The machine works 100 of the 108 minutes, 70 processing and 30 changing colour: busy $100/108 = 0.926 = (4/108) \times 25 = \lambda_T E[\omega]$.

**On the workshop.** A simulated day starts and ends empty, so the proof applies exactly: counting $N(t)$ from the executed logs of the 20 test days, FIFO without breakdowns has a day-average $L$ of 7.146 jobs and $\lambda_T \bar F_T = 7.146$, to every digit. The area under $N$, $n \bar F$ job-minutes, compares methods directly, since all face the same 60 orders a day: $60 \times 157.4 = 9{,}444$ under FIFO, $60 \times 124.9 = 7{,}494$ under CP-SAT, 21 % fewer. Little's law is also the standard check of a simulator's bookkeeping (exercise 5).

<details class="sol"><summary>Going further: the nominal λ against the day's own rate</summary>

The nominal rate gives a larger $\lambda \bar F = 157.4/20.83 = 7.56$, the work in progress of a day that never ends; a real day drains after its last arrival ($\lambda_T \approx 1/22.3$ per minute). With breakdowns: 9.670 against 10.90. Over its own day FIFO keeps the machines busy 0.765 of the time, not 0.82.

## SimPy in a few ideas {#simpy}

An event list by hand is error-prone for a workshop. SimPy [[ref:5]] lets you tell each actor's story in order and keeps the list for you.

::: define What is a SimPy process?
**In plain words:** a function that tells the life of one actor, an order stream, a machine, a cart, in the order things happen to it, saying "wait" wherever time passes.

**More precisely:** a Python generator registered with `env.process(gen)`. Each `yield` hands an event to the `simpy.Environment` and suspends the generator until the event is triggered; yielding `env.timeout(d)` means "sleep $d$ simulated minutes". `env.run()` repeatedly pops the earliest event from the heap, sets `env.now` to its time and resumes the processes waiting on it.

- Event :: any `simpy.Event`, a timeout or one that another process triggers with `succeed()`.
- Interrupt :: `proc.interrupt(cause)` throws `simpy.Interrupt` into a waiting process, whose pending timeout no longer resumes it: how a breakdown stops an operation.
:::

The arrivals of Part 2 as a process: the loop is the story, the timeouts are the gaps.

```python
import simpy

def arrivals(env, gaps):
    for name, g in zip("ABCD", gaps):
        yield env.timeout(g)              # sleep g simulated minutes
        print(f"t = {env.now}: order {name} arrives")

env = simpy.Environment()
env.process(arrivals(env, [0, 5, 7, 28]))
env.run()                                 # t = 0, 5, 12, 40
```

The breakdown of Part 2 as an interrupt: the machine finishes A at 20, starts B's colour change, is interrupted at 25 with 5 minutes left, waits out the 8-minute repair and finishes at 38, as in the trace.

```python
def machine(env):
    yield env.timeout(20)                 # A is processed from 0 to 20
    left = 10                             # then the colour change for B
    while left > 0:
        start = env.now
        try:
            yield env.timeout(left)
            left = 0
        except simpy.Interrupt as stop:
            left -= env.now - start       # keep the work already done
            yield env.timeout(stop.cause) # wait out the repair
    print(f"t = {env.now}: changeover done")

def breakdown(env, victim):
    yield env.timeout(25)
    victim.interrupt(8)                   # down for 8 minutes

env = simpy.Environment()
proc = env.process(machine(env))
env.process(breakdown(env, proc))
env.run()                                 # done at 38
```

The repository refines the second pattern: the machine cannot know the repair time, so the breakdown process triggers an event `up_event` when the repair is over, and the interrupted operation waits on it.

## The workshop in code {#code}

The scenario is drawn before the simulation starts, so the random inputs are the same whatever the decisions. `make_scenario` draws exponential gaps of mean $1/\lambda$ (Part 4), floors their cumulative sums to arrival minutes, and draws uniform families (Part 3) and due dates $r_j + k W_f$, $k$ uniform in $[2, 4]$. Then the breakdowns:

::: code jobshop/scenario.py:144-157
Each machine's downtime is the alternating renewal process of Part 5, generated far beyond any run and hidden from the controllers. Breakdowns have their own generator, `[seed, 2, m]` (the arrivals use `[seed, 1]`), so a seed gives the same jobs with and without breakdowns ([[d02#crn]]).
:::

`Shop.run` starts one process for the arrivals and one per machine with downtime, and runs until the last job is done. `Shop._arrivals` waits for each arrival, tells the controller (where CP-SAT re-plans, chapter [[c03]]) and asks for a routing.

::: code jobshop/shop.py:Shop._breakdowns
At `t_down` the machine is marked down, a fresh `up_event` is created, the work done so far is booked and the operation interrupted; `Shop._work`, a loop over setup and processing segments, then waits on `up_event`. The controller hears of the breakdown and may reroute the queue (chapter [[b02]]).
:::

::: code jobshop/shop.py:Shop._try_start
The only place a machine starts a job, and where the simulator asks a controller to sequence: `controller.sequence(shop, m)` returns a queued job, or `None` to wait (CP-SAT keeps a machine for a planned job on its way). The changeover comes from the data.
:::

Carts follow the same pattern; requests go oldest first to the nearest idle cart, for every method: simulated, not optimised.

**The controller interface.** The simulator takes no decision. It calls `controller.route(shop, job)` when a job needs a machine, `controller.sequence(shop, machine)` when a machine is free, and `controller.on_event(shop, kind, ...)` on every arrival, breakdown and repair. That makes the comparison fair: FIFO, the DQN agent and CP-SAT are objects with the same three methods, run on the same scenarios and judged by the same checker (chapter [[d02]]). Exercise 4 writes another.

## Common pitfalls {#pitfalls}

1. **"One order every 20.83 minutes."** That is the average: the test gaps range from 0 to 161 minutes.
2. **"A load of 0.60 means the machines are 60 % busy."** It counts fastest-route processing only; the busy fraction is 0.74 to 0.82, depending on the method.
3. **"MTBF is the time between two failures."** Here it runs from a repair to the next failure; failures are MTBF + MTTR = 140 min apart.
4. **"Little's law needs Poisson arrivals or FIFO."** It needs neither, only care with the window: exact on one that starts and ends empty.
5. **"Events at the same time happen at the same time."** They run one after the other in tie-breaker order, and results can depend on it.

## Check your understanding {#check}

:::: qa
Try to answer each question before opening it.

::: q 1. Why does the clock of an event-driven simulator jump rather than tick?
The state changes only at events: between two there is nothing to compute.
:::
::: q 2. In the repository's shop, what can be on the event list at a given moment?
The next arrival; each machine's next failure or repair; the end of each running segment and of each cart leg; SimPy's own bookkeeping.
:::
::: q 3. A machine has been up for 100 minutes. What is the expected time to its next failure?
120 minutes, as if just repaired: the exponential up time is memoryless. Real machines wear, and their failure rate grows with age; the model ignores that.
:::
::: q 4. Why does availability depend only on MTBF and MTTR, not on their distributions?
The long-run up fraction is $\sum X^{\mathrm{up}}_i / \sum (X^{\mathrm{up}}_i + X^{\mathrm{down}}_i)$, and by the law of large numbers each sum divided by $n$ tends to its mean. Only $E[X^{\mathrm{up}}] = \text{MTBF}$ and $E[X^{\mathrm{down}}] = \text{MTTR}$ survive the limit.
:::
::: q 5. Why is the load ρ = 0.60 a lower bound on how busy the machines are?
By Little's law applied to the machines, the busy fraction is $\lambda E[\omega]/m$ with $\omega_j$ the work actually done; that work includes changeovers and slower routes, so it is at least the fastest-route work in $\rho$.
:::
::: q 6. Why does the simulator leave every decision to a controller?
So that every method runs on the same model and scenario and only the decisions differ.
:::

:::: exercise Only the exponential is memoryless
Let $X \ge 0$ be continuous with $P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)$ for all $s, t \ge 0$ and $P(X > t) > 0$ for all $t$. Prove that $X$ is exponential. Then compute $P(X > 30 \mid X > 10)$ for the workshop's gaps.
::: hint
With $g(t) = P(X > t)$ the property is $g(s + t) = g(s)\, g(t)$. Find $g$ on the rationals, then use that $g$ is monotone.
:::
::: solution
By induction $g(nt) = g(t)^n$, so $g(1) = g(1/q)^q$ and $g(p/q) = g(1)^{p/q}$ for integers $p, q > 0$. Write $g(1) = e^{-\lambda}$; $\lambda$ is finite since $g(1) > 0$, and positive since $g(1) = 1$ would give $g = 1$ on the rationals, $X = \infty$. So $g(r) = e^{-\lambda r}$ for every rational $r \ge 0$. A survival function is nonincreasing: for rationals $r_1 < t < r_2$, $e^{-\lambda r_2} \le g(t) \le e^{-\lambda r_1}$, and letting $r_1, r_2 \to t$ gives $g(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$.
For the workshop, $P(X > 30 \mid X > 10) = P(X > 20) = e^{-20/20.83} = 0.383$.
:::

:::: exercise The first of several clocks

::: hint
$P(M > t) = \prod_i P(X_i > t)$. For $P(M = X_i)$, condition on $X_i = x$ and integrate the chance that all others exceed $x$.
:::
::: solution
$P(M > t) = \prod_i e^{-\lambda_i t} = e^{-\Lambda t}$, and $P(M = X_i) = \int_0^\infty \lambda_i e^{-\lambda_i x} \prod_{j \ne i} e^{-\lambda_j x} \, dx = \lambda_i \int_0^\infty e^{-\Lambda x} dx = \lambda_i / \Lambda$.

Measure $L$ on its own: count the jobs inside ($+1$ at an arrival, $-1$ at a departure) and at each change add $N \times$ the time since the last one to an area. ```python
import itertools, random
import simpy

class Machine:
    def __init__(self, env):
        self.env, self.colour, self.queue = env, "blue", []
        self.up, self.working, self.busy = True, False, 0.0
        self.flows, self.work, self.wake = [], [], env.event()
        self.inside, self.area, self.last, self.log = 0, 0.0, 0.0, []
        self.proc = env.process(self.run())

    def count(self, step):                      # +1 arrival, -1 departure
        now = self.env.now                      # area under N(t), measured
        self.area += self.inside * (now - self.last)
        self.inside, self.last = self.inside + step, now
        self.log.append((now, self.inside))

    def poke(self):                             # an arrival or a repair
        if not self.wake.triggered:
            self.wake.succeed()

    def wait_until(self, ok):
        while not ok():
            self.wake = self.env.event()
            yield self.wake

    def run(self):
        while True:
            yield from self.wait_until(lambda: self.queue and self.up)
            colour, p, arrival = self.queue.pop(0)                # FIFO
            left = (10 if colour != self.colour else 0) + p       # colour change
            self.colour = colour
            self.work.append(left)
            while left > 0:
                yield from self.wait_until(lambda: self.up)
                start, self.working = self.env.now, True
                try:
                    yield self.env.timeout(left)
                    left = 0
                except simpy.Interrupt:
                    left -= self.env.now - start                  # keep the work done
                self.working = False
                self.busy += self.env.now - start
            self.flows.append(self.env.now - arrival)
            self.count(-1)

def arrivals(env, mc, orders):                  # orders: (colour, p, gap)
    for colour, p, gap in orders:
        yield env.timeout(gap)
        mc.queue.append((colour, p, env.now))
        mc.count(+1)
        mc.poke()

def breakdowns(env, mc, cycles):                # cycles: (up time, repair time)
    for up, repair in cycles:
        yield env.timeout(up)                   # calendar time, busy or idle
        mc.up = False
        if mc.working:
            mc.proc.interrupt()
        yield env.timeout(repair)
        mc.up = True
        mc.poke()

def run(orders, cycles, n):
    env = simpy.Environment()
    mc = Machine(env)
    env.process(arrivals(env, mc, orders))
    env.process(breakdowns(env, mc, cycles))
    while len(mc.flows) < n:                    # until the last job has left
        env.step()
    return env.now, mc

# (a) Part 2: gaps 0, 5, 7, 28; down from 25 to 33
T, mc = run([("blue", 20, 0), ("red", 10, 5), ("blue", 15, 7), ("red", 25, 28)], [(25, 8)], 4)
F = sum(mc.flows) / 4
print(mc.log)                                   # (time, N) at every change
print(T, mc.flows, f"L {mc.area / T:.3f} (n/T)F {4 / T * F:.3f} busy {mc.busy / T:.3f}")

# (b) 50,000 orders, mean gap 40; MTBF 120, repairs uniform on [10, 30]
n, lam, arr, brk = 50_000, 1 / 40, random.Random(1), random.Random(2)
orders = ((arr.choice(("blue", "red")), arr.choice((10, 15, 20, 25)), arr.expovariate(lam)) for _ in range(n))
cycles = ((brk.expovariate(1 / 120), brk.uniform(10, 30)) for _ in itertools.count())
T, mc = run(orders, cycles, n)
F, omega = sum(mc.flows) / n, sum(mc.work) / n
print(f"L {mc.area / T:.3f} (n/T)F {n / T * F:.3f} lambda F {lam * F:.3f}")
print(f"busy {mc.busy / T:.4f} (n/T)E[omega] {n / T * omega:.4f} lambda E[omega] {lam * omega:.4f} E[omega] {omega:.2f}")
```

L 1.459 (n/T)F 1.459 lambda F 1.455

(b) Each side has its own accumulator: the area under $N(t)$, the flow times, the timed work segments, the planned work. On a window that starts and ends empty the sides agree to every digit, whatever the rule; a disagreement would reveal a bookkeeping error (a lost job, a double-counted segment). ## The chapter on one page {#summary}

::: steps
- **Event-driven simulation**: the clock jumps from event to event; the simulator and its controller interface, the course's synthetic digital twin, replay the same day under every method.
- **Poisson arrivals**: exponential gaps, $1/\lambda = 20.83$ min; memoryless; merging adds rates, splitting multiplies them.
- **Load and availability**: $\rho = \lambda E[W_{f(j)}]/m = 0.60$ for fastest routes; $A = 120/140 = 0.857$.
- **Little's law**: $L = \lambda \bar F$, exact on a window that starts and ends empty; applied to the machines, the busy fraction $\lambda E[\omega]/m$ with the work actually done, 0.82 for FIFO.
- **SimPy and the repository**: processes, timeouts, interrupts; `make_scenario` draws the inputs, `Shop` plays them, the controller decides.
:::

### The vocabulary, in one table

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Discrete-event simulation | A model whose state changes only at events; the clock jumps from one to the next. |
| Event list | Priority queue of future events keyed by time. |
| Digital twin (here) | The simulator plus its controller interface; synthetic, not synchronised with a plant. |
| Poisson process | Independent exponential gaps of mean $1/\lambda$. |
| Load $\rho$, busy fraction $u$ | $\lambda E[W_{f(j)}]/m$ with fastest-route work; $\lambda E[\omega]/m$ with the work actually done. |
| MTBF, MTTR, availability $A$ | Mean up time, mean repair time, long-run up fraction MTBF/(MTBF + MTTR). |
| Little's law | $L = \lambda \bar F$: work in progress equals arrival rate times mean flow time. |
| Controller interface | `route`, `sequence`, `on_event`: where decisions enter. |

**Scope.** This is a *terminating* simulation: a day starts empty and ends when the 60th job leaves, so runs are compared day against day, not with a steady state. Breakdowns ignore age and workload, repairs need no resource, and every distribution was chosen, not fitted. Law [[ref:1]] covers what a real study would add.

The flexible job shop

One paint machine was a sequencing problem: choose an order. A workshop of five machines adds a second decision: which machine does each step of each order. This chapter writes that flexible job shop down as constraints, then as Roy and Sussmann's disjunctive graph, in which a schedule is a choice of arrows: a choice with a cycle is impossible, one without gives the earliest timetable, whose length is the longest path. You will draw the graph of a small shop, prove both facts, and count the choices the course's workshop leaves.

Part 1

A workshop in plain words

The paint machine of chapters A1 and A2 had one decision: the order of the orders. In a workshop every order goes through several machines, one step after another, and a step can often be done on more than one machine: step 0 of a family-0 order runs on machine 1 in 6 minutes or on machine 2 in 10. So the workshop decides two things for every step:

  • where: which of the eligible machines (those able to do the step) will do it, the assignment (routing). Machine 1 is faster, but if it has a long queue, machine 2 may finish sooner;
  • when: in which order each machine works through what it receives: the paint-machine question on five machines at once, except that a step waits for the previous step of its order to end elsewhere, and for the ride.

The machines are coupled through the orders: putting an order first on machine 1 makes it arrive early at machine 2, which changes what machine 2 should do.

A small instance shows everything. Three jobs, three machines, each job visiting each machine once in its own order:

Job Step 0 Step 1 Step 2 Total
J0 M0, 2 min M1, 7 min M2, 7 min 16
J1 M1, 7 min M2, 4 min M0, 5 min 16
J2 M2, 2 min M0, 6 min M1, 2 min 10

Machine M1 has 7 + 7 + 2 = 16 minutes of work, the others 13 each, and no job takes longer than 16 minutes, so no schedule finishes before minute 16. The best finishes at 21, and exactly one way of ordering the machines achieves it: find it in the hero.

What is a flexible job shop?

In plain words: each order follows its own route of steps, and a step may be done on any of a few machines, at a machine-dependent speed. Deciding means choosing a machine for every step and an order for every machine.

More precisely: a set of jobs \mathcal J; job j consists of operations (j, 0), \dots, (j, K_j - 1) to be done in this order; operation (j,k) can be processed by any machine of a set \mathcal M_{jk} \subseteq \mathcal M, taking p_{jkm} minutes on machine m, without interruption; a machine does one operation at a time.

Operation
One step of one job, (j, k): what the code calls (job, op).
Route, eligible machines
The route of a job is its sequence of operations; \mathcal M_{jk}, the eligible machines of (j, k) (1 to 3 in the workshop), depend only on the family, and ws.alternatives(f, k) returns them with their times.
Assignment (routing) \mu
One machine \mu(j, k) \in \mathcal M_{jk} for each operation.
Sequence \sigma_m
The order in which machine m processes the operations assigned to it, as \sigma was the order of the paint machine.
Classical job shop
The special case |\mathcal M_{jk}| = 1: only the sequences are to be chosen. The 3 × 3 instance is one.
Flow shop
The special case where every job visits the machines in the same order.
Part 2

The constraints, written as mathematics

A schedule gives each operation a machine \mu(j, k), a start S_{jk} and an end E_{jk}. Its measures are those of chapter A1: C_j = E_{j,K_j-1}, T_j = \max(0, C_j - d_j), C_{\max} = \max_j C_j. Five kinds of constraints make it valid.

1. Assignment and duration. Every operation goes to exactly one eligible machine and runs there without interruption. With binary variables x_{jkm} = 1 when (j,k) runs on m,

\sum_{m \in \mathcal M_{jk}} x_{jkm} = 1, \qquad E_{jk} = S_{jk} + \sum_{m \in \mathcal M_{jk}} p_{jkm}\, x_{jkm}, \qquad x_{jkm} \in \{0, 1\}.

So \mu(j, k) is the machine with x_{jkm} = 1, and E_{jk} = S_{jk} + p_{jk\mu(j,k)}. Breakdowns are left to the simulator of chapter B1.

2. Release and the first move. Job j arrives at the load station at its release time r_j, and a cart carries it to its first machine. With \operatorname{st}(m) = m + 1 the station of machine m (station 0 is the load station) and \tau(a, b) the travel time between stations (notation), the job reaches its first machine at

\alpha_{j0} = r_j + \tau\big(0, \operatorname{st}(\mu(j, 0))\big), \qquad S_{j0} \ge \alpha_{j0},

plus a changeover if one is needed (constraint 5).

3. Precedence with transport. Step k + 1 cannot start before step k has ended and the job has been carried to the next machine:

\alpha_{j,k+1} = E_{jk} + \tau\big(\operatorname{st}(\mu(j,k)), \operatorname{st}(\mu(j,k+1))\big), \qquad S_{j,k+1} \ge \alpha_{j,k+1}.

\alpha_{jk} is the arrival of job j at the machine of (j,k); \tau(a, a) = 0. The ride depends on both machines chosen, so transport couples consecutive assignments; chapter C1 writes it as the transport lag \ell(m', m).

4. Capacity. A machine processes one operation at a time. For any two operations u and v of different jobs assigned to the same machine, one ends before the other starts:

\mu(u) = \mu(v) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad E_u \le S_v \ \ \text{or} \ \ E_v \le S_u .

The word "or" is the whole difficulty of scheduling. Constraints 1 to 3 are linear once the assignment is fixed; constraint 4 is a disjunctive constraint, a logical "or" of linear inequalities, one per pair on a machine (chapter C1 writes it for a solver). Two operations of one job need none: precedence keeps them apart.

5. Sequence-dependent changeovers. When a machine passes from a job of family f to one of family g it is re-equipped for s_m(f, g) minutes, zero if g = f, and the changeover starts only once the machine is free and the job has arrived (the convention of the repository's simulator and CP-SAT model). If v directly follows u on machine m,

S_v \ge \max(E_u, \alpha_v) + s_m\big(f(u), f(v)\big),

with f(u) the family of the job of u; for the first operation on a machine, S_v \ge \alpha_v. The changeover belongs to a pair of consecutive operations, so it cannot be folded into p_{jkm}.

A worked case, with the workshop's numbers. A family-0 job is released at r_j = 100; its step 0 goes to machine 1, 6 minutes. The ride from the load station is \tau(0, \operatorname{st}(1)) = 2, so \alpha = 102. Machine 1 last ran family 2 and is free from minute 98; s_1(2, 0) = 10. Then S = \max(98, 102) + 10 = 112 and E = 118: the machine idles from 98 to 102. Had it been busy until 105, the job would wait instead: S = 115, E = 121.

The objective. The makespan, the total tardiness and the planner's 10 \sum_j T_j + \sum_j C_j are regular measures (chapter A1): non-decreasing in every C_j. Part 4 turns this into a theorem: the decisions are the assignment and the sequences; the times follow.

Part 3

The disjunctive graph

Strip the problem to its core: the classical job shop with makespan, Jm \mid\mid C_{\max} (chapter A1): one machine per operation, no changeovers, no transport, all jobs present at time 0. Roy and Sussmann [1] drew it as a graph, still how the job shop is taught.

What is a disjunctive graph?

In plain words: a box per step; a solid arrow means "must finish first"; a dashed line between two boxes on the same machine means "one of them first, you choose".

More precisely: G = (V, C, D), with

  • nodes V: one per operation, plus two dummy nodes, 0 ("the start of the day") and \ast ("everything is done");
  • conjunctive arcs C: the routes, (j, k) \to (j, k + 1), plus 0 \to (j, 0) and (j, K_j - 1) \to \ast, fixed by the data;
  • disjunctive pairs D: for every machine, a pair \{u, v\} for every two operations of different jobs on it, an arc whose direction is not decided yet.
Arc weight w(u, v)
Here p_u, the processing time of the tail, and 0 for the arcs leaving 0. The arc reads "v cannot start before u has ended": S_v \ge S_u + w(u, v).

Two jobs on two machines with opposite routes give four boxes, two solid arrows and one dashed pair per machine (exercise 1). In the hero each machine has three operations, one per job, hence \binom{3}{2} = 3 pairs per machine and |D| = 9.

What is a selection?

In plain words: for each pair of operations that compete for a machine, a decision of which goes first. Making all these decisions is the scheduling.

More precisely: a selection \mathcal S picks one direction, u \to v or v \to u, for some of the pairs of D; it is complete when every pair is oriented. It turns G into the directed graph G(\mathcal S) = (V, C \cup \mathcal S).

Acyclic
With no directed cycle: no way to follow arrows and come back to the start.
Path length
The sum of the weights of its arcs; \operatorname{len}(u, v) is the length of a longest path from u to v in G(\mathcal S).
Critical path
A longest path from 0 to \ast; its operations run back to back.

Counting selections. The 9 pairs can be oriented in 2^9 = 512 ways. Many are absurd before any timing: on M0, J0.0 \to J1.2, J1.2 \to J2.1 and J2.1 \to J0.0 go round in a circle. A selection makes sense on one machine only if it is a total order, a sequence \sigma_m, and n_m operations have n_m! of them, so at most \prod_m n_m! = 3!^3 = 216 selections survive the machines one at a time. The routes remove more: only 64 of those are acyclic, as the numbers script finds by trying them all.

A cycle across machines. Two orientations can be enough. Put J1.2 before J0.0 on M0, and J0.1 before J1.0 on M1:

J0.0 \to J0.1 \to J1.0 \to J1.1 \to J1.2 \to J0.0.

Each job waits for the other. The numbers script counts 6 such cycle-closing pairs of orientations; the hero shows a cycle in red as soon as you create one.

Part 4

Cycle means impossible, longest path means makespan

Two facts make the graph useful, both due to Roy and Sussmann [1] and given in Pinedo's book [4].

Theorem. Let \mathcal S be a complete selection of a job shop whose processing times are all positive.

  1. If G(\mathcal S) has a directed cycle, no schedule processes the operations in the orders \mathcal S prescribes.
  2. If G(\mathcal S) is acyclic, S_v = \operatorname{len}(0, v) for every operation v is a valid schedule; every schedule that follows \mathcal S has S_v \ge \operatorname{len}(0, v); and its makespan is \operatorname{len}(0, \ast).

Proof of 1. A schedule follows \mathcal S when every arc u \to v of G(\mathcal S) holds as S_v \ge S_u + w(u, v). Suppose a cycle u_1 \to u_2 \to \dots \to u_q \to u_1 and write the inequality of each of its arcs,

S_{u_2} \ge S_{u_1} + p_{u_1}, \quad S_{u_3} \ge S_{u_2} + p_{u_2}, \quad \dots, \quad S_{u_1} \ge S_{u_q} + p_{u_q}.

Adding them, every S_{u_i} appears once on each side and cancels, leaving 0 \ge p_{u_1} + \dots + p_{u_q}. The right side is positive, so no start times satisfy all the inequalities. \square

Proof of 2. \operatorname{len}(0, v) is well defined: an acyclic graph has finitely many paths from 0 to v, and at least one, along v's route.

Lower bound. For a schedule that follows \mathcal S, the arc inequalities along any path 0 = v_0 \to \dots \to v_q = v add up to S_v \ge its length, hence S_v \ge \operatorname{len}(0, v).

Feasible. For an arc u \to v, a longest path to u followed by the arc is a path to v, so \operatorname{len}(0, v) \ge \operatorname{len}(0, u) + w(u, v): S_v = \operatorname{len}(0, v) satisfies every arc inequality, so precedence holds. Take two operations u, v on one machine. If they belong to different jobs, their pair is oriented, say u \to v, and S_v \ge S_u + p_u. If they belong to the same job, u earlier, the route arcs form a path from u to v whose first arc weighs p_u, so again S_v \ge S_u + p_u. Either way one ends before the other starts.

Makespan. The arcs into \ast come from the last operation of each job with weight p, so \operatorname{len}(0, \ast) = \max_j \big(\operatorname{len}(0, (j, K_j - 1)) + p_{j, K_j - 1}\big) = C_{\max}. \square

What the theorem buys.

Every valid schedule comes from an acyclic selection. Orient each pair by the order of the start times on its machine. Every arc then goes to an operation that starts strictly later (S_v \ge S_u + p_u > S_u), and a walk on which the start strictly increases cannot return to where it began.

The longest-path schedule is the best for its selection. The left-shifted schedule (LS) of A1 is exactly this longest path, the semi-active schedule, so for a regular measure nothing following the same selection does better. Hence

\min_{\text{schedules}} C_{\max} \;=\; \min_{\mathcal S \text{ complete and acyclic}} \; \operatorname{len}_{G(\mathcal S)}(0, \ast),

and likewise for any regular measure: the search is over orders, a finite set; the times are computed.

Only the critical path matters. Reversing a pair off a critical path leaves that path in the graph, so to shorten a schedule you must reverse an arc on it: the moves of local search for the job shop [4].

Computing it. Visit the nodes in a topological order (every arc goes forward) and set each start to the largest "start of a predecessor plus the arc's weight". Nodes never visited contain a cycle (each has an unvisited predecessor; walk backwards until one repeats). This pass, Kahn's procedure, is linear in nodes plus arcs; the hero and the numbers script use it.

The instance, solved by hand. The loads give a lower bound of 16, yet the optimum is 21. Look at the one pair on M1 between J0.1 and J1.0, both 7 minutes. Either way round, the graph contains a long path:

  • if J1.0 goes first, 0 \to J1.0 \to J0.1 \to J0.2 \to \ast has length 21 (7 + 7 + 7);
  • if J0.1 goes first, 0 \to J0.0 \to J0.1 \to J1.0 \to J1.1 \to J1.2 \to \ast has length 25 (2 + 7 + 7 + 4 + 5).

So every acyclic selection has C_{\max} \ge 21, and 21 is reached: with the sequences M0: J0.0, J2.1, J1.2; M1: J1.0, J0.1, J2.2; M2: J2.0, J1.1, J0.2, the longest-path schedule is

Machine First Second Third
M0 J0.0 [0, 2) J2.1 [2, 8) J1.2 [11, 16)
M1 J1.0 [0, 7) J0.1 [7, 14) J2.2 [14, 16)
M2 J2.0 [0, 2) J1.1 [7, 11) J0.2 [14, 21)

with critical path J1.0 → J0.1 → J0.2. The two arguments prove it optimal without enumeration; enumeration adds that it is unique: the 64 acyclic selections range from 21 to 42, with 21 once and 24 six times. Press J0, J1, J2 first in the hero (33 minutes), then flip pairs on its critical path and watch the length drop.

How hard is it? NP-hard already for three machines visited in the same order (F3 \mid\mid C_{\max}); this chapter only cites the result, from the survey of Graham, Lawler, Lenstra and Rinnooy Kan [3]. No known algorithm avoids an exponential search in the worst case, hence chapter C2 on searching cleverly.

Part 5

Assignment first: the flexible job shop

In the course's workshop an operation has 1 to 3 eligible machines. Brandimarte [2] was among the first to treat this flexible job shop, splitting it into routing and the job shop that results, both attacked by tabu search (a local search that forbids undoing recent moves).

Formally, a schedule is a pair (\mu, \mathcal S): an assignment (routing), one machine \mu(j, k) \in \mathcal M_{jk} per operation, and a selection of the job shop it creates. Fixing \mu fixes the processing times p_{jk\mu(j,k)} and decides which pairs exist: two operations compete only if they were sent to the same machine. The graph G_\mu keeps its nodes and routes, but its weights and pairs change with the assignment, and

\min_{(\mu, \mathcal S)} C_{\max} \;=\; \min_{\mu} \ \ \min_{\mathcal S \text{ acyclic for } \mu} \operatorname{len}_{G_\mu(\mathcal S)}(0, \ast).

There are \prod_{j,k} |\mathcal M_{jk}| assignments, each with its own selections. The flexible job shop contains the classical one (every |\mathcal M_{jk}| = 1), so it is at least as hard.

The instance, made flexible. Three operations get a second machine: J0.1 and J1.0 can also run on M2 in 6 minutes instead of 7 on M1, and J2.2 on M0 in 3 minutes instead of 2 on M1. The complete data, for reuse in chapter C1; each cell lists the eligible machines with their minutes:

J1 M1: 7 or M2: 6 M2: 4 M0: 5
J2 M2: 2 M0: 6 M1: 2 or M0: 3

Sending each operation to its fastest machine is tied for the worst choice (with M2, M2, M0): it saves 2 minutes of processing and piles five operations, 25 minutes of work, on M2. Moving a single operation balances the loads to 13, 9 and 19, and the best schedule reaches the load bound, 19 minutes. Four of the 8 assignments reach 19; none does better (exercise 3 proves it by hand). Two lessons carry over: assignment and sequencing interact, so neither can be decided well alone; and how busy a machine is matters more than how fast it is. This is why the repository's rules route a job to the machine with the earliest expected finish, which counts the queue, rather than to the fastest one, chapter B2.

Part 6

Where changeovers and transport enter the graph

The course's workshop adds release times, transport and changeovers. All three become arc weights, and the theorem survives, because its proofs used only one thing: each arc is an inequality "start of the head \ge start of the tail + a positive weight". With an assignment fixed:

  • the arc 0 \to (j, 0) weighs r_j + \tau(0, \operatorname{st}(\mu(j, 0))), the release plus the first ride;
  • the route arc (j, k) \to (j, k+1) weighs p_{jk\mu(j,k)} + \tau\big(\operatorname{st}(\mu(j,k)), \operatorname{st}(\mu(j,k+1))\big): processing, then the ride;
  • the machine arc u \to v on m weighs p_u + s_m(f(u), f(v)): finish u, then re-equip;
  • since the changeover also waits for the job (constraint 5), the arc into v along its route gains s_m(f(u'), f(v)), where u' is the operation directly before v on its machine (nothing if v is first). Unlike the others, this weight depends on the selection.

The CP-SAT planner of chapter C1 writes the ride this way, plus an allowance \delta = 2 min for an empty cart to come. The two carts themselves are a capacity that this model, like the planner, leaves out; the simulator of chapter B1 moves them.

In the hero's toy each job is its own family, so the changeover box means 1 minute whenever a machine switches job. Toggle the boxes: with 1-minute rides the optimum becomes 23, with 1-minute changeovers 23 too, with both 25; here the same selection stays optimal each time, which need not happen in general.

One caution. The graph has an arc for every ordered pair on a machine, but a changeover happens only between directly consecutive operations. If u, v, w run in this order, the arc u \to w charges a changeover s_m(f(u), f(w)) that never happens. It is harmless when the chain through v already implies it, that is when s_m(f(u), f(w)) \le s_m(f(u), f(v)) + p_v + s_m(f(v), f(w)): the triangle inequality of chapter A2 with p_v added on the right. The workshop breaks the plain inequality in 4 places, as A2 counts, but always satisfies this weaker one, since every changeover is at most 10 min \le 3 + 6 + 3: the smallest changeover twice plus the shortest operation. Rather than rely on it, the repository's model uses a circuit that knows who directly follows whom, chapter C1.

How many choices a day leaves. Test day 0 has 60 jobs, 240 operations. The number of assignments is the product of the per-job choices, about 10^{74}. Fix just one assignment, the fastest machine for every operation, and the five machines can still be sequenced in \prod_m n_m! \approx 10^{320} ways. Enumeration is hopeless: a method must decide locally, as the rules of chapter B2 do, or search with proofs, as CP-SAT does. The widget counts for the first n jobs of the day.

Going further: the detailed count

Assignments. On test day 0 the four families have 15, 13, 15 and 17 jobs, so

\prod_{j} \ \prod_{k} |\mathcal M_{jk}| \;=\; 24^{15} \cdot 8^{13} \cdot 36^{15} \cdot 12^{17} \;\approx\; 10^{74.1},

between 10^{70.0} and 10^{76.9} over the 20 test days.

Orders. With every operation on its fastest machine (ties, which occur twice, to the lower number), the machines receive 45, 75, 28, 15 and 77 operations: the fastest assignment is very unbalanced, the lesson of Part 5 at scale. Their orders number \prod_m n_m! \approx 10^{320.2}. Of the \sum_m \binom{n_m}{2} = 7{,}174 pairs sharing a machine, 32 belong to one job and are already ordered by its route; they are disjoint, and each halves the count, so \prod_m n_m! / 2^{32} \approx 10^{310.6} orders respect the routes. The other 7,142 pairs are disjunctive, with 2^{7142} \approx 10^{2150} orientations.

What the numbers do not say. They count a static problem with all 60 jobs present at once; in the real shop release times rule out most of these orders, and the planner of chapter C3 plans at most 25 jobs. Yet the first 25 jobs of test day 0 still leave 10^{31.5} assignments and 10^{93.0} route-respecting orders, and ten jobs 10^{24.0} orders.

Part 8

The code behind it

The workshop is a frozen data class (an immutable record): the fixed part of the shop, identical in every run.

jobshop/scenario.py · Workshop

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Workshop:
    n_machines: int
    n_agvs: int
    # routings[family][op] = {machine: processing time}; an operation has 1 to 3 eligible machines
    routings: tuple[tuple[dict[int, int], ...], ...]
    # setup[machine][previous family][next family]; zero on the diagonal
    setup: tuple[tuple[tuple[int, ...], ...], ...]
    # travel[a][b] between stations, handling included; zero on the diagonal
    travel: tuple[tuple[int, ...], ...]

    @property
    def n_families(self) -> int:
        return len(self.routings)

    @staticmethod
    def station(machine: int) -> int:
        return machine + 1

    def n_ops(self, family: int) -> int:
        return len(self.routings[family])

    def alternatives(self, family: int, op: int) -> dict[int, int]:
        return self.routings[family][op]

    def proc(self, family: int, op: int, machine: int) -> int:
        return self.routings[family][op][machine]

    def setup_time(self, machine: int, previous_family: int | None, family: int) -> int:
        """Changeover time on a machine. A machine that has not run anything yet needs none."""
        if previous_family is None:
            return 0
        return self.setup[machine][previous_family][family]

    def min_remaining_work(self, family: int, op: int) -> int:
        """Sum of the shortest processing times from operation `op` to the end of the routing."""
        return sum(min(alt.values()) for alt in self.routings[family][op:])

routings[f][k], the eligible machines, is a dictionary {machine: minutes}: keys \mathcal M_{fk}, values p_{fkm}, indexed by family f = f(j). setup_time is s_m(f, g), zero for a machine that has run nothing (previous_family=None). station(m) is \operatorname{st}(m); travel is \tau. min_remaining_work(f, 0) is W_f.

The workshop itself is drawn once from a seeded generator.

    setup = tuple(
        tuple(
            tuple(0 if f == g else int(rng.integers(3, 11)) for g in range(n_families))
            for f in range(n_families)
        )
        for _ in range(n_machines)
    )

    # layout: load station at the origin, machines on distinct cells of a 4 x 3 grid;
    # travel time = 1 min of handling + half the Manhattan distance, rounded up
    cells = [(x, y) for x in range(1, 5) for y in range(3)]
    picked = rng.choice(len(cells), size=n_machines, replace=False)
    pos = [(0, 1)] + [cells[int(i)] for i in picked]
    travel = tuple(
        tuple(0 if a == b else 1 + (abs(pa[0] - pb[0]) + abs(pa[1] - pb[1]) + 1) // 2 for b, pb in enumerate(pos))
        for a, pa in enumerate(pos)
    )
    return Workshop(n_machines, n_agvs, tuple(routings), setup, travel)

The stream [seed, 0] serves nothing else, so the workshop never changes when the scenarios do. Per family: 3 or 4 operations (all got 4); per operation, the number of eligible machines from [1, 2, 2, 3] and a base time from 8 to 24 minutes, scaled per machine by a factor in [0.8, 1.3]. Changeovers are drawn independently per machine and ordered pair of families, hence asymmetric. A move costs 1 minute plus half the Manhattan distance on a 4 × 3 grid, rounded up.

Part 9

Common pitfalls

  1. "A job shop is several single machines." The routes couple the machines: an order on one moves arrivals on the others. The cycle of Part 3 involves two machines and is invisible on either alone.
  2. "There are 2^{|D|} schedules." Most selections are cyclic: 512, then 216 total orders per machine, then 64 acyclic on the small instance.
  3. "The start times are decisions." For a regular measure they are not: once the assignment and the sequences are fixed, the earliest schedule is a longest-path computation, and it is the best for those sequences.
  4. "The fastest machine is the best machine." On the flexible instance it gives 25 minutes against 19; on the workshop it sends 77 operations to one machine and 15 to another.
  5. "A changeover is part of the processing time." It depends on which job came directly before, so it lives on the arcs between operations, not in the nodes.
  6. "The longest path of a partial selection is the makespan." It is a lower bound: orienting more pairs adds arcs and can only lengthen paths. The hero writes "≥" until all nine pairs are ordered; the solvers of chapter C2 start from such bounds.
Part 10

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question before opening it.

1. What are the two decisions of a flexible job shop, and which one does a classical job shop lack?

The assignment (routing), a machine \mu(j, k) \in \mathcal M_{jk} for each operation, and the sequence on each machine. A classical job shop has |\mathcal M_{jk}| = 1: the assignment is given.

2. Why is the capacity constraint not a linear inequality?

It says "u ends before v starts, or v ends before u starts". The feasible (S_u, S_v) form two half-planes whose union is not convex ((0, p_u) and (p_v, 0) are feasible, their midpoint is not), while linear inequalities describe convex sets. Orienting the pair makes it linear.

3. In the disjunctive graph, what is a node, a conjunctive arc and a disjunctive pair?

An operation (plus 0 and \ast); a fixed precedence inside a job; two operations of different jobs on one machine, direction to choose.

4. Where does the proof that a cycle is infeasible use positive processing times?

At the last step: summing around the cycle gives 0 \ge \sum p, a contradiction only if the sum is positive. With zero durations a cycle would be satisfiable, all its operations starting at the same instant.

5. Why does an acyclic complete selection never make two operations overlap on a machine?

Two operations of different jobs on it form a pair, oriented by the selection, and the arc gives S_v \ge S_u + p_u. Two of the same job are joined by a path of route arcs, which does the same.

6. Why a longest path, not a shortest one?

Each arc into v is a lower bound S_v \ge S_u + w; all must hold, so the start is the largest of them, and by induction the largest over all paths from 0.

7. How does flexibility change the graph?

Nodes and routes stay; weights become the times on the chosen machines; the pairs are those of operations sent to the same machine, so another assignment draws other pairs.

8. Why can the optimum of the 3 × 3 instance (21) exceed the load bound (16)?

The load bound ignores the routes. The pair J0.1–J1.0 on M1 forces a path of 21 one way and 25 the other, through the routes of J0 and J1: a machine can be idle because its next job is still elsewhere.

Part 11

Exercises

The code runs with the repository installed (or from the course zip); the snippets assume import itertools, math and from jobshop.scenario import make_workshop, make_scenario.

  1. Two jobs, two machines, opposite routes. J0 goes M0 (3 min) then M1 (2 min); J1 goes M1 (4 min) then M0 (1 min). Draw the graph, compute the makespan of each of the three acyclic selections by longest paths, and say which is optimal and why.

    Hint

    One pair per machine: \{J0.0, J1.1\} on M0 and \{J0.1, J1.0\} on M1. The cycle needs J1 to finish on M0 before J0 starts there, while J0 must finish on M1 before J1 starts there.

    Solution

    Write the selections as (M0 arc, M1 arc).

    • J0.0 \to J1.1, J1.0 \to J0.1: J0.0 and J1.0 start at 0; J0.1 at \max(3, 4) = 4, ending at 6; J1.1 at \max(4, 3) = 4, ending at 5. Makespan 6, the optimum: both machines start at once.
    • J0.0 \to J1.1, J0.1 \to J1.0: J0.0 [0, 3), J0.1 [3, 5), J1.0 [5, 9), J1.1 [9, 10). Makespan 10: J1 waits for all of J0.
    • J1.1 \to J0.0, J1.0 \to J0.1: symmetric, J1 first: J1.0 [0, 4), J1.1 [4, 5), J0.0 [5, 8), J0.1 [8, 10). Makespan 10.
    • J1.1 \to J0.0, J0.1 \to J1.0: the cycle J0.0 \to J0.1 \to J1.0 \to J1.1 \to J0.0. Impossible.

    The numbers script prints the same four lines.

  2. A single priority never makes a cycle. Rank the jobs once and for all, and on every machine let the higher-ranked job go first. Prove that this selection is acyclic, for any job shop. What does it give on the 3 × 3 instance with the ranking J0, J1, J2?

    Hint

    Label each node by its job's rank and its step number. Follow an arc: what can happen to the pair (rank, step)?

    Solution

    Give operation (j, k) the pair (\text{rank}(j), k), compared lexicographically. A route arc keeps the rank and increases the step; a machine arc goes to a job of strictly larger rank. Either way the pair strictly increases, so it cannot return to its start around a cycle: dispatching by a fixed priority always produces a schedule.

    On the instance, J0, J1, J2 gives 33 minutes and the reverse ranking 42, tied for the worst (3 selections reach 42). With makespan_of and on from exercise 5:

    print(makespan_of([sorted(x) for x in on]))                  # 33
print(makespan_of([sorted(x, reverse=True) for x in on]))    # 42

  3. Prove that 19 is optimal for the flexible instance. In the flexible instance of Part 5, J0.1 and J1.0 may each run on M1 (7 min) or M2 (6 min), and J2.2 on M1 (2 min) or M0 (3 min). Prove, without enumeration, that no assignment and order gives a makespan below 19, and that 19 is reached.

    Hint

    Split the 8 assignments by where J0.1 and J1.0 go. If both stay on M1, reuse the argument of Part 4. Otherwise compute the load of M2.

    Solution

    Both on M1. The pair J0.1–J1.0 on M1 has the times of the classical instance, so either 0 \to J1.0 \to J0.1 \to J0.2 \to \ast of length 21 or 0 \to J0.0 \to J0.1 \to J1.0 \to J1.1 \to J1.2 \to \ast of length 25 is in the graph: C_{\max} \ge 21.

    Both on M2. M2 carries J0.1 (6), J0.2 (7), J1.0 (6), J1.1 (4) and J2.0 (2): 25 minutes, so C_{\max} \ge 25.

    Exactly one on M2. M2 carries the moved operation (6) plus J0.2, J1.1 and J2.0 (7 + 4 + 2): 19 minutes, so C_{\max} \ge 19.

    So 19 bounds every assignment from below, and J0.1 on M1, J1.0 on M2, J2.2 on M1 reaches it (widget of Part 5).

  4. Count the workshop's choices. Using the repository, compute the assignment choices of each family, the logarithm of their product over the 60 jobs of test day 0, and, for the fastest-machine assignment, the operations per machine, the disjunctive pairs (different jobs only) and the machine orders that respect the routes.

  5. Solve the 3 × 3 instance by brute force. Write a function that, for each machine order (one permutation per machine), builds the arcs, computes the longest-path schedule or detects a cycle, and returns the makespan. Enumerate all 3!^3 machine orders of the instance, count the acyclic ones and find the optimum. Then add 1 minute of transport to every route arc and to the source arcs and solve again.

    Hint

    Kahn's procedure: keep the number of unvisited predecessors of each node; start from the nodes with none; when a node is visited, update its successors' starts with "start + weight" and decrease their counts. Nodes never visited mean a cycle.

    Solution 
    from collections import Counter
TOY = [[(0, 2), (1, 7), (2, 7)], [(1, 7), (2, 4), (0, 5)], [(2, 2), (0, 6), (1, 2)]]

def makespan_of(orders, tau=0):
    ops = [(j, k) for j in range(3) for k in range(3)]
    succ = {o: [] for o in ops}
    for j in range(3):
        for k in range(2):
            succ[(j, k)].append(((j, k + 1), TOY[j][k][1] + tau))
    for seq in orders:                      # consecutive arcs suffice for longest paths
        for u, v in zip(seq, seq[1:]):
            succ[u].append((v, TOY[u[0]][u[1]][1]))
    indeg = Counter(v for o in ops for v, _ in succ[o])
    start = {o: tau if o[1] == 0 else 0 for o in ops}
    queue = [o for o in ops if indeg[o] == 0]
    for u in queue:
        for v, w in succ[u]:
            start[v] = max(start[v], start[u] + w)
            indeg[v] -= 1
            if indeg[v] == 0:
                queue.append(v)
    if len(queue) < len(ops):
        return None                          # a cycle
    return max(start[(j, 2)] + TOY[j][2][1] for j in range(3))

on = [[(j, k) for j in range(3) for k in range(3) if TOY[j][k][0] == m] for m in range(3)]
for tau in (0, 1):
    vals = [makespan_of(p, tau) for p in itertools.product(*[itertools.permutations(x) for x in on])]
    ok = [v for v in vals if v is not None]
    print(tau, len(vals), len(ok), min(ok))      # 0 216 64 21   then   1 216 64 23

    The results match the hero.

Part 12

The chapter on one page

  1. The problem: choose an assignment (routing) \mu(j, k) \in \mathcal M_{jk}, an eligible machine for every operation, and a sequence on every machine.
  2. The constraints: assignment, release, precedence with transport, capacity as a disjunction, changeovers once the machine is free and the job there.
  3. The graph (Roy and Sussmann): routes as fixed arcs, machine conflicts as pairs to orient; on the 3 × 3 instance 512 selections, 216 that order each machine, 64 acyclic.
  4. The theorem: a cycle means no schedule; an acyclic complete selection gives the semi-active schedule S_v = \operatorname{len}(0, v), with C_{\max} = \operatorname{len}(0, \ast); on the instance, 21, unique.
  5. Flexibility (Brandimarte): the assignment changes weights and pairs; best 19, fastest machines 25.
  6. Extras: release and transport on route arcs, changeovers on machine arcs.
  7. The workshop: about 10^{74} assignments for a day; on the fastest machines alone, about 10^{320} machine orders.

The vocabulary, in one table

Word Meaning
Route, eligible machines \mathcal M_{jk} A job's sequence of operations; the machines able to do operation (j, k).
Flexible / classical job shop Several eligible machines per operation / exactly one.
Assignment (routing) \mu(j, k) One eligible machine per operation.
Sequence \sigma_m The order of the operations on machine m.
Arrival \alpha_{jk} When the job reaches the machine of step k: release or previous end, plus the ride.
Disjunctive graph Operations, conjunctive arcs (routes), disjunctive pairs (machine conflicts).
Selection \mathcal S An orientation of the pairs; complete when all are oriented.
Acyclic No directed cycle; for a complete selection, the same as "a schedule exists".
Semi-active schedule Every operation as early as its orders allow; the longest-path schedule.
Critical path A longest path from 0 to \ast, of length \operatorname{len}(0, \ast) = C_{\max}.

Scope. A static, deterministic problem: all jobs known, no breakdowns, carts not modelled. Chapter B1 simulates the dynamic shop, B2 builds selections on the fly with rules, C1 writes this model for a solver, C3 re-solves it at every event.

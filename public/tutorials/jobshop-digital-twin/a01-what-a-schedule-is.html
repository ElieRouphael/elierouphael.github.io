Which Job Should the Machine Do Next? · Part A, Scheduling problems · Chapter A1 of 3

What a schedule is

A workshop runs on one decision taken thousands of times a day: this machine is free, which job next? Answering it needs two precise notions: what a schedule is, and how one schedule beats another. This chapter defines both, from one paint machine with four orders to the course's simulated workshop. You will compute every measure by hand, see why "regular" measures reduce the search to orders, write the course's problem in Graham's three-field notation, and see why the benchmark judges methods on tardiness, not makespan.

Part 1

One machine, four orders

A paint shop has one machine. It is set up for blue, and a colour change takes 10 minutes of cleaning. Four orders wait at minute 0, listed in the order they came in:

Order Colour Painting time (min) Due at minute
A blue 20 30
B red 10 25
C blue 15 70
D red 25 60

The shop decides only the order in which they are painted. Take first come, first served (FIFO): A, B, C, D. The machine paints A from 0 to 20. B is red: cleaning 20 to 30, painting 30 to 40. C is blue: cleaning 40 to 50, painting 50 to 65. D is red: cleaning 65 to 75, painting 75 to 100.

That timetable is a schedule; its picture, one bar per task along a time axis, is a Gantt chart, the top row of the hero. The rows below follow each order from arrival (grey) through painting (coloured), with a tick at its due time and red where it is still unfinished after it.

How good is it? A is done at 20, ten minutes early. B is done at 40, due at 25: fifteen minutes late. C is done at 65, five minutes early. D is done at 100, due at 60: forty minutes late. In total the orders are 55 minutes late, 13.75 minutes per order on average, and the machine stops at minute 100, after 70 minutes of painting and 30 of cleaning.

Now reorder. Move D before C in the hero: ABDC paints D straight after B, with no colour change between them. The orders are now 40 minutes late in total, and none of the 24 possible orders does better. Press SST, shortest setup first: ACBD changes colour once and finishes at minute 80, the earliest possible, yet it is 50 minutes late in total. Press EDD, most urgent first: 85 minutes late, because serving B first costs a colour change at minute 0, and the due dates then alternate the colours: A, D, C.

Three lessons, made precise below: a schedule must be valid (no two orders at once, no skipped colour change); it must be measured, and reasonable measures disagree on the best order; and on one machine the decision is an order, 4! = 24 of them here, 3{,}628{,}800 for 10 orders.

Part 2

Jobs, timetables, and what makes one valid

What is a schedule?

In plain words: a timetable that says, for every piece of work, which machine does it and from when to when, and that could really be carried out.

More precisely: jobs j \in \mathcal J = \{0, \dots, n-1\} are processed on machines \mathcal M; job j is a chain of operations (j, k), k = 0, \dots, K_j - 1. A schedule gives each operation a machine m_{jk} and a start S_{jk}, with end E_{jk} = S_{jk} + p_{jk m_{jk}}. It is valid (feasible) when every constraint of the shop holds.

Job, operation
an order of a customer; one of its steps on one machine. The paint example has K_j = 1, the workshop K_j = 4.
Release time r_j
when the job becomes available. Nothing of it can start before.
Due date d_j
when the job was promised. Missing it is allowed, and penalised by the measures of Part 3.
Changeover s_m(f, g)
the time machine m needs to switch from family f (for the paint machine: the colour) to family g; zero when f = g, and none before a machine's first operation.

The paint machine, written precisely. With one operation per job, a job is described by its start S_j and completion C_j = S_j + p_j. Let \pi = (\pi(1), \dots, \pi(n)) be the order on the machine and s_{\pi(q)} the changeover before the q-th job (q counts positions; k stays an operation): 10 if its colour differs from the previous job's (or from blue for the first), else 0. A timetable is valid when, for every position q (with C_{\pi(0)} = 0),

\boxed{S_{\pi(q)} - s_{\pi(q)} \ \ge\ \max\left(C_{\pi(q-1)},\ r_{\pi(q)}\right)} .

The changeover occupies [S_{\pi(q)} - s_{\pi(q)}, S_{\pi(q)}); it cannot begin while the previous job is painting (capacity) nor before the job has arrived (release), and it cannot be skipped (changeover). As in the repository, a changeover needs the job at the machine; a shop that prepares machines in advance would relax that.

A worked example with release times. Three jobs, one machine, no changeovers: (r, p, d) = (0, 8, 10) for job 1, (5, 4, 12) for job 2, (12, 6, 25) for job 3. In the order 1, 2, 3: job 1 runs 0–8, job 2 8–12, job 3 12–18. In the order 2, 1, 3, job 2 cannot start before 5, so the machine is idle from 0 to 5 although job 1 is waiting; then job 2 runs 5–9, job 1 9–17, job 3 17–23. Both are valid.

In the workshop (five machines, alternative routes, two carts called AGVs, automated guided vehicles), a valid schedule satisfies:

  1. Completeness and eligibility: each operation is processed for p_{jkm} minutes on one machine m of its eligible set \mathcal M_{jk}; a breakdown may suspend it, and it resumes on the same machine with nothing else in between.
  2. Capacity: a machine does one thing at a time, and nothing while it is down.
  3. Changeovers: before each operation the machine spends exactly s_m(f, g) minutes, f the family of the operation it ran before; none before its first.
  4. Precedence and release: the changeover of (j, k), if any, and so S_{jk} - s, starts after (j, k-1) has ended and the job has been carried to the machine; for (j, 0), after r_j.
  5. Transport: a cart is in one place at a time and needs \tau(a, b) minutes from station a to station b.

The repository's independent checker, jobshop/checker.py:check_schedule, re-derives each of them from the executed log without looking at how the schedule was made; all 480 runs of the benchmark pass it. Chapters A3 and C1 write these constraints as mathematics and as a solver model.

Try it on the paint machine: type start times, the order is read from them, and a small checker applies the boxed inequality.

Set B to 25: its colour change would start at 15, while A paints until 20, and the checker refuses. Press "Delay B and all after it by 5": still valid, no longer as early as possible.

Part 3

Measuring a schedule

A measure, or objective, turns a timetable into one number to be made small. The common ones are built from completion times.

What are completion, lateness, tardiness and flow time?

In plain words: when a job is finished; how far that is from its promise, negative when early; how late it is, counting early as zero; how long it spent in the shop.

More precisely: for job j,

Completion time
C_j = E_{j, K_j-1}, the end of its last operation.
Lateness
L_j = C_j - d_j.
Tardiness
T_j = \max(0, L_j); earliness e_j = \max(0, -L_j) (lower case: E_{jk} stays the end of an operation), so L_j = T_j - e_j.
Late indicator
U_j = 1 if C_j > d_j, else 0.
Flow time
F_j = C_j - r_j, from arrival to completion.

The measures of a schedule are sums, means and maxima of these:

\begin{gathered} C_{\max} = \max_j C_j, \qquad L_{\max} = \max_j L_j, \qquad T_{\max} = \max_j T_j, \\ \sum_j C_j, \qquad \sum_j F_j, \qquad \sum_j T_j, \qquad \sum_j U_j . \end{gathered}

The everyday "minutes late" of Part 1 is tardiness; lateness keeps the sign, so early jobs count negative. C_{\max} is the makespan, when the last job leaves, counted from minute 0. The mean tardiness \bar T = \frac1n \sum_j T_j is the headline measure of the course: the average delay per order, early orders counting as on time.

FIFO, measured. With C = (20, 40, 65, 100) for A, B, C, D and d = (30, 25, 70, 60),

\begin{gathered} L = (-10,\ 15,\ -5,\ 40), \qquad T = (0,\ 15,\ 0,\ 40), \\ \sum_j T_j = 55, \qquad \bar T = 55/4 = 13.75, \end{gathered}

L_{\max} = T_{\max} = 40, two late orders, \sum_j C_j = 225, C_{\max} = 100. All orders wait from 0, so F_j = C_j. Tick the arrivals box in the hero: ABCD's timetable is unchanged, since each order has arrived when its turn comes, but \sum_j F_j = 225 - 30 = 195.

The three-job example, measured. Order 1, 2, 3: C = (8, 12, 18), no tardiness, \sum C_j = 38, \sum F_j = 21, C_{\max} = 18. Order 2, 1, 3: C = (17, 9, 23) for jobs 1, 2, 3, T_1 = 7, L_{\max} = 7, \bar T = 7/3 = 2.33, \sum C_j = 49, \sum F_j = 32, C_{\max} = 23. The first is better on every measure.

Weighted sums. With weights w_j \ge 0, \sum_j w_j C_j and \sum_j w_j T_j favour important customers: with w = (3, 1, 1), order 1, 2, 3 scores 3 \cdot 8 + 12 + 18 = 54, order 2, 1, 3 scores 83. Weights also combine measures. The CP-SAT planner of chapter C1 minimises

J = 10 \sum_j T_j + \sum_j C_j ,

summed over the jobs planned at each event: a minute of tardiness weighs ten minutes of completion, so among plans of equal tardiness the earlier one wins. It is a trade, not a strict priority. FIFO scores 10 \cdot 55 + 225 = 775, SST 10 \cdot 50 + 190 = 690, and the best of the 24 orders is again ABDC with 10 \cdot 40 + 215 = 615.

Relations, each one line of algebra:

  • T_{\max} = \max(0, L_{\max}), because \max(0, \cdot) is non-decreasing: minimising L_{\max} also minimises T_{\max}, not conversely (when no job is late, all have T_{\max} = 0).
  • \sum_j F_j = \sum_j C_j - \sum_j r_j and \sum_j L_j = \sum_j C_j - \sum_j d_j, where \sum_j r_j and \sum_j d_j are data: total flow time, total completion time and total lateness have the same minimisers (exercise 2, pitfall 1).
  • \bar T = \frac1n \sum_j T_j: mean and total tardiness rank schedules alike.
  • With every d_j = 0, T_j = L_j = C_j: total tardiness becomes \sum_j C_j and L_{\max} becomes C_{\max}. Part 5 uses this.
Part 4

Regular measures, and why an order is enough

All the measures above share one property: finishing a job later never improves them.

What is a regular measure?

In plain words: a measure that can only get worse, or stay the same, when any job finishes later. Waiting never helps.

More precisely: \gamma(C_0, \dots, C_{n-1}) is regular if C_j \le C'_j for all j implies \gamma(C) \le \gamma(C').

Regular
C_{\max}, \sum C_j, \sum w_j C_j, \sum F_j, L_{\max}, T_{\max}, \sum T_j, \sum w_j T_j, \sum U_j, and nonnegative weighted sums of them, such as 10 \sum T_j + \sum C_j.
Not regular
total earliness \sum e_j, or the just-in-time cost \sum (e_j + T_j).

In the timetable widget, tick "show the measures" and press "Delay B and all after it by 5" on the FIFO timetable (A 0, B 30, C 50, D 75): total tardiness rises from 55 to 65, the makespan from 100 to 105, L_{\max} from 40 to 45, but total earliness falls from 15 to 10, as C now finishes exactly on time.

A timetable in which every job starts as early as its order on the machine allows is called semi-active [2].

Theorem (left shift). On one machine with release times and sequence-dependent changeovers, every regular measure has an optimal schedule that is semi-active. It is determined by its sequence, so an optimum is found among the n! sequences.

Proof. Take a valid schedule S with order \pi, and build the semi-active timetable of the same order:

S'_{\pi(q)} = \max\left(C'_{\pi(q-1)},\ r_{\pi(q)}\right) + s_{\pi(q)}, \quad C'_{\pi(0)} = 0 .

The changeovers are the same, since they depend only on the order. By induction, C'_{\pi(q)} \le C_{\pi(q)} for all q. It holds for q = 0. If it holds for q - 1, then, using it inside the maximum and then the validity of S,

\begin{aligned} C'_{\pi(q)} &= \max\left(C'_{\pi(q-1)}, r_{\pi(q)}\right) + s_{\pi(q)} + p_{\pi(q)} \\ &\le \max\left(C_{\pi(q-1)}, r_{\pi(q)}\right) + s_{\pi(q)} + p_{\pi(q)} \\ &\le S_{\pi(q)} + p_{\pi(q)} = C_{\pi(q)} . \end{aligned}

S' is valid by construction and no job finishes later, so \gamma(C') \le \gamma(C). So for every valid S, the semi-active schedule of its order is at least as good. The best of the n! semi-active schedules is therefore at least as good as every valid schedule, which proves both that an optimum exists and that one is semi-active. \square

In the workshop the same induction runs over all operations at once, in order of their start times, which respects every job's route and every machine's order. With a fixed transport lag, as in the CP-SAT model of C1, no operation starts later, so a plan is fixed by an order on each machine plus as-early-as-possible starts (A3); with real carts, whose trips depend on the timing, only approximately. Non-regular measures break the argument: one job with p = 20, d = 30 has earliness 10 when started at 0, none when started at 10.

Semi-active is not "never idle". A non-delay schedule never leaves a machine idle while a job waits [2]; dispatching rules (rules such as FIFO that, whenever a machine frees, pick the next job from those waiting) produce such schedules, and with release times they can miss the optimum. Job 1 with (r, p, d) = (0, 10, 100), job 2 with (1, 1, 2): non-delay starts job 1 at 0, and job 2 ends 9 minutes late; waiting one minute, job 2 runs 1–2 and job 1 2–12, both on time. Both are semi-active; only the worse is non-delay. The re-planning controller of C3 can hold a machine for a planned job already in the shop; nothing can wait for an order not yet released, since arrivals are not known in advance.

On the paint machine with everything waiting, a semi-active schedule has no idle time: the 24 permutations are all there is, and the hero's "best" buttons enumerate them. With arrivals, DCBA must idle until D arrives at 15 and ends at 125.

Part 5

Graham's three fields

Problems differ in their machines, constraints and measure. Graham, Lawler, Lenstra and Rinnooy Kan [1] gave each a one-line name with three fields, \alpha \mid \beta \mid \gamma. The paint machine is 1 \mid s_{jk} \mid \sum T_j: one machine; changeovers that depend on the sequence; total tardiness.

What does α | β | γ say?

In plain words: the machines, then the special rules of the shop, then what is minimised.

More precisely (with Pinedo's conventions [2]). The notation has its own letters: in s_{ijk}, i is a machine and j, k are two consecutive jobs; in Pm, m counts machines. Elsewhere in the course m is a machine, k an operation and i a test day.

\alpha, machines
1 one machine; Pm m identical parallel machines, a job goes to any one; Fm flow shop, every job visits the machines in the same order; Jm job shop, each job its own route, one machine per operation; FJm flexible job shop, each operation on any machine of an eligible set, with machine-dependent times. (Pinedo's FJc means c work centres of identical parallel machines; ours, with overlapping eligible sets, is Brandimarte's flexible job shop [6], with no single standard symbol: we write FJm, eligibility included.)
\beta, constraints
empty means the defaults: all jobs at time 0, no changeovers, no interruptions. r_j release times; s_{jk} sequence-dependent changeovers, s_{ijk} if they also depend on the machine; prec precedences; brkdwn breakdowns.
\gamma, objective
a measure of Part 3: C_{\max}, L_{\max}, \sum C_j, \sum w_j C_j, \sum T_j, \sum w_j T_j, \sum U_j.

From the simplest to this course's:

  • 1 \mid\mid \sum C_j: one machine, minimise total completion time. Shortest job first (SPT) is optimal (Smith [3]), proved in chapter A2.
  • 1 \mid\mid L_{\max}: most urgent first (EDD) is optimal (Jackson [4]), also in A2.
  • 1 \mid\mid \sum T_j: no simple rule is optimal; the problem is NP-hard (Du and Leung [5]), in the ordinary sense. NP-hard means: no algorithm is known that solves every instance (every set of numbers for the problem) in a time growing like a power of n, and finding one would solve thousands of other open problems; in practice, exact solution time explodes with n. Chapter A2 gives the argument.
  • 1 \mid s_{jk} \mid C_{\max} and 1 \mid s_{jk} \mid \sum T_j: the paint machine is an instance of both; its makespan with all orders waiting is 70 + 10 \times (colour changes), so minimising it minimises changeover time, the travelling-salesman view of A2 (colours visited like cities, changeovers as distances).
  • P2 \mid\mid C_{\max}: two identical machines, already NP-hard [2]. Jobs of 3, 3, 2, 2, 2: 3 + 3 against 2 + 2 + 2 reaches the bound \sum_j p_j / 2 = 6; longest first to the less loaded machine gives 7 and 5.
  • Jm \mid\mid C_{\max}: the classical job shop, a batch released at once; NP-hard [2]. Chapter A3 turns it into a disjunctive graph whose longest path is the makespan.

This course's problem is

FJm \mid r_j,\ s_{ijk} \mid \sum_j T_j \qquad (m = 5),

that is, a flexible job shop whose operations (j, k) may run on one to three machines \mathcal M_{jk} with times p_{jkm}; release times; changeovers depending on the machine and on the families of consecutive jobs j, j', s_{ijj'} = s_i(f(j), f(j')); total tardiness, equivalently \bar T. The line leaves out what the course also models: arrivals revealed only when they happen (a dynamic, or online, problem [7]); random breakdowns (brkdwn says that they happen, not how); two carts with travel times \tau(a, b); and a measure averaged over 20 random test days (D2).

Hardness travels upward. Problem A is a special case of B if every instance of A can be rewritten, in polynomial time, as an instance of B with the same optimal schedules: one machine is a flexible job shop with one machine and one operation per job; \sum C_j is \sum T_j with every d_j = 0. A fast algorithm for B would then solve A, so if A is NP-hard, so is B. With 1 \mid\mid \sum T_j as a special case (r_j = 0, no changeovers), the general problem FJm \mid r_j, s_{ijk} \mid \sum T_j, for any n and any data, is NP-hard even before the randomness: the course compares methods rather than looking for a formula.

Part 6

Why the objective matters

Here are all 24 paint orders at once, on four measures.

The 24 sequences on four measures (click a dot to load it)

With all orders waiting, the makespan takes four values, 80 to 110, reached by 4, 8, 8 and 4 sequences; FIFO's 100 is shared by eight sequences, ranked 13th to 20th, while FIFO is 3rd for total tardiness. Choosing the measure is choosing the schedule.

On the benchmark. The repository runs seven methods on the same 20 simulated days of 60 jobs: the four rules, the self-learning agent (DQN), quick re-planning (Greedy replan) and optimiser re-planning (CP-SAT) (B2, C3, D1). The widget shows mean measures from results/runs.csv, among them the utilisation, the share of the five machines' time from 0 to C_{\max} spent processing.

Without breakdowns, the highest mean tardiness, 15.54 minutes, is 11.66 times the lowest, 1.33 (the rounded 15.5 / 1.3 give 11.9). Under frequent breakdowns the spreads are 2.7 % (makespan), 3.0 % (utilisation), 28.1 % (flow time) and a factor of 3.15 (tardiness). Without breakdowns, the shortest makespan belongs to shortest setup first, only fifth on tardiness; optimiser re-planning has a shorter makespan than FIFO on 15 of the 20 days, but a lower mean tardiness on all 20.

Why the makespan cannot tell them apart. Jobs arrive all day, the last one on average at minute 1,208.2 (between 895 and 1,491). The makespan is the last arrival plus the time to clear what remains, 139.4 minutes on average without breakdowns: the demand sets it, a method moves only its tail. Utilisation divides processing time, set by the routing (the choice of machine for each operation), by 5\,C_{\max}, so it barely moves either. Makespan suits a batch released at once; a shop that promises dates and takes orders all day is judged on its promises.

Part 7

The code behind it

Every measure is computed from the executed log, never from a plan; the same log goes to the checker, and scripts/evaluate.py writes runs.csv only if no run has a checker error.

jobshop/metrics.py · run_metrics

def run_metrics(ws: Workshop, scenario: Scenario, log: RunLog) -> dict[str, float]:
    completion: dict[int, int] = {}
    proc_total = setup_total = 0
    for s in log.segments:
        completion[s.job] = max(completion.get(s.job, 0), s.end)
        if s.kind == "proc":
            proc_total += s.end - s.start
        else:
            setup_total += s.end - s.start
    tardiness = np.array([max(0, completion[j.idx] - j.due) for j in scenario.jobs], dtype=float)
    flow = np.array([completion[j.idx] - j.arrival for j in scenario.jobs], dtype=float)
    makespan = max(completion.values())
    empty = sum(g.end - g.start for g in log.legs if g.job is None)
    loaded = sum(g.end - g.start for g in log.legs if g.job is not None)
    return {
        "makespan": float(makespan),
        "mean_tardiness": float(tardiness.mean()),
        "max_tardiness": float(tardiness.max()),
        "tardy_share": float((tardiness > 0).mean()),
        "mean_flow_time": float(flow.mean()),
        # share of the machine-time available until the last job leaves that is spent processing
        "utilisation": proc_total / (ws.n_machines * makespan),
        "setup_time": float(setup_total),
        "agv_empty_share": empty / max(1, empty + loaded),
    }

The log holds segments (machine, job, op, kind "setup" or "proc", start, end) and legs, one per cart trip. The first loop makes C_j the largest end among job j's segments, the end of its last operation, and adds up processing and changeover minutes. tardiness is T_j = \max(0, C_j - d_j), flow is F_j = C_j - r_j (j.arrival is r_j, j.due is d_j). The dictionary holds \bar T (mean_tardiness), T_{\max}, the late share \frac1n \sum_j U_j (tardy_share), the mean flow time, the utilisation (changeovers excluded, over 5\,C_{\max}), the changeover total and the carts' empty share.

One day, checked by hand. FIFO on test day 0 without breakdowns: 386 machine segments, 348 cart legs, no checker error, and run_metrics returns the row of runs.csv exactly. From the segments, 16 of the 60 jobs are late, 408 minutes in total, so \bar T = 408/60 = 6.8; \sum C_j - \sum r_j = 49{,}720 - 40{,}936 = 8{,}784, a mean flow time of 146.4. The machines processed for 4,077 minutes and changed over for 1,039, of 5 \times 1381 = 6{,}905 machine-minutes: utilisation 0.5904. Those changeover minutes are what a better choice of the next job can win back (B2, C3).

Part 8

Common pitfalls

  1. "Minimising total lateness keeps orders on time." \sum_j L_j = \sum_j C_j - \sum_j d_j, with \sum_j d_j = 185 for the paint orders whatever the order: it is minimised by CABD, 55 minutes late in total. Early jobs cancel late ones in a sum of L_j; the \max(0, \cdot) of tardiness stops that.
  2. "Makespan tells how well the shop is run." Only for a batch released at once. With arrivals all day and no breakdowns, the seven methods' means differ by 1.3 %.
  3. "Higher utilisation is better." Sending a job to a slower machine raises the processing minutes, hence the utilisation, while delaying the job.
  4. "A schedule is a sequence." Only for regular measures on one machine; in the workshop it also assigns machines, and under a just-in-time measure idle time is a decision too.
  5. "All measures rank methods alike." Under breakdowns optimiser re-planning beats quick re-planning on mean tardiness (19.3 against 24.0 minutes), not on the share of late jobs (0.447 against 0.441). Choose the headline measure before looking (D2).
Part 9

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question before opening it.

1. On one machine with all jobs at time 0 and no changeovers, why do all semi-active schedules have the same makespan?

They have no idle time, so the machine works from 0 to \sum_j p_j. With changeovers, C_{\max} = \sum_j p_j + \sum_q s_{\pi(q)} depends on the order: 80 to 110 for the paint machine.

2. Why does the course count tardiness rather than lateness?

L_j is negative for an early job, so a sum of lateness lets early jobs cancel late ones, and is equivalent to total completion time. T_j = \max(0, L_j) counts only broken promises.

3. Why do \sum_j F_j and \sum_j C_j have the same minimisers?

\sum_j F_j = \sum_j C_j - \sum_j r_j, and the release times are data. The objectives differ by a constant.

4. Which are regular: L_{\max}, \sum U_j, \sum e_j, 10 \sum T_j + \sum C_j?

All but \sum e_j, which decreases when a job finishes later, closer to its due date.

5. Why may a search for minimum total tardiness on one machine look only at sequences?

Total tardiness is regular, so by the left-shift theorem some optimal schedule is semi-active, and a semi-active schedule is determined by its sequence.

6. Read 1 \mid s_{jk} \mid \sum T_j field by field.

One machine; sequence-dependent changeovers, otherwise the defaults (all jobs at time 0, no interruptions); minimise total tardiness. The paint machine's problem.

7. Why is the course's problem, in general, NP-hard?

For any n and any data, it contains 1 \mid\mid \sum T_j, NP-hard by Du and Leung, as the special case with one machine, one operation per job, no releases and no changeovers. A fixed small instance is not "NP-hard": the term is about how solution time grows with n.

8. Why does the benchmark rank methods by mean tardiness, not makespan?

With arrivals all day, the makespan is the last arrival plus a short tail (without breakdowns the methods differ by 1.3 %), while the promises, which the shop is judged on, differ by a factor of 11.66.

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution; the coding exercise runs from the course download.

  1. Every measure, by hand. For the EDD sequence B, A, D, C (all orders waiting at 0, machine set up for blue), draw the Gantt chart and compute C_j, L_j, T_j, then C_{\max}, \sum C_j, L_{\max}, \sum T_j, \bar T, \sum U_j and the changeover time. Check with the hero.

    Hint

    B is red, so the machine changes colour first; after that, every consecutive pair alternates.

    Solution

    B: changeover 0–10, paints 10–20, L = -5. A: changeover 20–30, paints 30–50, L = 20. D: changeover 50–60, paints 60–85, L = 25. C: changeover 85–95, paints 95–110, L = 40. So T = (20, 0, 40, 25) for A, B, C, D, C_{\max} = 110, \sum C_j = 20 + 50 + 85 + 110 = 265, L_{\max} = 40, \sum T_j = 85, \bar T = 21.25, three late orders, 40 minutes of changeover.

  2. Sums and maxima of flow time. (a) Write out the one-line proof that \sum_j F_j and \sum_j C_j have the same minimisers; in the hero, tick the arrivals, press best ΣC and read \sum F_j = \sum C_j - 30. (b) Is the same true of C_{\max} and F_{\max} = \max_j F_j? Try one machine, no changeovers, three jobs with (r, p) = (0, 10), (0, 10), (10, 1).

    Hint

    (a) What in \sum_j C_j - \sum_j r_j depends on the schedule? (b) A constant can be pulled out of a sum, not out of a maximum: each F_j subtracts its own r_j.

    Solution

    (a) \sum_j F_j = \sum_j C_j - R with R = \sum_j r_j fixed by the data, so the two order all valid schedules identically. With arrivals at 0, 5, 10, 15, R = 30, and ACBD is best for both: \sum C_j = 190, \sum F_j = 160.

    (b) No. The four orders not starting with job 3 have C_{\max} = 21 (starting with job 3 idles the machine: 31). F_{\max} is 20 for 1, 2, 3 and 2, 1, 3, but 21 for 1, 3, 2 and 2, 3, 1, whose last job ends at 21 with r = 0. Four orders minimise C_{\max}, two minimise F_{\max}.

  3. Which measures are regular. Prove that a nonnegative weighted sum and a maximum of regular measures are regular. Then decide, with a proof or a two-job counterexample: \sum_j U_j; \sum_j T_j^2; C_{\max} - \min_j C_j (the spread of the completions).

    Hint

    Regular means non-decreasing in each C_j. For the spread, delay only the first job to finish.

    Solution

    If C \le C' componentwise and \gamma_1, \gamma_2 are regular, then w_1 \gamma_1(C) + w_2 \gamma_2(C) \le w_1 \gamma_1(C') + w_2 \gamma_2(C') for w_1, w_2 \ge 0, and \max(\gamma_1(C), \gamma_2(C)) \le \max(\gamma_1(C'), \gamma_2(C')).

    \sum_j U_j: each U_j = [C_j > d_j] is a non-decreasing step, so the sum is regular. \sum_j T_j^2: T_j \ge 0 is non-decreasing in C_j and x \mapsto x^2 is non-decreasing on x \ge 0: regular. The spread is not: with completions (10, 20) it is 10; delaying the first job to 15 gives (15, 20) and spread 5, smaller although a job finished later.

  4. Name the problem. Write \alpha \mid \beta \mid \gamma for: (a) a printer with jobs queued at 8 a.m., to minimise the mean time until a job is printed; (b) two identical ovens and a batch of trays ready at once, to finish all as early as possible; (c) the paint machine judged on its worst delay; (d) three machines visited in the same order by every job, all ready at 0, minimise the makespan. Then show that 1 \mid\mid \sum C_j is a special case of 1 \mid\mid \sum T_j.

    Hint

    Mean and total completion time are equivalent. For the last part, pick due dates that make T_j = C_j.

    Solution

    (a) 1 \mid\mid \sum C_j. (b) P2 \mid\mid C_{\max}. (c) 1 \mid s_{jk} \mid L_{\max} (or T_{\max}). (d) F3 \mid\mid C_{\max}. Given an instance of 1 \mid\mid \sum C_j, set every d_j = 0: since C_j \ge 0, T_j = C_j, so \sum_j T_j = \sum_j C_j for every schedule and an optimum of one is an optimum of the other. An algorithm for total tardiness therefore solves total completion time.

  5. Forced idle time. With arrivals at A 0, B 5, C 10, D 15, compute the timetable of C, D, A, B, then C_{\max}, \sum T_j and \sum F_j. Where is the machine idle? Is the timetable semi-active, non-delay?

    Hint

    C cannot start before 10, and C is blue.

    Solution

    Idle 0–10; C paints 10–25; D: changeover 25–35, paints 35–60; A: changeover 60–70, paints 70–90; B: changeover 90–100, paints 100–110. C_{\max} = 110, T = (60, 85, 0, 0) for A, B, C, D, \sum T_j = 145, F = (90, 105, 15, 45), \sum F_j = 255. Semi-active, since no job of this order can start earlier; not non-delay, since A waits from 0 while the machine is idle.

  6. Your own measures, against the repository's. Write my_metrics(scenario, log) returning \sum C_j, \sum F_j, \sum r_j, C_{\max}, \bar T and L_{\max} from a run's log. Simulate most urgent first and shortest setup first on test days 0, 1, 2 without breakdowns, compare with run_metrics, and check the identity \sum F_j = \sum C_j - \sum r_j against its independently computed mean flow time.

    Hint

    Completion is the largest end over a job's segments. A rule is RuleController("EDD"), a day make_scenario(ws, seed, mtbf=None), a run Shop(ws, scenario, controller).run().

    Solution 
    from jobshop.metrics import run_metrics
from jobshop.rules import RuleController
from jobshop.scenario import make_scenario, make_workshop
from jobshop.shop import Shop

def my_metrics(scenario, log):
    C = {}
    for s in log.segments:
        C[s.job] = max(C.get(s.job, 0), s.end)
    jobs = scenario.jobs
    return dict(sumC=sum(C[j.idx] for j in jobs), sumF=sum(C[j.idx] - j.arrival for j in jobs),
                sum_r=sum(j.arrival for j in jobs), Cmax=max(C.values()),
                meanT=sum(max(0, C[j.idx] - j.due) for j in jobs) / len(jobs),
                Lmax=max(C[j.idx] - j.due for j in jobs))

ws = make_workshop()
for rule in ("EDD", "SST"):
    for seed in range(3):
        sc = make_scenario(ws, seed, mtbf=None)
        log = Shop(ws, sc, RuleController(rule)).run()
        mine, theirs = my_metrics(sc, log), run_metrics(ws, sc, log)
        print(rule, seed, mine["Cmax"], theirs["makespan"], round(mine["meanT"], 2),
              theirs["mean_tardiness"], mine["Lmax"],
              abs((mine["sumC"] - mine["sum_r"]) / len(sc.jobs) - theirs["mean_flow_time"]) < 1e-9)

    EDD: makespans 1384, 1270, 1403, mean tardiness 2.95, 11.60, 2.08; SST: 1367, 1252, 1403 and 6.13, 9.27, 2.20, all as run_metrics, the flow identity included. On day 0, SST is about twice as late as EDD for a similar makespan.

Part 11

The chapter on one page

  1. A schedule gives every operation a machine and a start time, and must be valid: capacity, changeovers, precedence, release, eligibility, transport, as the repository's checker verifies.
  2. Measures come from completion times: L_j, T_j = \max(0, L_j), F_j, U_j, then their maxima, sums, means and weighted sums. \sum F_j, \sum C_j and \sum L_j differ by constants.
  3. Regular measures never improve when a job finishes later; for them an optimal semi-active schedule exists, so on one machine the decision is a sequence. Non-delay schedules can miss the optimum.
  4. Three fields \alpha \mid \beta \mid \gamma name a problem; this course's, FJm \mid r_j, s_{ijk} \mid \sum T_j, is NP-hard in general because it contains 1 \mid\mid \sum T_j, with online arrivals, breakdowns and carts on top.
  5. Paint machine: FIFO 55 minutes late, the best order ABDC 40, the shortest makespan 80 at 50 minutes late or more. Workshop, without breakdowns: makespan and utilisation differ by 1.3 % and 1.2 % across methods, mean tardiness by a factor of 11.66.

The vocabulary, in one table

Word Meaning
Job, operation An order; one of its steps on one machine.
Release r_j, due date d_j When the job can start; when it was promised.
Changeover s_m(f, g); valid (feasible) Time to switch machine m from family f to g; meeting every constraint of the shop.
Gantt chart Bars along a time axis, one row per machine or job.
C_j, L_j, T_j, e_j, F_j Completion; signed distance to the due date; delay, zero if early; earliness; time in the shop.
Makespan C_{\max}, mean tardiness \bar T When the last job ends; the average delay per job.
Utilisation Share of machine time up to C_{\max} spent processing.
Regular measure Non-decreasing in every completion time.
Semi-active, non-delay Every job as early as its order allows; no machine idle while a job waits.
Dispatching rule Picks the next job whenever a machine frees.
\alpha \mid \beta \mid \gamma Machines, constraints, objective.
Special case, NP-hard Rewritable, in polynomial time, inside a larger problem; no known algorithm polynomial in n.

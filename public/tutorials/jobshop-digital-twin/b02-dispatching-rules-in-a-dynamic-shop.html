Dispatching rules in a dynamic shop

A machine has just finished an operation and three jobs wait in front of it: which one starts? A dispatching rule answers with one number per waiting job, its priority index, and starts the best, without a plan. This chapter writes the course's four rules as indices, derives the routing formula and the rerouting after a failure, reads the rules on twenty simulated days, derives the single-server queue that explains why breakdowns favour avoiding changeovers, and builds a composite index, ATCS, term by term. By the end you will be able to write any rule as an index, compute by hand where a job is routed and which job ATCS starts, and explain why the best rule depends on how often machines fail.

Part 1

The question a free machine asks

In plain words. Machine m2 finishes a job; three jobs of different families, lengths and due dates wait in its buffer, and the controller's sequence method (B1) says which starts. A dispatching rule answers locally: it scores each job in this queue and starts the best, with no plan, in microseconds (completely reactive scheduling, in the survey of Ouelhadj and Petrovic [7]). Rules are simple, robust and free; they can be beaten because a local look cannot see what is coming (Part 6).

How often does the choice matter? A simulated day has 60 jobs of 4 operations, so 240 decisions: each operation starts after one call to sequence. Under most urgent first only 24.1 % of the decisions of a calm day have a real choice, two or more jobs waiting (mean queue 1.30 jobs); with frequent breakdowns, 42.2 % (mean queue 1.60). Rules differ through a minority of decisions, more when machines fail.

What is a dispatching rule?

In plain words: a recipe a machine applies each time it becomes free: score every waiting job from what is known now, start the best.

More precisely: at a decision epoch t (a minute at which machine m is idle and up and its queue \mathcal Q_m(t) is not empty), the rule gives each j \in \mathcal Q_m(t) a priority index I_j(t) and starts

j^\star = \arg\min_{j \in \mathcal Q_m(t)} I_j(t),

ties broken by a second index and finally by the job number. The index may use the job's data, the time and the state of machine m, not the other machines' queues: the rule is local.

Priority index
The score I_j(t); smallest first here, except for ATCS (largest first).
Non-delay
A free machine with a waiting job starts one; every rule of the course is non-delay.
Policy
A map from the shop's state to a decision, written \pi in chapter D1 for the learning agent.
Part 2

Four rules as priority indices

The four rules of jobshop/rules.py. Job j waits for operation k on machine m; q_j is when it entered this queue; g_m is the family m is set up for (that of the last job it ran; none if it has run nothing, and then no changeover); s_m(g_m, f(j)) is the changeover of A3.

Rule Index I_j(t), smallest first Ties Depends on t or g_m?
First come, first served (FIFO) q_j job number no: static
Shortest job first (SPT) p_{jkm} job number no: static
Most urgent first (EDD) d_j job number no: static
Shortest setup first (SST) s_m(g_m, f(j)) due date, then job number yes: dynamic

FIFO uses the entry into this queue, not the release time r_j; EDD uses the job's due date for every operation; SST breaks ties by due date, so among jobs needing no changeover it is EDD.

The paint machine as indices. A2 gives each rule's order and total tardiness on the four orders; none finds the best, A B D C with 40 minutes. What the index view adds: FIFO, SPT and EDD sort one row fixed at t = 0 (arrival ranks, painting times, due dates), while SST scores the pair (changeover, due date) anew at each decision, and its tie-break decides twice. Set up for blue: A (0, 30), B (10, 25), C (0, 70), D (10, 60), so A, by its due date. Still blue: C. Still blue, B and D both need 10: B, by its due date. Now red: D needs 0. A C B D, 50 minutes.

What separates a static index from a dynamic one?

In plain words: a static index is a label on the job, so two waiting jobs never swap ranks while they wait; a dynamic index also depends on the clock or the machine, and ranks can change between decisions.

More precisely: I_j is static if I_j(t) depends on neither t nor the machine's state while j waits. Then, on one machine without arrivals, dispatching produces the jobs sorted by index (exercise 1 proves it): at every decision the smallest remaining index is the next element of the sorted list. A dynamic index must be recomputed at every decision, and its sequence need not be a sort.

Part 3

Earliest expected finish, and rerouting

In plain words. When a job arrives or finishes an operation, the controller picks which eligible machine does its next one: routing. The repository does what a shopper does at the checkouts: estimate when the job would be done at each machine, go where that is earliest. The four rules and the agent of D1 share this routing, so Part 4 compares sequencing only. When a machine fails, the jobs in its queue are routed again: rerouting.

A real reroute. Test day 0, frequent breakdowns, shortest setup first: machine m4 fails at minute 124 with job 0 (family 0, operation 2, due at 207) in its queue. The operation can run on m4 (7 min) or m1 (10 min).

  • Stay: the job is there, so it could start at 124; m4 is expected back at 124 + 20 = 144 (the mean repair time), then 13 minutes of the interrupted operation and 17 committed to job 2, on its way: m4 is free for job 0 at 174 and job 0 is done at \max(124, 174) + 7 = 181.
  • Move: the cart needs 3 minutes, so the job could start at 127; m1 is up and busy for 22 more minutes, free at 146: done at \max(127, 146) + 10 = 156.

The job moves to the slower machine and expects to finish 25 minutes earlier; even with the real end of the repair, minute 137, staying would give \max(124, 137 + 13 + 17) + 7 = 174. Job 2, not yet at m4, is counted ahead of job 0. The sliders show when the decision flips.

What is earliest-expected-finish routing?

In plain words: send each job to the machine where, by a rough estimate made now, it would be finished first.

More precisely: minimise the expected completion \hat C_{jm} below over the eligible machines, ties by the shorter processing time, then the lower machine number. It assumes all committed work runs before j, counts no changeovers for j, ignores the wait for a cart, and replaces the repair by MTTR = 20 min (the real one lasts uniform [10, 30] min).

The formula, one symbol at a time. Job j needs operation k at time t and stands at station b_j (the station of the machine it is at, or the load station 0). For each eligible machine m \in \mathcal M_{jk}, route_earliest_finish computes:

  • R_{jm} = t + \tau(b_j, m+1): the ready time, when the job could be at m after the cart's travel (no travel if it is already there); the same R_{jm} that chapters C1 and C3 read from a snapshot.
  • \hat t^{\,\text{up}}_m: when m is expected up: t if it is up; if it is down, \hat t^{\,\text{up}}_m = \max\big(\operatorname{round}(t^{\text{down}}_m + \text{MTTR}),\ t+1\big), with t^{\text{down}}_m the minute m failed. The real repair time is hidden (B1), so the controller assumes the mean, and at least one more minute if that estimate is overdue.
  • \mathrm{rem}_m: what remains of the operation in progress on m, its changeover included; 0 if m is idle.
  • \phi_m = \hat t^{\,\text{up}}_m + \mathrm{rem}_m: when m could start another job, the \phi_m of the planner's snapshot (C3).
  • U_m = \sum_{i \in \mathcal Q_m \cup \mathcal I_m,\ i \ne j} p_{i k_i m}: the work committed to m by the jobs in its queue \mathcal Q_m or on their way to it \mathcal I_m, each at its own next operation k_i, j excluded.
  • \hat C_{jm} = \max(R_{jm},\ \phi_m + U_m) + p_{jkm}: when j is expected done on m.

The job goes to \arg\min_m (\hat C_{jm}, p_{jkm}, m), compared lexicographically. In the example, m4 has R = 124 and \phi + U = 144 + 13 + 17, so \hat C = 181; m1 has R = 127 and \phi + U = 124 + 22 + 0, so \hat C = 156.

Rerouting, and what it is worth. When machine m fails, each job waiting in its queue is routed again and moved by cart if another machine now gives an earlier finish; jobs on a cart or waiting for one keep their destination. With frequent breakdowns shortest setup first moves 16.9 jobs a day out of 34.5 it re-examines. Switching rerouting off raises its mean tardiness from 34.1 to 37.5 min, and that of most urgent first from 37.6 to 39.1 (exercise 5).

Part 5

Why shortest setup first gains under breakdowns

In plain words. A changeover is machine time that produces nothing. Avoiding one gives capacity back to every job behind, and what that is worth depends on the load. In a calm shop capacity is cheap and urgency matters more. When machines fail 14 % of the time, each saved minute shortens many waits, and shortest setup first catches up.

Measured. Shortest setup first saves 107 changeover minutes a day against most urgent first without breakdowns, 195 with them, having more to choose from: two or more jobs wait at 40.4 % of its decisions, not 24.0 %.

A rough load calculation. Five machines and one job every 20.83 minutes offer 5 \times 20.83 = 104.15 machine-minutes per job; the course's load \rho = 62.5 / 104.15 = 0.60 counts only fastest-route work (B1). Under either rule a job receives 67.1 minutes of processing without breakdowns and 67.4 with them, more than 62.5 because routing balances machines (chapter B1 measures 67.3 under first come, first served). Changeovers add 17.1 minutes per job under most urgent first and 15.3 under shortest setup first in the calm shop, 16.9 and 13.7 with breakdowns. With breakdowns only a share A = 0.857 of machine time is available. The effective load is work over available capacity:

\rho_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\bar p + \bar s}{5 / \lambda} \cdot \frac{1}{A}: \qquad \begin{array}{lcc} & \text{EDD} & \text{SST} \\ \text{no breakdowns} & 0.808 & 0.791 \\ \text{frequent breakdowns} & 0.944 & 0.908 \end{array}

with A = 1 in the calm shop: 0.60 becomes about 0.8 with real routes and changeovers, and above 0.9 with 14 % of machine time lost. What are a few points of load worth? The simplest queue answers, in five steps.

The single-server queue, derived. Pretend the shop is one server, the M/M/1 queue: Poisson arrivals at rate \lambda, exponential service times of mean 1/\mu, one job served at a time, load \rho = \lambda / \mu < 1. Let N(t) be the number of jobs inside, waiting or in service. Both clocks are memoryless (B1), so N moves by single steps: up at rate \lambda (an arrival), down at rate \mu while N \ge 1 (a service ends), a birth-death process.

What is the stationary distribution of a queue?

In plain words: the share of a long run that the system spends with exactly n jobs inside.

More precisely: \pi_n = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac1T \,\big|\{t \in [0, T] : N(t) = n\}\big|, when the limits exist; \sum_n \pi_n = 1. (Not the policy \pi of D1, which carries no index.)

Step 1, balance across a cut. Cut between states n and n+1. N crosses the cut upwards only from n, at rate \lambda; downwards only from n+1, at rate \mu. Crossings alternate, so in the long run they are equally frequent: \lambda \pi_n = \mu \pi_{n+1}, that is \pi_{n+1} = \rho\, \pi_n and \pi_n = \rho^n \pi_0.

Step 2, normalise. \sum_n \rho^n \pi_0 = \pi_0 / (1 - \rho) = 1 needs \rho < 1 and gives \pi_0 = 1 - \rho: the server is idle a share 1 - \rho of the time, as the load says. So \pi_n = (1-\rho)\rho^n, a geometric distribution.

Step 3, the mean number inside. Differentiating \sum_n \rho^n = 1/(1-\rho) and multiplying by \rho gives \sum_n n \rho^n = \rho / (1-\rho)^2, so

L = \sum_{n \ge 0} n\,(1-\rho)\,\rho^n = \frac{\rho}{1-\rho}.

Step 4, Little's law. Chapter B1 proves L = \lambda \bar F for any queue, with \bar F the mean flow time (the law is usually written L = \lambda W). Hence \bar F = L / \lambda = \frac{1/\mu}{1-\rho}, and the mean wait before service is \bar F - \frac1\mu = \frac1\mu \cdot \frac{\rho}{1-\rho}: the mean service time times \rho/(1-\rho), the formula Law states [6]. The numbers script checks step 3 by summing the series (L = 4.000 at \rho = 0.8).

Step 5, what a lost minute costs. A changeover is service time that produces nothing: with \bar s minutes of it per job, \rho = \lambda(\bar p + \bar s)/(5A), and saving \Delta s minutes per job lowers the load by \lambda\, \Delta s / (5A). The cost of load is

\frac{dL}{d\rho} = \frac{1}{(1-\rho)^2},

which grows without bound as \rho \to 1: the same saved minute is worth more the busier the shop. At most urgent first's effective loads the slope is 27.2 in the calm shop and 319.3 with breakdowns, 11.7 times more.

The workshop through this lens. In the calm shop shortest setup first saves \Delta s = 1.79 changeover minutes per job against most urgent first; step 5 lowers \rho_{\text{eff}} by 1.79 / 104.15 = 0.017, and L falls from 4.22 to 3.79, by 0.43 jobs. With breakdowns it saves 3.24 minutes, a drop of 3.24 / (104.15 \times 0.857) = 0.036: L falls from 16.87 to 9.84, by 7.03 jobs, 2.17 jobs per minute saved per job against 0.24. (The calm table also holds 0.04 minutes more processing per job under most urgent first.)

A direction, not a model. The workshop is not an M/M/1 queue: five machines, four operations per job on different machines, processing times from a table rather than exponential, changeovers that depend on the order, and a day of 60 jobs that drains out instead of a steady state. In the model the mean flow times of the two rules would be in the ratio of their L, 1.12 in the calm shop and 1.71 with breakdowns; results/runs.csv gives 145.6 against 141.2 min and 202.9 against 191.0, ratios of 1.03 and 1.06. The model overstates the effect several-fold and predicts no tardiness; what carries over is the shape: the measured flow-time gap grows from 4.3 to 11.8 minutes (computed before rounding) as the load rises, because waiting grows like 1/(1-\rho) and its slope like 1/(1-\rho)^2.

The worst job tells the other half. Keeping a machine on its family makes a job of another family wait through several runs: with breakdowns shortest setup first's maximum tardiness exceeds most urgent first's by +58\ [+24, +96] min day by day.

Part 6

What a rule cannot see

A rule decides with the queue in front of it, now. Three things are outside its view.

Other machines' queues. The index uses only \mathcal Q_m(t) and machine m: it cannot prefer the job whose next machine is idle to one whose next machine is the bottleneck.

Future arrivals. Every rule here is non-delay, and a non-delay rule can lose by not waiting for a job about to arrive (A1). In A1's example the job worth waiting for is more urgent; changeovers add a second reason, a job of the current family. The paint machine, set up for blue, has only B (red, 10 min, due 25) at minute 0; A (blue, 20 min, due 30, less urgent) arrives at minute 1. Starting B pays two colour changes, total tardiness 20; idling one minute for A pays one, total 16.

Grouping families across a horizon. Shortest setup first avoids the next changeover, one greedy step at a time; avoiding them over a sequence is the travelling-salesman structure of A2. Yet the fewest changeovers is not the goal. On the six orders of m2 in the hero, shortest setup first spends 20 minutes on changeovers and is 109 minutes late in total; most urgent first spends 46 and is 135 late; the best of all 720 orders, J0 J3 J4 J1 J5 J2, spends 29, more than shortest setup first, and is 101 late.

A plan over every machine's next operations sees all three: chapters C1 and C3 build it (with frequent breakdowns, 19.3 min of mean tardiness against 34.1 for the best rule). Inside the rule, one thing can still be done: weigh several criteria at once.

Part 7

The composite index ATCS, term by term

In plain words. A composite rule multiplies three factors: favour jobs short for their importance (they free the machine soon), favour jobs about to be late, penalise jobs that need a changeover. The apparent tardiness cost with setups rule, ATCS, of Lee, Bhaskaran and Pinedo [2] extends the apparent tardiness cost rule (ATC) of Vepsalainen and Morton [5] with the changeover factor; "apparent" because the index is an estimate, made from the slack now, of the tardiness cost a job will incur if it waits. With weights w_j (all 1 in this course), when machine m is free at t and set up for family g_m:

\boxed{I_j(t) = \frac{w_j}{p_j}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{\max(\mathrm{slack}_j(t),\ 0)}{k_1 \bar p}\right) \exp\!\left(-\frac{s_m(g_m, f(j))}{k_2 \bar s}\right)}

and the largest index starts. \mathrm{slack}_j(t) = d_j - p_j - t is the slack, below; s_m(g_m, f(j)) the changeover from g_m to the family of j, \bar p the average processing time, \bar s the average changeover, k_1, k_2 > 0 two scaling parameters.

First factor, w_j / p_j: Smith's ratio. If every waiting job is certain to be late, T_j = C_j - d_j and \sum_j w_j T_j = \sum_j w_j C_j - \sum_j w_j d_j, the last sum independent of the order: weighted tardiness becomes weighted completion time, which Smith's rule [3] minimises on one machine by ordering on decreasing w_j/p_j, weighted shortest processing time (WSPT), proved by the exchange argument of A2. WSPT is the right rule for jobs already lost.

Second factor: urgency through the slack. The slack of job j at time t is \mathrm{slack}_j(t) = d_j - t - P_j(t), with P_j(t) the processing still to do on its fastest route: how long the job can still wait and finish on time. That is the shop's definition, which the learning agent of D1 uses too; on one machine, where P_j(t) = p_j, it is the ATCS slack d_j - p_j - t. If \mathrm{slack}_j \le 0 the job cannot be early (with \mathrm{slack}_j < 0 it is late whatever happens): the factor is 1 and ATCS is WSPT among such jobs, as the first factor says it should be. If \mathrm{slack}_j > 0 the job can wait, and its priority is discounted by \exp(-\mathrm{slack}_j / (k_1 \bar p)): the slack counted in average jobs, divided by k_1. A slack of k_1 average jobs keeps e^{-1} = 0.3679 of the priority.

Third factor: the changeover penalty. \exp(-s_m(g_m, f(j)) / (k_2 \bar s)) is 1 for a job of the current family and shrinks with the changeover, measured in average changeovers; k_2 sets how hard.

The parameters are scales, and their limits are simple rules (exercise 2). As k_1, k_2 \to \infty: WSPT. As k_1 \to 0, among jobs of positive slack the smallest slack wins (the minimum slack rule, close to most urgent first when processing times are similar), and any job of non-positive slack beats them all. As k_2 \to 0: shortest setup first, with ATC breaking ties. Dividing by \bar p and \bar s makes k_1, k_2 dimensionless; Lee, Bhaskaran and Pinedo give rules of thumb for them [2] (Pinedo's textbook presents ATC and ATCS [1]), which we replace by a scan. On one machine we take \bar p as the mean processing time of the orders and \bar s as the mean changeover over ordered pairs of distinct orders: for the paint machine \bar p = 17.5 and \bar s = 6.6667, since 8 of the 12 pairs need a 10-minute change.

By hand on the paint machine, k_1 = k_2 = 1. Slack is counted in units of k_1 \bar p = 17.5 min, changeovers in units of k_2 \bar s = 6.667 min, and a colour change costs the factor e^{-10/6.667} = 0.2231.

C: slack 55, \frac{1}{15} \times 0.0432 = 0.00288. D: slack 35 and a change, \frac{1}{25} \times 0.1353 \times 0.2231 = 0.00121. A starts.

Over the slider grid. On the paint machine a band of parameters reaches the optimum, from any k_2 at k_1 = 0.1 to k_2 = 2 alone at k_1 = 5. On the six orders of m2 a band from (k_1, k_2) = (0.25, 0.1) to (3, 1.5) reaches 101, the best of 720 orders, against 109 for the best single rule: the band depends on the instance.

Part 8

The code behind it

ATCS is not in the repository; its README names it as the missing stronger baseline. The four indices and the choice:

jobshop/rules.py · 16-39

def _fifo(shop: Shop, m: MachineRT, j: JobRT):
    return (j.queued_at, j.idx)


def _spt(shop: Shop, m: MachineRT, j: JobRT):
    return (shop.ws.proc(j.family, j.op, m.idx), j.idx)


def _edd(shop: Shop, m: MachineRT, j: JobRT):
    return (j.due, j.idx)


def _sst(shop: Shop, m: MachineRT, j: JobRT):
    return (shop.ws.setup_time(m.idx, m.last_family, j.family), j.due, j.idx)


# FIFO: first in the queue; SPT: shortest processing time; EDD: earliest due date;
# SST: shortest setup time (stay on the current family if possible), ties by due date.
RULES = {"FIFO": _fifo, "SPT": _spt, "EDD": _edd, "SST": _sst}


def pick(rule: str, shop: Shop, m: MachineRT) -> JobRT:
    key = RULES[rule]
    return min(m.queue, key=lambda j: key(shop, m, j))

Each rule returns a tuple and pick takes the min over the queue; Python compares tuples lexicographically, so the later entries are the tie-breaks. _sst asks for the changeover from m.last_family, which is None on a machine that has run nothing (then every changeover is 0 and the due date decides). Shop._try_start calls sequence whenever a machine is free, up and has a queue, and a RuleController always returns a job: non-delay.

The routing formula of Part 3:

jobshop/rules.py · 43-58

def workload(shop: Shop, m: MachineRT, skip: JobRT | None = None) -> int:
    """Processing time committed to a machine: queued and inbound jobs, without `skip`."""
    return sum(shop.ws.proc(j.family, j.op, m.idx) for j in m.queue + m.inbound if j is not skip)


def route_earliest_finish(shop: Shop, jr: JobRT) -> int:
    """Eligible machine with the earliest expected finish (travel + work ahead + processing)."""
    best, best_key = None, None
    for machine, proc in shop.ws.alternatives(jr.family, jr.op).items():
        m = shop.machines[machine]
        arrival = shop.now + shop.ws.travel[jr.station][shop.ws.station(machine)]
        free = shop.expected_free(m) + workload(shop, m, skip=jr)
        key = (max(arrival, free) + proc, proc, machine)
        if best_key is None or key < best_key:
            best, best_key = machine, key
    return best

workload is U_m: processing times on this machine of the jobs queued at it or travelling to it (m.queue + m.inbound), with the condition if j is not skip leaving out the job being routed. In route_earliest_finish, arrival is R_{jm}, free is \phi_m + U_m, and the key (max(arrival, free) + proc, proc, machine) is (\hat C_{jm}, p_{jkm}, m), compared lexicographically; expected_free (in shop.py) is \phi_m = \hat t^{\,\text{up}}_m + \mathrm{rem}_m. No changeover appears in the key.

jobshop/rules.py · 82-91

    def on_event(self, shop: Shop, kind: str, **info) -> None:
        if kind != "breakdown":
            return
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        m = info["machine"]
        for jr in list(m.queue):
            target = route_earliest_finish(shop, jr)
            if target != m.idx:
                shop.reroute(jr, target)
        self.decision_time_s += time.perf_counter() - t0

RuleController.route and .sequence, just above in the file, answer the simulator's two questions with route_earliest_finish and pick. on_event is the rerouting: it ignores arrivals and repairs; on a breakdown it re-routes every job in a copy of the failed machine's queue (reroute removes jobs from it) and moves a job only when the target changes.

Part 9

Common pitfalls

  1. "EDD uses the operation's due date." It uses the job's d_j for every operation.
  2. "The rule decides every time a machine frees." Only when two or more jobs wait (Part 1).
  3. "Routing accounts for changeovers." \hat C_{jm} is an estimate from processing minutes, not a schedule.
  4. "A lower mean means better on every day." Shortest setup first has the lower mean with breakdowns, wins 11 days of 20, and its paired interval contains zero (D2).
  5. "Fewer changeovers is always better." Changeovers cost capacity; they are not the objective (Part 6).
Part 10

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question before opening it.

1. What makes an index static, and why does it matter?

It depends only on the job's data. On one machine without arrivals the dispatch order is then a sort; a dynamic index must be recomputed at every decision.

2. Why is shortest setup first dynamic?

Its index is the changeover from the machine's current family, which changes with every job started.

3. Write the routing key, and say what it leaves out.

\hat C_{jm} = \max(t + \tau(b_j, m+1),\ \phi_m + U_m) + p_{jkm}, ties by p_{jkm} then m. It leaves out changeovers, the real service order of the queue, the wait for a cart and the real repair time.

4. When a machine fails, which jobs can move?

Only those waiting in its queue. Jobs on a cart or waiting for one keep their destination; nothing is rerouted at a repair.

5. Why does most urgent first have the smallest maximum tardiness?

On one machine, all jobs present at the start and no changeovers, earliest due date minimises the maximum lateness (Jackson [4]); in the shop the bias remains.

6. Give two reasons why shortest setup first gains on most urgent first under breakdowns.

Capacity is scarcer, so a saved changeover minute shortens more waits (the slope 1/(1-\rho)^2 of Part 5); and queues are longer (40.4 % of its decisions with a choice, not 24.0 %), so it saves more: 195 minutes a day, not 107.

7. In ATCS, what happens to a job with negative slack?

Its urgency factor is 1: among such jobs ATCS ranks by w_j/p_j times the changeover factor, Smith's rule for jobs already lost.

8. What do k_1 \to 0 and k_2 \to 0 turn ATCS into?

k_1 \to 0: minimum slack, jobs of non-positive slack first. k_2 \to 0: shortest setup first, with ATC breaking ties.

Part 11

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. The coding exercises use the repository and its .venv; they do not modify it.

  1. Dispatching a static index is sorting. On one machine, every job present at the first decision and no arrivals, a non-delay rule dispatches by an index I_j, ties broken by the job number. (a) Prove that if the index is static, the dispatched sequence is the list of the jobs sorted by (I_j, j). (b) Show that this fails for ATCS: on the paint machine with k_1 = k_2 = 1, sort the orders by their index at t = 0, compare with the dispatched order, and say which index changed rank and why. (c) Deduce that dispatching shortest job first on one machine without changeovers minimises \sum_j C_j.

    Hint

    (a) Induction on the number of decisions: which jobs are in the queue at the k-th one? (b) The indices at every decision are in Part 7.

    Solution

    (a) Let (l_1, \dots, l_n) be the jobs sorted by the key (I_j, j); job numbers are distinct, so the key orders the jobs strictly and the list is unique. Claim: the k-th job started is l_k. Suppose the first k-1 were l_1, \dots, l_{k-1} (nothing to prove for k = 1). There are no arrivals and every decision starts one job, so at the k-th decision the queue is exactly \{l_k, \dots, l_n\}. The index is static, so every key is what it was at the first decision, and the smallest key in the queue is that of l_k, uniquely. By induction the sequence is the sorted list.

    (b) At t = 0 the indices are A 0.02824, B 0.00947, C 0.00288, D 0.00121: sorted, A B C D, 55 minutes of tardiness. Dispatched: A B D C, 40 minutes. D overtakes C at minute 40: time has consumed D's slack, and the machine, now red, owes D no changeover, so D's index has risen from 0.00121 to 0.04000 while C's is 0.00631. (On both hero queues SST's sort at t = 0 happens to equal its dispatch: a dynamic index may give a sort.)

    (c) The index p_j of shortest job first is static, so by (a) dispatching produces the SPT sequence, which minimises \sum_j C_j without changeovers by Smith's theorem (A2). With arrivals the queue at a decision is no longer the rest of the sorted list, and the argument fails.

  2. The limits of ATCS. Prove: (a) as k_1 \to 0, among jobs of positive slack the smallest slack wins, and a job of non-positive slack beats any job of positive slack; (b) as k_2 \to 0, a strictly smaller changeover wins; (c) as k_1, k_2 \to \infty, ATCS becomes WSPT.

    Hint

    Write the ratio I_a / I_b and see which factor goes to 0 or \infty while the others stay bounded.

    Solution

    With x_a = \max(\mathrm{slack}_a, 0), I_a / I_b = \frac{w_a p_b}{w_b p_a} \exp\!\big(-\frac{x_a - x_b}{k_1 \bar p}\big) \exp\!\big(-\frac{s_m(g_m, f(a)) - s_m(g_m, f(b))}{k_2 \bar s}\big). (a) For fixed k_2 the outer factors are positive constants; if x_a < x_b the middle one tends to +\infty as k_1 \to 0, which covers both 0 < \mathrm{slack}_a < \mathrm{slack}_b and \mathrm{slack}_a \le 0 < \mathrm{slack}_b. (b) For fixed k_1, if s_m(g_m, f(a)) < s_m(g_m, f(b)) the last factor tends to +\infty as k_2 \to 0. (c) Both exponentials tend to 1, and I_a/I_b \to (w_a/p_a)/(w_b/p_b).

  3. ATCS by hand with another parameter. Compute ATCS on the paint machine with k_1 = 2, k_2 = 1. Which order results, and why does C overtake B at minute 20?

    Hint

    Now k_1 \bar p = 35; the changeover scale is still 6.667 min. At minute 20 B's slack is negative and C's is 35.

    Solution

    At t = 0: A \frac{1}{20} \times e^{-10/35} = \frac{1}{20} \times 0.7515 = 0.03757, B 0.01454, C 0.01385, D 0.00328: A. At t = 20, still blue: B \frac{1}{10} \times 0.2231 = 0.02231; C's slack is exactly one urgency scale, \frac{1}{15} \times e^{-35/35} = \frac{1}{15} \times 0.3679 = 0.02453, and it needs no change. C overtakes B: the doubled k_1 makes C's slack look small, and B pays the colour change. At t = 35, still blue: B 0.02231 against D 0.00893, so B, then D. The order is A C B D, 50 minutes, shortest setup first's order.

  4. Implement ATCS in the shop (coding). Write an ATCSController with the routing and rerouting of RuleController and sequencing by ATCS. Use the shop slack \mathrm{slack}_j(t) = d_j - t - P_j(t), counting the operation at hand at its time on m and the operations after it on their fastest machines. Choose (k_1, k_2) on the validation days 5000–5009 only, then run the 20 test days at both levels, check every schedule with check_schedule, and compare with the best rule day by day.

    Hint

    Subclass RuleController and override sequence. Use shop.ws.min_remaining_work(j.family, j.op + 1). Take \bar p over the queue and \bar s as the machine's mean off-diagonal changeover, a scale that stays fixed while the queue changes. Score (k_1, k_2) by the mean tardiness over both levels on the validation days.

    Solution

    A sketch; the full version, with the grid search, is in code/b02_numbers.py.

    import math, numpy as np
from jobshop.rules import RuleController
from jobshop.scenario import make_workshop

WS = make_workshop()
SBAR = [np.mean([WS.setup[m][f][g] for f in range(4) for g in range(4) if f != g]) for m in range(5)]

class ATCSController(RuleController):
    def __init__(self, k1, k2):
        super().__init__("SST"); self.name, self.k1, self.k2 = "ATCS", k1, k2
    def sequence(self, shop, m):
        if len(m.queue) == 1:
            return m.queue[0]
        p = {j.idx: shop.ws.proc(j.family, j.op, m.idx) for j in m.queue}
        pbar = np.mean(list(p.values()))
        def index(j):
            slack = j.due - shop.ws.min_remaining_work(j.family, j.op + 1) - p[j.idx] - shop.now
            s = shop.ws.setup_time(m.idx, m.last_family, j.family)
            return math.exp(-max(slack, 0) / (self.k1 * pbar)) * math.exp(-s / (self.k2 * SBAR[m.idx])) / p[j.idx]
        return max(m.queue, key=lambda j: (index(j), -j.idx))

On the test days every schedule passes the checker. Part 12

The chapter on one page

  1. A rule is an index I_j(t), computed when a machine frees: local, non-delay. FIFO, SPT, EDD are static; SST is dynamic.
  2. Routing: \hat C_{jm} = \max(t + \tau(b_j, m+1),\ \phi_m + U_m) + p_{jkm}, with MTTR for the repair and no changeovers; rerouting after a failure.
  3. Results: EDD best without breakdowns; with them SST has the lower mean, from changeovers saved near saturation (the M/M/1 slope 1/(1-\rho)^2), but an uncertain lead; EDD keeps the smallest worst case.
  4. Blind spots: other queues, future arrivals, grouping across a horizon.
  5. ATCS: Smith's ratio, urgency by slack, changeover penalty; limits WSPT, minimum slack, SST; not significantly better than the best rule in the shop.

The vocabulary, in one table

Word Meaning
Dispatching rule A local recipe picking the next job of a free machine from its queue.
Priority index I_j(t) A job's score at a decision; smallest first (ATCS: largest).
Static, dynamic index Fixed while the job waits; or changing with t or the set-up.
Non-delay A free machine with a waiting job never stays idle.
Earliest expected finish \hat C_{jm} \max(R_{jm}, \phi_m + U_m) + p_{jkm}, minimised over the eligible machines; U_m the committed work.
M/M/1 queue One server, Poisson arrivals, exponential service; L = \rho/(1-\rho) from the birth-death balance.
Rerouting Routing again the queue of a machine that fails.
Slack \mathrm{slack}_j(t) d_j - t - P_j(t): how long the job can wait and be on time; d_j - p_j - t on one machine.
WSPT, minimum slack Largest w_j/p_j first; smallest slack first.
ATC, ATCS Apparent tardiness cost (with setups); k_1, k_2 its urgency and changeover scales.

Scope. Local, non-delay rules with the repository's routing, on one synthetic workshop at one load; rules that look at the next machine's queue are not covered, and learning which rule to use is chapter D1.

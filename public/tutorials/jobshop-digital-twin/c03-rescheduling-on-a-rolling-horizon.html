Rescheduling on a rolling horizon

A plan is out of date as soon as an order arrives or a machine stops. This chapter turns the planner of chapters C1 and C2 into a controller: at every event it photographs the shop, decides what is settled, plans again, and carries out only the beginning of the new plan. You will be able to write that snapshot down, prove when re-planning cannot make the plan worse, measure how much consecutive plans differ, and say how much of the gain comes from re-planning and how much from the solver.

Part 1

A plan is a forecast; a controller re-forecasts

Chapters C1 and C2 built a planner: given the orders in the shop, it returns the best plan it can find. But a plan is made from what is known at one instant; minutes later an order arrives, or a machine stops, and the plan is a stale forecast.

The remedy is the one a navigation app uses: make the plan again whenever the world changes, and only ever act on its first steps. Used this way, a planner becomes a controller: a rule that maps what the shop looks like now to what the machines do next.

The paint machine with a surprise. The paint machine of chapter A2 is set up for blue, needs 10 minutes per colour change, and has four orders at minute 0; their best order is A B D C, 40 minutes of total tardiness. A fifth order E arrives at minute 35: blue, 5 minutes of painting, due at 50. Three ways to run the machine:

  • Plan once. Keep A B D C and append E: A 0–20, a changeover, B 30–40 (15 late), D 40–65 (5 late), a changeover, C 75–90 (20 late), E 90–95 (45 late): 85 minutes.
  • Re-plan when E arrives. B started its changeover at minute 20, so it is frozen: the machine is free at 40, set up for red. The best of the six orders of C, D and E (Exercise 1) is E C D: E 50–55 (5 late), C 55–70, D 80–105 (45 late). With B's 15: 65 minutes.
  • Clairvoyant. Knowing E from the start, the best of all 120 orders is A C E B D: A 0–20, C 20–35, E 35–40, B 50–60 (35 late), D 60–85 (25 late): 60 minutes. It keeps the machine blue for E and sacrifices B.

Re-planning recovers 20 of the 25 minutes that foresight would save. The other 5 are the price of myopia: B went on the machine before anyone knew about E. Make E red and re-planning reaches the clairvoyant optimum, A B E D C with 50 minutes. Move E's arrival earlier and the gap closes: less is frozen when the news arrives.

Part 2

The snapshot of a running shop

To plan again, the controller needs the shop's state in the terms of chapter C1. At minute 117, where the hero opens, order 0 is on a cart to m0: where and when it arrives are settled. Order 2 waits at m4, but may still be sent to m1.

What is a snapshot?

In plain words: a photograph of the shop at an event, in the planner's language: what each order still has to do, where it may go next and how soon it can be there, and when each machine is free.

More precisely: the snapshot \Sigma_t at time t lists, for each machine, the time \phi_m \ge t it is expected to be free and the family g_m it is set up for, and, for each order j with a decision left, its first undecided operation \kappa_j, the machines \mathcal R_j \subseteq \mathcal M_{j \kappa_j} it may still start on and, for each m \in \mathcal R_j, the ready time R_{jm}, the earliest minute it can be at m. The model of chapter C1 is solved for the operations (j, k), k \ge \kappa_j, with (j, \kappa_j) restricted to \mathcal R_j and arriving no earlier than R_{jm} (C1).

Frozen
no longer changeable by a new plan: an operation already running, the destination of an order on or waiting for a cart.
Free to change
everything else; an order queued at a machine may still be sent to another eligible machine.
Expected free time
\phi_m uses the mean repair time for a broken machine; the real one is hidden.

Each of the five states of an order (JobState in jobshop/shop.py) gives its ready times a different form. Write \operatorname{st}_j for the station of order j, \operatorname{st}(m) = m + 1 for that of machine m (the load station is 0), \delta = 2 for the cart allowance, and \ell(m', m) for the transport lag of chapter C1: 0 if m = m', else \delta + \tau(\operatorname{st}(m'), \operatorname{st}(m)).

State First operation to plan Machines \mathcal R_j Ready time R_{jm}
new, at the load station k all of \mathcal M_{jk} t + \delta + \tau(\operatorname{st}_j, \operatorname{st}(m))
queued at a machine k all of \mathcal M_{jk} t if \operatorname{st}(m) = \operatorname{st}_j, else t + \delta + \tau(\operatorname{st}_j, \operatorname{st}(m))
waiting for a cart k its destination only t + \delta + \tau(\operatorname{st}_j, \operatorname{st}(\text{dest}))
on a cart k its destination only the cart's arrival time
processing on m' k + 1, none if k is the last all of \mathcal M_{j, k+1} \phi_{m'} + \ell(m', m)

The operation in progress is not in the plan: it appears only through \phi_{m'}. On the machine side,

\phi_m = \hat t^{\,\text{up}}_m + \mathrm{rem}_m, \qquad \hat t^{\,\text{up}}_m = \begin{cases} t & \text{if } m \text{ works,} \\ \max\big(\operatorname{round}(t^{\text{down}}_m + \text{MTTR}),\ t + 1\big) & \text{if } m \text{ is down since } t^{\text{down}}_m, \end{cases}

the expected free time of chapter B2, with \mathrm{rem}_m what remains of the operation in progress, its changeover included (0 if idle); t + 1 covers a repair that has outlasted MTTR.

Read one by hand. The minute-117 snapshot has fewer orders on purpose than snapshot 101 of chapters C1 and C2 (minute 891, 8 orders), though the same 19 operations to place: its 6 orders can be read line by line. The travel times used are \tau(1, 2) = \tau(4, 1) = \tau(4, 3) = \tau(0, 1) = 2 and \tau(5, 2) = 3.

  • Order 0, on a cart to m0 for its last operation: frozen, R_{0,0} = 117, the cart's arrival.
  • Order 4, at m0 (station 1), waiting for a cart to m1: frozen destination, R_{4,1} = 117 + 2 + \tau(1, 2) = 121.
  • Order 2, queued at m4 (station 5): R_{2,4} = 117 if it stays, R_{2,1} = 117 + 2 + \tau(5, 2) = 122.
  • Order 3, queued at m3 (station 4), which is down: R_{3,3} = 117, since it stands there; 117 + 2 + 2 = 121 on m0 or m2.
  • Order 1, processed on m4 until 120; its next operation runs only on m4: R_{1,4} = 120.
  • Order 5, new; its first operation runs only on m0: R_{5,0} = 117 + 2 + \tau(0, 1) = 121.

The machines: \phi = (117, 117, 136, 129, 120), since m2 broke at 116 and is expected at 116 + 20, m3 at 109 + 20. CP-SAT returns the plan \Pi_{117} with objective 1,194 (not proven optimal), against 1,227 for the greedy warm start; it keeps order 3 on m3 (a 4-minute changeover, start at 133).

Part 3

Re-planning at events, executing the beginning

What is event-driven re-planning on a rolling horizon?

In plain words: every time something happens (an arrival, a breakdown, a repair), take a snapshot, make a new plan for all the orders in the shop, and follow it until the next event replaces it. Only the beginning of each plan is ever carried out.

More precisely: at each event time t_1 \le t_2 \le \dots the controller takes the snapshot \Sigma_{t_i} of the orders \mathcal J_i (at most the 25 most urgent, Part 5) and computes a plan

\Pi_{t_i} \in \mathcal P(\Sigma_{t_i}), \qquad J(\Pi_{t_i}) \le J(\Pi^{\text{warm}}_{t_i}), \qquad J(\Pi) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal J_i} \big(10\, T_j(\Pi) + C_j(\Pi)\big),

with \mathcal P(\Sigma) the plans consistent with the snapshot and \Pi^{\text{warm}}_{t_i} the greedy warm start of Part 4. \Pi_{t_i} is the best plan CP-SAT finds within its budget, a minimiser of J over \mathcal P(\Sigma_{t_i}) when proven optimal (chapter C2 counts how often on the benchmark; on the hero's morning, 34 of the 37 non-empty plans). The shop follows \Pi_{t_i} on [t_i, t_{i+1}).

Receding horizon
plan far, act near, plan again; the plan's window moves with time.
Event-driven
re-planning is triggered by events, not by a clock.
Plan drift
what happens differently from the plan before the next event.

The idea from control. This is the receding-horizon principle of model predictive control, taught in the MPC course: an open-loop optimiser becomes a feedback law because it is re-run on the measured state [2]. Here the state is discrete; the trigger is an event, since between events nothing changes that the plan did not predict except the carts' timing (Part 6); and each plan runs every known order to completion.

How many re-plans a day? One per arrival, breakdown and repair: without breakdowns, exactly the 60 arrivals, on every test day. With breakdowns, each machine fails about once per MTBF + MTTR = 140 minutes in the long run (chapter B1 counts the cycles), and each failure brings two events. Over a day of length C_{\max} = 1415, the mean makespan of the CP-SAT runs with breakdowns,

\mathbb E[\text{re-plans}] \approx n + 2 \cdot 5 \cdot \frac{C_{\max}}{\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR}} = 60 + 10 \cdot \frac{1415}{140} = 161.1 ,

against 158.9 recorded on average (from 137 to 177). The hero's morning has 43 events in 314 minutes, one every 7.3 minutes: 12 arrivals, 16 breakdowns, 15 repairs, more than the formula predicts (Exercise 4); the 6 before the first order (minute 51) give empty plans.

Going further: is the shortfall of 2.2 re-plans real?

The days differ by 11.4 re-plans (standard deviation). Compared day by day with its own prediction 60 + 10 \cdot C_{\max,i}/140, the count falls short by 2.2 on average, with a standard error of 2.5: within one standard error, so noise explains the gap.

Executing the beginning: strict plan following. Between events, the plan answers the simulator's two questions (chapter B1): an order goes to its planned machine, and a free machine starts the next order of its planned sequence; if that order is not there yet, the machine waits for it, even with others in its queue, so as not to break the plan's grouping of families. The repository chose this on six validation seeds with CP-SAT, before the test: mean tardiness 8.2 against 9.0 minutes.

Orders the plan does not know, left out by the cap (Part 5), are routed by earliest expected finish (chapter B2); a waiting machine starts one only if it fits, ending by the planned start of the next planned operation. The test ignores the changeover back, up to 10 minutes of possible delay. On the benchmark the cap never binds.

Part 4

Greedy list scheduling: warm start and ablation

What is greedy list scheduling?

In plain words: look at the next unplaced operation of every order, work out for each eligible machine when it would start and finish there, place the best by a simple preference, and repeat. No choice is revisited.

More precisely: with \phi_m and g_m from the snapshot, a candidate (operation (j, k), machine m) gets

S = \max(\phi_m,\ \alpha_{jk}) + s_m(g_m, f(j)), \qquad E = S + p_{jkm},

with the arrival \alpha_{jk} = R_{jm} for the first operation and \alpha_{jk} = E_{j,k-1} + \ell(\mu(j, k-1), m) afterwards, \mu(j, k-1) the machine already chosen for the previous operation. A preference key picks one candidate; it is placed, \phi_m becomes E, g_m becomes f(j), and the order's next operation becomes a candidate.

"finish"
earliest end first.
"start"
earliest start first, then earliest due date.
"edd"
most urgent order first.
"previous"
the previous plan's machines and order, re-timed; new operations afterwards by earliest end.

The cost. N placement steps (N operations to plan, 19 here), each scanning at most n orders on at most three machines: \mathcal O(3Nn), 0.47 ms per re-plan without breakdowns and 0.51 ms with them. As the warm start, warm_start hands the best of the greedy plans to CP-SAT as a complete hint (chapter C2, [3]) and keeps it as the fallback; on the hero's morning CP-SAT beat it in 14 of the 37 plans, by 211 objective units in total and 33 at most. As the ablation, quick re-planning (Greedy replan) is the same controller with the solver removed (use_solver=False), which separates what re-planning does from what the solver adds.

Why carry the previous plan forward? Because re-timing it cannot hurt when nothing went worse than predicted.

Proposition. Let \Pi be a plan made for snapshot \Sigma, and \Sigma' a later snapshot that contains no order \Pi did not plan, in which every ready time and every machine's free time is no later than \Pi assumed and every machine is set up as \Pi expected. Re-time \Pi on \Sigma', keeping each operation's machine and each machine's order. Then no operation starts later than in \Pi, and J over the orders of \Sigma' does not increase.

Proof. With machines and machine orders fixed, the constraints of \Sigma' have the two forms of the left-shift theorem (A1): bounds b'_o built from \phi'_m, R'_{jm} and changeovers, and predecessor arcs whose constants are lags and changeovers. By assumption b'_o \le b_o; the constants are those of \Sigma, since a lag depends only on the two machines and a changeover only on the family before, which is unchanged. So \Pi's timetable meets every constraint of \Sigma'. The "previous" key places the operations in increasing order of their start in \Pi, which respects each machine's order and each order's sequence, each at \max(\phi_m, \alpha_{jk}) + s_m(g_m, f(j)), its tightest bound: it computes the earliest-start timetable (LS) of \Pi's machines and orders. By the theorem no operation starts or ends later than in \Pi, so every C'_j \le C_j, and J is a regular measure (A1). \square

Since solve_snapshot returns a plan no worse than the warm start, itself no worse than the re-timed plan, in the nominal case the new plan costs no more than the old one. With an arrival, the solver minimises the total, newcomer included, and may make old orders later, as D in Part 1.

Checked by the numbers script: re-timed on the next snapshot with no new order, the plan never cost more at the 7 repairs no later than MTTR; it did at 2 of the 6 later repairs (by 6 and 13 units) and at 10 of the 12 breakdowns, outside the nominal case.

Part 5

The 25-job cap

The model of chapter C1 grows faster than the shop. A fresh order brings an optional interval per eligible machine of each operation: 9, 7, 10 and 8 for families 0 to 3. Worse, a machine with c candidate operations has (c + 1)^2 arcs in its circuit (chapter C1 counts them), one Boolean literal each. One fresh order per family gives the machines q = (6, 7, 8, 6, 7) candidates; n fresh orders in an even mix (n a multiple of 4) give c_m = n\, q_m / 4 and exactly \sum_m (c_m + 1)^2 = 14.625\, n^2 + 17 n + 5 arcs: 1,077 for 8 orders, 8,837 for 24, 24,085 for 40. With a fixed deterministic budget (chapter C2), a larger model gets less search per variable.

The controller therefore plans at most the 25 most urgent orders. On the benchmark the cap never binds: no CP-SAT re-plan is capped, and the greedy runs never held more than 16 orders at an event without breakdowns, 21 with them. A tighter cap, with the greedy planner on the test days (an illustration):

Part 6

What a plan cannot see

Myopia. J sums over the orders in the shop; an order arriving in ten minutes counts for nothing, so a plan may leave a machine set up for the wrong family, as with E in Part 1. Exercise 5 measures the opposite: what is lost by re-planning less often.

Certainty equivalence. A broken machine is expected back after exactly MTTR = 20 minutes; a real repair lasts from 10 to 30. Say a repair takes 10 or 30 minutes with probability 1/2 each, and an order is planned to finish exactly at its due date behind it: its tardiness is 0 or 10, expected 5, against a planned 0. The early case cannot make up for the late one, because tardiness stops at 0. Replacing a random quantity by its mean inside an optimisation is certainty equivalence; for tardiness it is optimistic.

The general statement is Jensen's inequality: for a convex function \varphi and a random variable X, \mathbb E[\varphi(X)] \ge \varphi(\mathbb E[X]). For two values it is the definition of convexity, the chord above the curve; the general case we cite [4]. Why is tardiness convex in a repair duration D? In the planner's model (fixed machine sequences, fixed lags), every start and end is computed from free times, ready times and durations by + and \max only. Unfolded, a completion time is the largest, over chains of operations, of a starting time plus the durations along the chain. D enters only through the broken machine's free time \phi_m = t^{\text{down}}_m + D + \mathrm{rem}_m, and a chain starts there or not, so it contains D once with coefficient 1 or not at all. Hence C_j(D) = \max(c_1, c_2 + D) for two constants: convex and nondecreasing. Then T_j = \max(0, C_j - d_j) is convex too, and

\mathbb E\big[T_j(D)\big] \ \ge\ T_j\big(\mathbb E[D]\big) .

A plan's tardiness is a lower bound on what that plan should expect (for the plan, not exactly for the shop, where the order of events can itself change with D). With D uniform on [10, 30], the order planned to finish exactly at its due date expects

\mathbb E[\max(0, D - 20)] = \int_{20}^{30} \frac{x - 20}{20}\, dx = \frac{10^2}{40} = 2.5 \text{ minutes},

and (10 - \eta)^2 / 40 with a margin of \eta minutes (Exercise 3).

The carts are a fixed lag. The model has no carts (AGVs): a move is a lag \tau(a, b) + \delta, as if a cart always came 2 minutes after being called. In the simulator they are real resources. Over the 20 test days with breakdowns (greedy re-planning, 5,000 loaded moves) the wait for a cart has median 2 minutes, where \delta comes from, but mean 2.91 and 90th percentile 7: 24 % of moves do not wait and 48 % wait more than 2 minutes.

Going further: how far does the shop drift from the plan?

Compare each operation's actual start with its start in the last plan made before it. On the hero's morning, of the 36 operations started before minute 314, 28 started exactly on time, 7 early and 1 late, never by more than 2 minutes (mean −0.31). Drift is reset at the next event: 7.3 minutes later on average on this morning, 8.9 over a day with breakdowns (1,415 minutes for 158.9 events) and 22.4 without. So the fixed-lag approximation costs little here.

Part 7

Nervousness

Step the hero from event 21 to event 22 (minute 117, order 5 arrives) with the outline on: of the 15 operations in both plans, 4 change machine, and planned starts move by 4.2 minutes on average. In a real workshop each change costs something the simulator does not charge: operators prepare the next order. Planners call a schedule that changes too often nervous, its opposite stable [1].

How is nervousness measured?

In plain words: compare each new plan with the one it replaces, on the operations in both: how many moved to another machine, how far their planned start moved, and on how many machines the next job changed.

More precisely: for consecutive plans \Pi_{i-1}, \Pi_i (short for \Pi_{t_{i-1}}, \Pi_{t_i}) with common operations \mathcal O_i, machines m_i(o) and starts S_i(o),

\begin{aligned} \nu^{\text{mach}}_i &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal O_i|} \sum_{o \in \mathcal O_i} \mathbb 1\big[m_i(o) \ne m_{i-1}(o)\big], \\ \nu^{\text{start}}_i &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal O_i|} \sum_{o \in \mathcal O_i} \big|S_i(o) - S_{i-1}(o)\big|, \end{aligned}

pooled over a day with weights |\mathcal O_i|, together with the share of machines whose first planned operation after t_i changed.

On the hero's morning, 35 pairs of consecutive plans share 332 operations. CP-SAT moved 47 to another machine (14.2 %), shifted planned starts by 3.7 minutes on average and changed the next operation on 35 of the 152 (machine, re-plan) pairs (23 %); 6 pairs changed nothing. The greedy controller, on its own run of the same morning, moved 17.2 %, shifted starts by 4.9 minutes and changed the next operation on 26 % of the machines.

Going further: nervousness by kind of event

After a repair, usually the nominal case of Part 4, the CP-SAT plan moved none of 123 shared operations and shifted starts by 1.2 minutes; after a breakdown it moved 26 of 121 and shifted them by 5.9 minutes; after an arrival, 21 of 88 and 4.3 minutes.

Nothing in J charges for these changes. Two standard remedies, not in the repository, trade tardiness for stability: a penalty on deviations from the previous plan, or a time fence freezing every operation that starts within [t, t + h].

Part 8

Results: where the gain comes from

On the 20 test days, mean tardiness in minutes from results/runs.csv: the best rule 7.0 (most urgent first, EDD) without breakdowns and 34.1 (shortest setup first, SST) with them, quick re-planning 3.1 and 24.0, optimiser re-planning 1.3 and 19.3. Methods are compared day by day on paired differences \Delta_i, with 95 % bootstrap intervals (chapter D2). On each day the total gain splits exactly in two,

\underbrace{\bar T^{\text{CP-SAT}}_i - \bar T^{\text{rule}}_i}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{\big(\bar T^{\text{greedy}}_i - \bar T^{\text{rule}}_i\big)}_{\text{re-planning}} + \underbrace{\big(\bar T^{\text{CP-SAT}}_i - \bar T^{\text{greedy}}_i\big)}_{\text{the solver}} ,

so the means split exactly too; the intervals do not add.

Most of the gain is re-planning: −3.94 of −5.68 minutes without breakdowns (69 %), −10.12 of −14.82 with them (68 %). The solver's part is real, both its intervals exclude zero, but it costs 1.61 seconds per re-plan against half a millisecond, about 3,000 times as long (wall clock, indicative). Against the best rule, CP-SAT re-planning cuts mean tardiness by 81 % and 43 %, greedy re-planning by 56 % and 30 %.

A visible part of what re-planning buys is fewer changeovers: from 1,025 minutes a day (EDD) to 633 with greedy re-planning and 635 with CP-SAT without breakdowns, from 820 (SST) to 618 and 633 with them. A plan over all the orders can group families; a rule looking at one queue cannot. How much of the gain this explains, the totals do not say; under breakdowns CP-SAT spends more on changeovers than the greedy plan and is still less late.

The widget shows what averages hide: greedy re-planning loses to the rule on 2 days without breakdowns and 3 with them, the solver loses to the greedy plan on 1 and 3, yet the total is a gain on all 20 days. The hero's 12-order morning, run to completion, even ends with the greedy controller ahead, 0.17 against 0.33 minutes: one short run is an anecdote.

Part 9

The code behind it

The snapshot is a direct transcription of the table of Part 2.

jobshop/cpsat.py · take_snapshot

def take_snapshot(shop: Shop, jobs: list[JobRT], allowance: int) -> Snapshot:
    ws = shop.ws
    machine_free = [shop.expected_free(m) for m in shop.machines]
    machine_family = [m.last_family for m in shop.machines]
    plan_jobs = []
    for jr in jobs:
        alternatives = ws.alternatives(jr.family, jr.op)
        if jr.state is JobState.PROCESSING:
            if jr.op + 1 == jr.n_ops:
                continue  # on its last operation: nothing left to decide
            nxt = ws.alternatives(jr.family, jr.op + 1)
            ready = {k: machine_free[jr.dest] + transport_lag(ws, jr.dest, k, allowance) for k in nxt}
            plan_jobs.append(PlanJob(jr.idx, jr.family, jr.due, jr.op + 1, ready))
            continue
        if jr.state is JobState.IN_TRANSIT:
            ready = {jr.dest: jr.eta}  # destination and arrival time are settled
        elif jr.state is JobState.WAIT_TRANSPORT:
            ready = {jr.dest: shop.now + allowance + ws.travel[jr.station][ws.station(jr.dest)]}
        else:  # QUEUED at a machine (it may move to another one) or NEW at its current station
            ready = {
                k: shop.now if ws.station(k) == jr.station else shop.now + allowance + ws.travel[jr.station][ws.station(k)]
                for k in alternatives
            }
        plan_jobs.append(PlanJob(jr.idx, jr.family, jr.due, jr.op, ready))
    return Snapshot(shop.now, machine_free, machine_family, plan_jobs)

machine_free is \phi_m, machine_family is g_m, ready holds the R_{jm}. IN_TRANSIT keeps the destination at the cart's arrival jr.eta; the last branch, QUEUED or NEW, is where rerouting lives: every eligible machine, at shop.now if the order stands there.

The controller re-plans in on_event, called at every arrival, breakdown and repair.

jobshop/cpsat.py · CPSATController.on_event

    def on_event(self, shop: Shop, kind: str, **info) -> None:
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        jobs = sorted(shop.active_jobs(), key=lambda j: (j.due, j.idx))
        if len(jobs) > self.max_jobs:  # most urgent jobs are planned, the others follow EDD
            jobs = jobs[: self.max_jobs]
            self.n_capped += 1
        snap = take_snapshot(shop, jobs, self.allowance)
        plan = solve_snapshot(
            shop.ws, snap, allowance=self.allowance, det_time=self.det_time,
            workers=self.workers, seed=self.seed, previous_plan=self.plan,
            use_warm_start=self.use_warm_start, use_solver=self.use_solver,
        )
        self.n_replans += 1
        self.n_optimal += plan.status == "OPTIMAL"
        self.n_greedy += plan.status == "GREEDY"
        if plan.ops or not snap.jobs:
            self.plan = plan.ops
            self.sequence_of = {}
            for (job, op), (machine, start, _) in plan.ops.items():
                self.sequence_of.setdefault(machine, []).append((start, job, op))
            for seq in self.sequence_of.values():
                seq.sort()
        self.decision_time_s += time.perf_counter() - t0
        # queued jobs the new plan wants elsewhere are moved, then idle machines look again
        for m in shop.machines:
            for jr in list(m.queue):
                target = self.plan.get((jr.idx, jr.op))
                if target is not None and target[0] != m.idx:
                    shop.reroute(jr, target[0])
        shop.kick()

Orders are sorted by due date and cut to max_jobs; solve_snapshot receives the previous plan for the "previous" greedy key; sequence_of stores each machine's planned operations by start. The last loop is the only physical action of a re-plan: shop.reroute sends a queued order where the new plan wants it.

Executing the beginning of the plan is sequence, called whenever a free machine has a queue.

jobshop/cpsat.py · CPSATController.sequence

    def sequence(self, shop: Shop, m: MachineRT) -> JobRT | None:
        seq = self.sequence_of.get(m.idx, [])
        while seq and self._started(shop, seq[0]):
            seq.pop(0)
        in_queue = {(j.idx, j.op): j for j in m.queue}
        if self.strict and seq:
            # follow the planned order on the machine, waiting for the next planned job if needed
            start, job, op = seq[0]
            if (job, op) in in_queue:
                return in_queue[(job, op)]
            unplanned = [j for j in m.queue if (j.idx, j.op) not in self.plan]
            fits = [
                j for j in unplanned
                if shop.now + shop.ws.setup_time(m.idx, m.last_family, j.family) + shop.ws.proc(j.family, j.op, m.idx) <= start
            ]
            return min(fits, key=lambda j: (j.due, j.idx)) if fits else None
        for _, job, op in seq:  # non-delay: earliest planned job among those present
            if (job, op) in in_queue:
                return in_queue[(job, op)]
        return min(m.queue, key=lambda j: (j.due, j.idx))

With strict, only the head of the machine's planned order counts: start it if it is in the queue, else the most urgent unplanned order that fits before the head's planned start, else None: wait. fits omits the changeover back, the gap of Part 3.

The loop of greedy_plan, warm start and ablation:

    family = list(snap.machine_family)
    next_op = {job.idx: job.first_op for job in snap.jobs}
    last_done: dict[int, tuple[int, int]] = {}  # job -> (machine, end) of its last scheduled operation
    ops: dict[tuple[int, int], tuple[int, int, int]] = {}
    todo = {job.idx: job for job in snap.jobs}
    while todo:
        best = None
        for job in todo.values():
            op = next_op[job.idx]
            for k, p in ws.alternatives(job.family, op).items():
                if op == job.first_op:
                    if k not in job.ready:
                        continue
                    arrive = job.ready[k]
                else:
                    k_prev, end_prev = last_done[job.idx]
                    arrive = end_prev + transport_lag(ws, k_prev, k, allowance)
                start = max(free[k], arrive) + ws.setup_time(k, family[k], job.family)
                end = start + p
                if rule == "finish":
                    key = (end, job.due, job.idx, k)
                elif rule == "start":
                    key = (start, job.due, end, job.idx, k)
                elif rule == "edd":
                    key = (job.due, job.idx, end, k)
                else:
                    old = previous_plan.get((job.idx, op)) if previous_plan else None
                    key = (0, old[1], job.idx, k) if old is not None and old[0] == k else (1, end, job.idx, k)
                if best is None or key < best[0]:
                    best = (key, job, op, k, start, end)
        _, job, op, k, start, end = best
        ops[(job.idx, op)] = (k, start, end)
        free[k], family[k] = end, job.family
        last_done[job.idx] = (k, end)
        next_op[job.idx] += 1
        if next_op[job.idx] == ws.n_ops(job.family):
            del todo[job.idx]
    return Plan(ops, plan_cost(snap, ws, ops), "GREEDY")

free, family and next_op are \phi_m, g_m and each order's next operation. A first operation may only go to a machine of its ready map, which is how frozen destinations are respected. The "previous" key (0, old start, ...) puts first every operation that keeps its old machine, in the order of its old start: the order of the proof.

Part 10

Common pitfalls

  1. "The executed schedule is the last plan." No plan is executed in full; the executed schedule is stitched from the beginnings of 60 to 177 plans a day.
  2. "Re-planning more often is always better." In the nominal case a new plan cannot be worse on the snapshot, but the snapshot is optimistic. At minute 109 (event 20) the plan sends order 3 to m3, expected back at 129. The repair took 19 minutes; a 30-minute one would have kept m3 down until 139 with order 3 waiting in front of it.
  3. "The plan's tardiness is what to expect." It is a lower bound for that plan, by Jensen's inequality.
  4. "Frozen means fixed in time." A frozen destination fixes the machine, not the start.
  5. "The solver is where the gain comes from." About two thirds of it come from re-planning with a greedy plan.
Part 11

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question before opening it.

1. What turns a planner into a controller?

Re-running it on the measured state at every event, and applying only the beginning of each plan.

2. Why is an order on a cart treated differently from an order in a queue?

The cart is already driving, so the snapshot keeps its destination, with the cart's arrival as R_{jm}. A queued order can still go to any eligible machine.

3. Why does an operation in progress not appear in the plan?

Nothing about it is left to decide; it enters only through \phi_m and the ready times of its order's next operation.

4. Why re-plan at events and not every minute?

Between events nothing happens that the plan did not predict, except the carts' timing.

5. Why does strict plan following let a machine stand idle with orders in its queue?

The plan wants another order next, typically of the same family or more urgent; starting a queued order would spend a changeover and break the grouping.

6. What does the "previous" greedy rule guarantee, and when?

Re-timing it does not increase the objective if no order is new, no ready or free time is later than assumed, and every machine is set up as expected.

7. Why is the planned tardiness optimistic?

The planner replaces random repair times by their mean; tardiness is convex in a repair duration, so by Jensen's inequality its expectation is at least its value at the mean.

8. How much of CP-SAT's gain over the best rule comes from the solver?

About a third: −1.7 of −5.7 minutes without breakdowns, −4.7 of −14.8 with them, at about 3,000 times the computing time.

Exercises

  1. Re-plan the paint machine by hand. With E blue, 5 minutes, due at 50, arriving at minute 35, list the six orders of C, D and E after B with their total tardiness. Then show that A C E B D, with 60, beats every order starting with A B.

    Hint

    From minute 40 the machine is red. Blue orders after a red one need a 10-minute changeover, and so does D after a blue one.

    Solution

    The tardiness of C, D and E: C D E 105, C E D 65, D C E 70, D E C 60, E C D 50, E D C 80. With B's 15 the best is E C D, 65. Any order starting A B costs at least 15 + 50 = 65 > 60, so A C E B D is strictly better: it keeps the machine blue until E is painted. Re-planning cannot reach it because B was frozen when E arrived.

  2. When re-timing hurts. The proposition of Part 4 assumes that every machine is set up as the plan expected and free no later than planned. (a) On one machine with a 5-minute changeover between families 0 and 1, find two orders and a plan whose re-timed version costs more although the machine is free earlier than planned. (b) Find a two-order example where the machine is free later and the cost increases. (c) Which events of the workshop produce each situation?

    Hint

    (a) The changeover s_m(g, f(j)) depends on the family g before the operation: give the machine the other family. (b) Delay \phi.

    Solution

    (a) X: family 0, 10 minutes, due 20; Y: family 1, 10 minutes, due 35. Planned with the machine free at 10, set up for family 0: X 10–20, Y 25–35, J = 20 + 35 = 55. Free at 8 but set up for family 1: X 13–23, Y 28–38, both 3 late, J = 10 \cdot 3 + 23 + 10 \cdot 3 + 38 = 121: the step "the changeover is unchanged" fails. (b) Two 10-minute orders due at 10 and 20: from \phi = 0, objective 30; from \phi' = 5, 10 \cdot 5 + 15 + 10 \cdot 5 + 25 = 140. (c) (a): an unplanned order filling a gap (Part 3); (b): a breakdown or a late repair.

  3. The price of betting on the mean. A repair lasts D uniform on [10, 30] minutes and the planner assumes 20. An order is planned to finish \eta minutes before its due date, 0 \le \eta \le 10, and each minute of repair delays it by a minute. Show that its expected tardiness is (10 - \eta)^2 / 40.

    Hint

    It is late by \max(0, D - 20 - \eta); integrate against the density 1/20.

    Solution

    \mathbb E[\max(0, D - 20 - \eta)] = \int_{20 + \eta}^{30} \frac{x - 20 - \eta}{20}\, dx = \frac{(10 - \eta)^2}{40}: 2.5 minutes at \eta = 0, 0.625 at \eta = 5, against a planned 0. That is Jensen's inequality for the convex function \max(0, \cdot).

  4. Count the re-plans of one morning. Predict the number of events of the hero's morning (12 orders, minutes 0 to 314, 5 machines with frequent breakdowns) as Part 3 does for a full day, and compare with the 43 observed. Is 43 surprising? Answer with a simulation of the breakdowns of many mornings.

    Hint

    One failure per 140 minutes and machine in the long run (chapter B1). make_scenario(ws, seed, n_jobs=1, mtbf=120) gives a day's breakdowns in downtime[m], a list of (failure, repair) minutes; count those before 314.

    Each machine fails about 314/140 = 2.24 times, so the prediction is 12 + 10 \cdot 314/140 = 34.4 events, 22.4 of them downtime events. Test day 0 has 31 (16 breakdowns, 15 repairs).   5. Re-plan only at arrivals. Write a controller that re-plans with the greedy planner at arrivals only, ignoring breakdowns and repairs, and run it on the 20 test days with frequent breakdowns. Compare with greedy re-planning at every event, 24.03 minutes.

    Hint

    Subclass CPSATController, call the parent's on_event only when kind == "arrival", and use use_solver=False so the 20 runs take seconds.

    Solution 
    import numpy as np
from jobshop.cpsat import CPSATController
from jobshop.metrics import run_metrics
from jobshop.scenario import LEVELS, make_scenario, make_workshop
from jobshop.shop import Shop

class ArrivalsOnly(CPSATController):
    def on_event(self, shop, kind, **info):
        if kind == "arrival":
            super().on_event(shop, kind, **info)

ws = make_workshop(); tard = []
for seed in range(20):
    sc = make_scenario(ws, seed, mtbf=LEVELS["frequent breakdowns"])
    log = Shop(ws, sc, ArrivalsOnly(use_solver=False)).run()
    tard.append(run_metrics(ws, sc, log)["mean_tardiness"])
print(np.mean(tard))     # 28.80

Part 13

The chapter on one page

  1. A controller re-plans: at every event, snapshot \Sigma_t, plan \Pi_t, execute only its beginning.
  2. The snapshot freezes running operations and cart destinations; ready times R_{jm} say how soon each order can be at each machine.
  3. Strict plan following makes a machine wait for its next planned order.
  4. Greedy list scheduling: the warm start and, alone, the ablation. Re-timing the previous plan cannot increase a regular objective (A1) in the nominal case.
  5. The 25-job cap bounds a model whose circuits grow quadratically; it never binds on the benchmark.
  6. What a plan cannot see: future orders (myopia), the spread of repair times (optimistic by Jensen), the carts.
  7. Nervousness: consecutive CP-SAT plans move 14.2 % of their common operations; nothing limits it.
  8. Results: re-planning earns 69 % and 68 % of CP-SAT's gain over the best rule.

The vocabulary, in one table

Word Meaning
Snapshot The running shop in the planner's terms.
Frozen Not changeable by a new plan.
Event-driven, receding horizon A new plan at every event; act on its beginning, plan again.
Strict plan following Machines run their planned order and wait for the next planned job.
List scheduling Placing operations one by one at their earliest feasible start.
Ablation The same system with one component removed.
Myopia, certainty equivalence Planning without future orders; planning with mean durations.
Plan drift, nervousness Reality against the plan; a plan against the previous one.

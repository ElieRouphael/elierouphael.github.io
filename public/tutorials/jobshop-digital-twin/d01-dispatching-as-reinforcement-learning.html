Dispatching as reinforcement learning

Chapter B2 showed that no single dispatching rule is best everywhere. Can a program learn, from simulated days, which rule to apply in which situation? Below, a small Q-learning agent learns in your browser to choose among four rules on a paint-like machine. By the end you will be able to write a dispatcher as a Markov decision process, design a reward that adds up to the objective and prove it, run Q-learning by hand, explain the Double DQN the repository trains, and judge whether a learned policy really beats a rule.

Part 1

A rule selector that learns

Chapter B2 found that the best rule depends on the day: most urgent first (EDD) wins in a calm shop, shortest setup first (SST) under frequent breakdowns, and on a given day another rule may beat both. A human dispatcher would not pick one rule for life. She would look at the queue and think the urgent order is lost anyway, stay on this colour, or nothing is urgent, take the short job. Can a program learn such judgement by trial and error on the simulator of chapter B1, with nobody telling it which rule was right?

That is reinforcement learning: an agent takes decisions, the environment answers with a new situation and a number, the reward, and the agent adjusts its behaviour to collect as much reward as possible over a run. No decision is labelled right or wrong. The repository's self-learning agent (DQN) does this on the workshop; this chapter builds it up from a toy.

The paint machine, made random. The hero applies this to a machine like the paint machine of chapter A1: two colours, a 5-minute changeover, six orders waiting at time 0 with random painting times and due dates. Whenever the machine is free and two or more orders wait, the agent sees a coarse description of the situation and picks FIFO, SPT, EDD or SST; after each decision it is charged the lateness that decision made certain (Part 3).

Learning, choosing, testing. The agent learns from training instances: fresh random runs, one after another (a run is called an episode). Every 100 runs its current choices are scored, without exploring, on 200 fixed validation instances, and the best-scoring snapshot of what it has learned, the checkpoint, is kept. Its honest score is then measured on 200 other test instances, which played no part in learning or choosing.

Read the hero. Untrained, the agent has learned nothing yet, so every rule looks equally good and ties go to FIFO: its curve starts at FIFO's 133.8 minutes on the validation instances. Within a few hundred runs it is below every fixed rule; the checkpoint kept comes at episode 4000 with 77.2. On the test instances:

Policy Total tardiness, test (min)
FIFO, first come, first served 137.0
SPT, shortest job first 99.6

The agent beats the best single rule by 8.9 minutes, 10 %, and training seeds 1 to 5 all land between 79.1 and 79.4. Three lessons are already visible. A state-dependent choice of rule can beat every single rule when situations call for different rules. What it sees limits it more than its four rules. A rule selector may switch rules at every decision, so its ceiling is the best succession of rules for each instance: of the 4^5 = 1{,}024 successions, the best, chosen in hindsight, averages 66.1, within 0.4 minutes of the optimum. (One rule per whole instance gives only 75.0.) The four rules, well switched, almost suffice; the agent stops at 79.1 because its 18 coarse situations force one rule onto queues that call for different ones, and because it learns from noisy samples. The room depends on the problem: at the paint machine's 10-minute changeover SST is right more often (102.6 on test) and the agent gains less (99.4). In the workshop, Part 8 shows, it gains nothing measurable.

Part 2

The dispatcher as a Markov decision process

What is a Markov decision process?

In plain words: a game against chance played in turns. At each turn the player sees the situation, chooses a move, receives a score and lands in a new situation that depends, at random, only on the current situation and the move.

More precisely: a tuple (\mathcal S, \mathcal A, P, R, \gamma) of states, actions, transition probabilities P(s' \mid s, a), expected rewards R(s, a) and a discount \gamma \in [0, 1]. A policy \pi maps states to actions. The Markov property: s_{k+1} and r_{k+1} depend on the past only through (s_k, a_k).

Decision index
k counts decisions; decision k happens at clock time t_k (minutes). The notation page's s_t, a_t, r_{t+1} are this chapter's s_k, a_k, r_{k+1}. This k is not the operation index k of the notation page.
Episode
one run: a simulated day, or one instance of six orders in the toy.
Model-free
the agent never sees P or R; it only samples transitions.

On the paint machine. A decision happens whenever the machine is free and two or more orders wait: five per instance, the last order needing no choice. The action is one of the four rules; the order it picks is painted, after a changeover if its colour differs. The transition is that painting, until the machine is free again. The state is one of 18 situations, combining three things: how many orders wait (2, 3–4, 5–6); the colour situation (every order needs a change; the most urgent order has the current colour; the most urgent needs a change but another does not); and whether the most urgent order is tight: its slack after its own changeover, d_j - t - s - p_j, is below one changeover. Training visited 17 of them. In the first two colour situations EDD and SST pick the same order (with no same-colour order every changeover is equal, and SST, like the repository's, breaks ties by due date), so they really compete only in the third column of the hero's table. The reward is Part 3's subject.

The workshop: decision points. The agent decides at one kind of event: a machine is free and at least two jobs wait in its queue. Decisions are irregular in time, so the process is semi-Markov, and k and the discount count decisions, not minutes. On 20 scenarios generated like the training days (seeds 100000–100009 at both levels) under a random policy a day held 17 to 184 decisions, 80 on average.

Actions. a_k \in \{\text{FIFO}, \text{SPT}, \text{EDD}, \text{SST}\}: the job that rule picks is started. Routing stays the earliest-expected-finish rule of chapter B2. The agent is a rule selector, a policy over four heuristics, not over jobs; Part 8 turns on this.

State. jobshop/dqn.py:features returns 29 numbers, each divided by a fixed scale so that all are of similar size (slacks are divided by 100 minutes and clipped at ±3). With the slack \sigma_j(t) = d_j - t - W_j(t), where W_j(t) is the shortest processing time still to do on job j:

  • for each of the four jobs the rules would pick (20 numbers): processing time here, changeover needed, slack, share of operations left, time waited in this queue;
  • for the queue (5): length, share with negative slack, smallest and mean slack, share needing no changeover;
  • for the shop (4): jobs present, share with negative slack, share of machines down, pending transport requests.

Transition. The simulator of chapter B1, until the next decision point anywhere in the shop. Is it Markov? Not exactly. The features omit the other queues, the carts and the failure clocks, and one agent serves all five machines on a single chain of decisions, so s_{k+1} often belongs to another machine. The process is partially observed; the theory below is applied to it as an approximation.

Part 3

A reward that adds up to the tardiness

Total tardiness is known only at the end. A reward of 0 at every decision and -\sum_j T_j at the end is correct but nearly useless: with 80 decisions a day, which one caused the lateness? We want a reward at every decision that sums to the score. Idea: charge lateness as soon as it is certain.

What is the committed tardiness?

In plain words: the minutes of lateness the shop is already sure to deliver: finished jobs at their real lateness, every other job at the lateness it would have if nothing delayed it further.

More precisely: with \mathcal F(t) the finished jobs and \mathcal U(t) the unfinished jobs in the shop,

E(t) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal F(t)} T_j + \sum_{j \in \mathcal U(t)} \max\bigl(0,\ t + W_j(t) - d_j\bigr).

The reward of decision k is r_{k+1} = -\bigl(E(t_{k+1}) - E(t_k)\bigr)/c with c = 50 minutes per unit, and the last decision K receives r_{K+1} = -\bigl(\sum_j T_j - E(t_K)\bigr)/c.

In the toy nothing is in progress at a decision, so W_j = p_j, E(t) = \sum_{\text{done}} T_j + \sum_{\text{waiting}} \max(0, t + p_j - d_j), and c = 1.

By hand, on test instance 0 of the widget below. Its orders, painting time / due date: A 5/13, B 7/49, C 13/47, D 20/77, E 21/53, F 22/27; B and D are red, the others blue. The agent applies EDD, SST, EDD, SPT, SST. At t = 40, A, F and C are done on time. Of the waiting orders, B has t + p - d = 40 + 7 - 49 = -2 and D 40 + 20 - 77 = -17, both counting 0, and E has 40 + 21 - 53 = 8: E(40) = 8. SPT paints B, which after a 5-minute changeover ends at 52, 3 minutes late, and E's term grows to 52 + 21 - 53 = 20: E(52) = 3 + 20 = 23. SST then paints D, on time at 72, and E last, ending at 98, 45 minutes late. At the five decisions E is 0, 0, 0, 8, 23, and the run ends at a total tardiness of 48: the rewards are 0, 0, -8, -15, -25, and they sum to -48.

Lemma: E never decreases, and ends at the total tardiness. Follow one job's term.

  • At its arrival r_j, W_j is its fastest-route work W_{f(j)}, and d_j - r_j \ge 2 W_{f(j)} (the notation page's due-date factor is at least 2), so r_j + W_j - d_j < 0: it enters at 0, without a jump.
  • While it waits for a machine or a cart, or rides one, W_j is constant and t grows.
  • When an operation starts, W_j jumps from this operation's fastest time to its time on the chosen machine, which is at least as long. During a changeover or a breakdown W_j is constant; during processing W_j falls as fast as t rises, so t + W_j is constant.
  • When its last operation ends at C_j, its term becomes \max(0, C_j - d_j) = T_j, the same value, now frozen.

Each term is nondecreasing, so E is, and once every job is finished E = \sum_j T_j. In particular every reward is \le 0. \square

Theorem: the rewards telescope. For a run with decisions k = 0, \dots, K,

\begin{aligned} \sum_{k=0}^{K} r_{k+1} &= -\frac1c \Bigl[\bigl(E(t_1) - E(t_0)\bigr) + \dots + \bigl(\textstyle\sum_j T_j - E(t_K)\bigr)\Bigr] \\ &= -\frac{\sum_j T_j - E(t_0)}{c}, \end{aligned}

since every intermediate E(t_k) appears once with each sign. \square

The fine print. The sum is exactly -\sum_j T_j/c only when E(t_0) = 0. In the toy due dates are at least the painting time, so E(0) = 0. In the workshop, on 20 scenarios generated like the training days (seeds 100000–100009 at both levels), E(t_0) = 0 on 17, and 1, 5 and 12 minutes on the others: lateness committed before any free machine had a choice. On scenario 100002 with breakdowns the rewards, times c, sum to -224 minutes for a tardiness of 225. The optimal policy is unaffected: until the first decision every machine started the only job it had, so E(t_0) is the same for every policy, and maximising the expected sum of rewards is exactly minimising the expected total tardiness. On the repository's test scenario (seed 91, 40 jobs, breakdowns) there are 79 decisions, the tardiness is 2,056 minutes and the rewards sum to -2056/50 to machine precision.

A known construction. This reward is the sparse one, -\sum_j T_j/c at the end, plus \Phi(s_{k+1}) - \Phi(s_k) with \Phi = -E/c (and \Phi = 0 at the end). Ng, Harada and Russell [4] proved that such potential-based shaping, \gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s), never changes the optimal policy; with \gamma = 1 this is a second proof that the reward is safe.

What discounting breaks. The agent maximises the discounted return \sum_k \gamma^k r_{k+1} with \gamma = 0.97. Since every reward is \le 0 and \gamma^k \le 1, when E(t_0) = 0,

-\frac{\sum_j T_j}{c} \;\le\; \sum_{k=0}^{K} \gamma^k r_{k+1} \;\le\; 0,

with equality on the left, for \gamma < 1, only if r_{k+1} = 0 for every k \ge 1: all lateness is committed before the second decision. Maximising it is a different objective: lateness committed k decisions ahead counts \gamma^k, a weight that halves every 22.8 decisions and is 0.090 at decision 79, the end of a typical day. The effective horizon is 1/(1-\gamma) = 33.3 decisions. On seed 91 the discounted return is -578.3/50 against -2056/50. Ng's invariance would need \gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s); the code uses \Phi(s') - \Phi(s), so with \gamma < 1 the equivalence is lost (Part 5 says why the repository discounts anyway). Charging lateness when it becomes certain, not when the job leaves, keeps much of a decision's consequence close to it.

Discounted with \gamma = 0.97, the rewards of test instance 0 sum to -43.35; with \gamma = 0.9, to -33.82.

Part 4

Bellman's equation and Q-learning

The return from decision k is G_k = r_{k+1} + \gamma r_{k+2} + \gamma^2 r_{k+3} + \dots, the discounted score of the rest of the run.

What is an action value?

In plain words: how good it is, on average, to apply a given rule in a given situation and then go on deciding in a given way, scored over the rest of the run.

More precisely: Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb E[G_k \mid s_k = s, a_k = a, \text{then } \pi], and Q^\star(s, a) = \max_\pi Q^\pi(s, a). A policy picking \arg\max_a Q^\star(s, a) in every state is optimal.

Greedy policy
\pi(s) = \arg\max_a Q(s, a) for an estimate Q.
Bootstrapping
estimating a value from other estimated values (unrelated to the statistical bootstrap of chapter D2's intervals).

Bellman's equation, derived. Factoring \gamma out of all terms after the first gives G_k = r_{k+1} + \gamma G_{k+1}. Take expectations given (s_k, a_k) = (s, a), with \pi followed afterwards:

Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb E\bigl[\, r_{k+1} + \gamma\, G_{k+1} \bigm| s_k = s, a_k = a \bigr].

Average G_{k+1} in two stages (the tower rule): first over what happens after the next state s_{k+1} = s', then over s'. By the Markov property the future after s' does not depend on how the run got there, and from s' the policy takes \pi(s'), so \mathbb E[G_{k+1} \mid s_{k+1} = s', \text{history}] = Q^\pi(s', \pi(s')) and

Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb E\bigl[\, r_{k+1} + \gamma\, Q^\pi\bigl(s', \pi(s')\bigr) \bigm| s_k = s, a_k = a \bigr].

For the optimal values, the best continuation from s' is to act optimally from there, worth \max_{a'} Q^\star(s', a'): this is the principle of optimality, which we cite from Sutton and Barto [1]. So

\boxed{Q^\star(s, a) = \mathbb E\bigl[\, r_{k+1} + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^\star(s', a') \bigm| s_k = s, a_k = a \bigr]},

with the max taken as 0 when the run ends. Sutton and Barto (Sections 3.6 and 4.1) state that it has a unique solution in a finite problem when \gamma < 1 or every run ends. For \gamma < 1 the proof rests on the right-hand side being a contraction (applied to two guesses of Q, it brings them at least a factor \gamma closer); it is given in Puterman [13], and we cite it without proof.

By hand (\gamma = 1). In s_0, rule a costs 2 minutes and leads to s_1; rule b costs 4 and ends the run. In s_1, a costs 6 and b costs 3, both ending it. Backwards: Q^\star(s_1, a) = -6, Q^\star(s_1, b) = -3; Q^\star(s_0, a) = -2 + \max(-6, -3) = -5 and Q^\star(s_0, b) = -4. The best first move is b, although a has the better immediate reward: what a reward-by-reward dispatcher cannot see. With \gamma = 0.5, Q(s_0, a) = -3.5 and a becomes optimal: the discount changes the answer.

Q-learning: the equation from samples. Each transition (s, a, r, s') is one sample of what is inside the expectation. Q-learning moves the estimate a little towards it:

\boxed{Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \alpha\,\delta}, \qquad \delta = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a') - Q(s, a).

(This \delta is the TD error, not the cart allowance of the notation page.) At Q = Q^\star the temporal-difference error \delta has mean zero, so Q^\star is the fixed point the updates hover around: a stochastic approximation. Watkins and Dayan [5] proved convergence in a finite Markov problem with \gamma < 1 if every pair is updated infinitely often with steps satisfying \sum \alpha = \infty, \sum \alpha^2 < \infty (other results cover runs that always end). The toy's runs end after five decisions, but its 18 coarse states are not Markov: two different queues can share a state, so convergence to Q^\star is not guaranteed, whatever the steps. The wobble of the hero's curve comes from both the constant \alpha = 0.1 and this aliasing, at any \gamma, and it is why the best checkpoint is kept rather than the last.

Exploring. The \varepsilon-greedy behaviour takes a random rule with probability \varepsilon; in the toy \varepsilon falls linearly from 1 (towards 0 at 60 % of the episodes) and is floored at 0.05, reached at 57 %. Q-learning is off-policy: its target uses \max_{a'}, so it learns the greedy policy while behaving otherwise. Starting all estimates at 0 when every reward is \le 0 makes untried rules look best, a free push to explore (optimistic initialisation).

What the toy agent learned (the hero's table, at the defaults). Where the urgent order has the current colour it applies EDD or SST, which there pick the same order. In the conflict column with three or more orders waiting and the urgent order tight, it takes the short job (SPT). A plausible reading: when the urgent order has little slack left, clearing short orders first limits how many others become late. Nobody programmed this.

Part 5

From a table to a network: Double DQN

The workshop's state is 29 real numbers: no table can hold it. The deep Q-network of Mnih et al. [2] replaces the table by a fitted function.

What is Q_\theta?

In plain words: a function with adjustable numbers \theta that takes the 29 features and returns four scores, one per rule. It is fitted: the numbers are nudged in small steps downhill on an error (gradient descent), so that the scores match targets.

More precisely: a neural network 29 \to 64 \to 64 \to 4: each layer multiplies its input by a matrix of weights and adds biases, and every layer but the last replaces negative values by 0 (ReLU); 6,340 parameters (weights and biases) in all. The steps are taken by Adam, a gradient method with a step size per parameter.

Online network
Q_\theta, the one being trained.
Target network
Q_{\theta^-}, a slow copy of it used to build the targets.

Why fitting is Q-learning. For a table, take the error \tfrac12\bigl(y - Q(s, a)\bigr)^2 with the target y = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a') held fixed. Its derivative with respect to the entry Q(s, a) is -(y - Q(s, a)) = -\delta, so a gradient step of size \alpha is Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \alpha\delta: exactly the Q-learning update. The network does the same through \theta:

y = r + \gamma\,(1 - \text{end}) \max_{a'} Q_{\theta^-}(s', a'), \qquad \min_\theta\ \mathbb E\bigl[\ell\bigl(y - Q_\theta(s, a)\bigr)\bigr],

where end = 1 if the transition ends the run and 0 otherwise, and \ell is the loss below. The target is held constant in the gradient, although it is built from estimates too: that is why the method is called a semi-gradient. Three devices make it stable:

  • Experience replay (Lin [7]): each step trains on a batch of past transitions drawn at random, breaking the correlation of successive decisions.
  • A target network \theta^-, so the target does not move with every step. The repository uses the soft update of Lillicrap et al. [8], \theta^- \leftarrow (1 - \tau)\theta^- + \tau\theta: with \tau = 0.005 an old weight's influence halves every 139 steps.
  • A robust loss: the Huber loss [9], \tfrac12 u^2 for |u| \le 1 and |u| - \tfrac12 beyond, so one disastrous day does not give a huge gradient; the gradient's norm is also clipped.

Why discount? These targets are built from the network's own estimates. With \gamma < 1 an error shrinks by \gamma at each step back and the targets stay bounded: a bias, Part 3's change of objective, traded for stability.

The overestimation bias. For any random variables, \mathbb E[\max_a X_a] \ge \max_a \mathbb E[X_a]: for each fixed a', \max_a X_a \ge X_{a'}, so \mathbb E[\max_a X_a] \ge \mathbb E[X_{a'}] for every a', the best one included. It is strict when, with positive probability, an action that is not the best in mean has the largest estimate. Two actions by hand: both rules are worth 0 and each estimate is +1 or -1 with probability \tfrac12, independently; the four cases give maxima 1, 1, 1, -1, so \mathbb E[\max] = 0.5 while \max \mathbb E = 0. For two actions of equal value with independent \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2) errors the bias is \sigma/\sqrt\pi = 0.564\,\sigma (exercise 3). The DQN target takes this max over noisy estimates, and bootstrapping copies the bias backwards (Thrun and Schwartz [10]).

Double DQN (van Hasselt [11]; van Hasselt, Guez and Silver [3]) chooses with one network and evaluates with the other:

\boxed{y = r + \gamma\,(1 - \text{end})\; Q_{\theta^-}\bigl(s', \arg\max_{a'} Q_\theta(s', a')\bigr)}.

If the two estimates had independent errors, the value read for the chosen \hat a would be unbiased given \hat a, so its mean would be \mathbb E[Q^\star(s', \hat a)] \le \max_a Q^\star(s', a): no upward bias. In the coin example it gives exactly 0. The two networks of DQN are not independent (one lags the other), so the bias is reduced, not removed.

The code: the reward and the training step

jobshop/dqn.py · 105-124

def estimated_tardiness(shop: Shop) -> int:
    """Tardiness already certain at this time: finished jobs count in full, an unfinished job
    counts for the lateness it would have if all its remaining work started now, without waiting.
    It never decreases and equals the total tardiness of the run when every job is finished."""
    total = 0
    for j in shop.jobs.values():
        if j.completion is not None:
            total += max(0, j.completion - j.due)
        else:
            total += max(0, shop.now + remaining_work(shop, j) - j.due)
    return total


def remaining_work(shop: Shop, j: JobRT) -> int:
    """Shortest processing time still to do on a job (the operation in progress counts for what is left)."""
    if j.state is JobState.PROCESSING:
        m = shop.machines[j.dest]
        left = m.rem_proc - (shop.now - m.seg_start if m.seg_start is not None and m.seg_kind == "proc" else 0)
        return left + shop.ws.min_remaining_work(j.family, j.op + 1)
    return shop.ws.min_remaining_work(j.family, j.op)

The definition of E(t) term by term: a finished job adds \max(0, C_j - d_j); any other job, started or not, adds \max(0, t + W_j(t) - d_j), with remaining_work returning W_j(t): for a job in progress, what is left of the current operation (rem_proc, less the time already spent in a running processing segment) plus the fastest times of the later ones. Changeovers are not counted: during one, W_j is constant, so the job's term still grows with t; counting a queued job's future changeover would let E drop when its machine changes family or the job is rerouted. Jobs not yet arrived are not in shop.jobs.

jobshop/dqn.py · 195-217

    def act(self, shop: Shop, state: np.ndarray) -> int:
        """Epsilon-greedy action; closes the previous transition with the tardiness added since."""
        accrued = estimated_tardiness(shop)
        if self._pending is not None:
            prev_state, prev_action, prev_accrued = self._pending
            reward = -(accrued - prev_accrued) / self.config.reward_scale
            self.buffer.push(prev_state, prev_action, reward, state, False)
            self.optimize()
        if self.rng.random() < epsilon_by_step(self.steps_done, self.config):
            action = self.rng.randrange(len(ACTIONS))
        else:
            action = greedy_action(self.policy_net, state)
        self.steps_done += 1
        self._pending = (state, action, accrued)
        return action

    def end_episode(self, total_tardiness: int) -> None:
        if self._pending is not None:
            prev_state, prev_action, prev_accrued = self._pending
            reward = -(total_tardiness - prev_accrued) / self.config.reward_scale
            self.buffer.push(prev_state, prev_action, reward, np.zeros_like(prev_state), True)
            self.optimize()
        self._pending = None

accrued is E(t_k). If a decision is pending, its transition is now complete: reward -(E(t_{k+1}) - E(t_k))/c with reward_scale = 50, next state the one just observed, not terminal; it goes to the buffer and one gradient step follows. Then the \varepsilon-greedy action, and (state, action, E(t_k)) waits for the next decision, which may come from another machine. end_episode closes the last decision with the true total tardiness and a terminal flag: together they are the telescoping theorem in code, checked by the repository's test test_rewards_of_an_episode_sum_to_minus_the_total_tardiness.

    def optimize(self) -> float | None:
        config = self.config
        if len(self.buffer) < max(config.batch_size, config.min_buffer_size):
            return None
        batch = self.buffer.sample(config.batch_size)
        states = torch.as_tensor(np.asarray(batch.state), dtype=torch.float32)
        actions = torch.as_tensor(batch.action, dtype=torch.int64).unsqueeze(1)
        rewards = torch.as_tensor(batch.reward, dtype=torch.float32).unsqueeze(1)
        next_states = torch.as_tensor(np.asarray(batch.next_state), dtype=torch.float32)
        terminals = torch.as_tensor(batch.terminal, dtype=torch.float32).unsqueeze(1)
        current_q = self.policy_net(states).gather(1, actions)
        targets = compute_td_targets(
            rewards, next_states, terminals, self.policy_net, self.target_net, config.gamma, config.double_dqn
        )
        loss = functional.smooth_l1_loss(current_q, targets)
        self.optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(self.policy_net.parameters(), config.grad_clip_norm)
        self.optimizer.step()
        soft_update(self.target_net, self.policy_net, config.tau_soft_update)
        return float(loss.item())

Nothing happens before 1,000 transitions are stored. Then 64 are drawn at random, current_q reads Q_\theta(s, a) for the actions taken (gather picks one column per row), smooth_l1_loss is the Huber loss against the targets, the gradient norm is clipped at 10, Adam steps, and the target network moves by the soft update. One decision, one step: 117,671 decisions per training seed on average.

jobshop/dqn.py · compute_td_targets

def compute_td_targets(rewards, next_states, terminals, policy_net, target_net, gamma: float, double_dqn: bool):
    """y = r + gamma * (1 - terminal) * Q_target(s', a*), a* chosen by the online net in Double DQN."""
    with torch.no_grad():
        next_q_target = target_net(next_states)
        if double_dqn:
            best_actions = policy_net(next_states).argmax(dim=1, keepdim=True)
        else:
            best_actions = next_q_target.argmax(dim=1, keepdim=True)
        return rewards + gamma * next_q_target.gather(1, best_actions) * (1.0 - terminals)

The boxed target under torch.no_grad(). best_actions is the argmax of the online network in Double DQN (of the target network otherwise); the value is read from the target network, times \gamma and 1 - \text{end}. The tests check both branches: with online values (0, 9, 0, 0) and target values (4, 1, 7, 0), Double DQN returns 1 and plain DQN 7.

Part 7

Training curves and checkpoint selection

scripts/train_dqn.py trains five agents (training seeds 0–4), each on 1,500 days of 60 jobs alternating the breakdown levels, on scenario seeds 100000 + 1000 \times training seed + day, 100001 to 105500. Every 25 days the greedy network is scored on 20 validation runs (seeds 5000–5009, both levels) by mean tardiness per job, and the best checkpoint is kept; finally the seed with the best validation is kept: seed 3, 21.98 at day 1150. A seed takes 19–20 minutes.

On the same validation runs the rules score FIFO 47.54, SPT 32.96, EDD 26.99 and SST 24.29 minutes per job. Two things stand out.

  • Validation does not trend. Seed 3's first check is 27.42, its last 27.93, and its 60 checks range from 21.98 to 33.81. Averaged over the five seeds, the training-day tardiness falls from 28.9 over the first 100 days to 24.0 over the last 100, mostly because \varepsilon falls to about 0.07: the agent explores less, not necessarily chooses better.
  • Selection finds lucky networks. Four of the five kept checkpoints beat SST on validation, yet only 3 % of all 300 checks do. The minimum of 60 noisy scores is biased low, \mathbb E[\min] \le \min \mathbb E, the inequality of Part 5 again; the kept agent's 2.31-minute margin over SST, on the runs that chose it, should be expected to vanish on new days. Chapter D2 treats this optimism properly.
Part 8

Results: no better than the best rule, and why

The test: 20 days per level (seeds 0–19), unused for training, selection or tuning, from results/runs.csv. Two ideas of chapter D2, in plain words. A paired difference compares the two methods on the same simulated day, so that the luck of the day, its arrivals and breakdowns, cancels. The bracket after a number is a 95 % interval: the range of values compatible with the noise of 20 days; if the bracket of a difference contains 0, the data cannot tell the two methods apart. Chapter D2 derives both.

Both intervals contain zero: no measurable difference from the best rule, and no gain over it; the intervals also allow the agent to be up to 1.5 and 5.1 minutes worse. It does stay close to the best rule at both levels although that rule changes (EDD, then SST), which no single rule manages. The "best rule" is picked on the test itself, which flatters the baseline slightly (D2).

The policy class. At each decision the agent chooses among at most four jobs, at one machine, now. It cannot pick a fifth job, keep a machine idle for a job about to arrive, coordinate machines so that each runs one family, or route. The re-planning controller of chapter C3 does all that. Total changeover time per day, mean of the 20 calm days: 635 minutes for CP-SAT, 918 for SST, 1,025 for EDD, 988 for the agent. How much room do the rules themselves leave? The hindsight row, one rule per whole day, gains 0.38 and 2.15 minutes over the best fixed rule (7.02 − 6.64 and 34.15 − 32.00), but that is only a lower bound, since a selector may switch rules at every decision. In the toy the same bound said 13.0 minutes while the best successions of rules left 21.9, of which the agent captured 8.9. In the workshop the true ceiling of rule successions is unknown: with about 80 decisions a day there are about 4^{80} of them, far too many to enumerate.

Credit assignment and noise. A day holds about 80 decisions on five machines, on one chain. The reward between two decisions mixes this decision's consequences with other machines', routing's and breakdowns'; in a calm 60-job day under an untrained agent, 36 of 67 rewards are exactly 0. Deciding which decision deserves the blame is the credit assignment problem, and \gamma = 0.97 adds a 33-decision horizon. Meanwhile one agent's last 20 validation scores have a standard deviation of 2.32 minutes on average, the size of the effect being sought.

What could work better. Change the policy class, not the training budget: choose the job directly, scoring every waiting job with a network; describe the shop by a graph, the disjunctive graph of chapter A3, read by a graph neural network as in Zhang et al. [12]; or decide when to re-plan, keeping the CP-SAT controller of chapter C3 and learning which events deserve a new plan. Judge them against a stronger baseline such as the composite ATCS rule of chapter B2. The result says that this agent, with this state, these actions and this budget, does not beat the best rule; it says nothing about reinforcement learning in general.

Part 9

Common pitfalls

  1. "The rewards always sum to minus the total tardiness." Only undiscounted, and only up to E(t_0), which is zero on 17 of the 20 scenarios generated like the training days. The correction is the same for every policy.
  2. "Discounting is a technicality." With \gamma = 0.97 the lateness committed at the end of a day counts 0.090; the two-decision example shows a discount changing the best action.
  3. "A Q-value predicts the tardiness in minutes." It is a discounted sum of scaled rewards, learned through a biased max, from features that do not determine the future. Only the ranking is used.
  4. "The best validation score is the agent's performance." It is the minimum of 60 noisy checks per seed over five seeds, biased low. The test measures.
  5. "A rule selector finds the best schedule if trained long enough." In the toy, perfectly switched rules would reach 66.1 against an optimum of 65.7, yet the agent stops at 79.1 whatever the seed: its 18 coarse states force one rule onto different situations. What an agent sees bounds it as much as what it may do; in the workshop, add that the rules can neither idle a machine nor coordinate machines.
Part 10

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question before opening it.

1. In the two-decision example of Part 4, with γ = 0.8, which first move is optimal?

Q^\star(s_0, a) = -2 + 0.8 \times (-3) = -4.4, below Q^\star(s_0, b) = -4, so b. The two tie at \gamma = 2/3; below that, a wins.

2. Why is the agent's problem only approximately Markov?

Its 29 features omit much of the shop, and successive states often belong to different machines: equal feature vectors can have different futures.

3. Why does the committed tardiness never decrease?

For each job t + W_j(t) is constant while processed, grows while it waits, travels, is set up or stopped, and jumps up if it starts on a slower machine; finished jobs are frozen at T_j.

4. State the telescoping identity precisely.

\sum_{k=0}^{K} r_{k+1} = -\bigl(\sum_j T_j - E(t_0)\bigr)/c, undiscounted; -\sum_j T_j/c when E(t_0) = 0.

5. What does Q-learning do with one transition (s, a, r, s')?

It moves Q(s, a) a fraction \alpha towards r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a'), one sample of the right-hand side of Bellman's equation.

6. Why is \mathbb E[\max_a X_a] \ge \max_a \mathbb E[X_a], and why does it matter?

Because \max_a X_a \ge X_{a'} for each a' and expectation preserves it. The DQN target maximises over noisy estimates, so it is biased upwards, and bootstrapping spreads the bias.

7. What does Double DQN change?

The action in the target is chosen by the online network and evaluated by the target network: Q_{\theta^-}(s', \arg\max_{a'} Q_\theta(s', a')).

8. The agent beats SST on validation (21.98 against 24.29) but not on test. Contradiction?

No. The 21.98 is the minimum of 300 noisy checks, selected on those very runs; only 3 % of checks beat SST. On unseen days the margin disappears.

Exercises

  1. Rewards by hand on the paint machine. Take the paint example of the notation page: set up for blue, a 10-minute changeover; A blue, 20 min, due 30; B red, 10 min, due 25; C blue, 15 min, due 70; D red, 25 min, due 60. With SST at every decision and c = 1, compute E at each decision and the rewards, and check that they sum to minus SST's total tardiness of 50. Repeat for FIFO (55).

    Hint

    A decision happens while two or more orders wait: at t = 0, after the first order and after the second. At each, E is the tardiness of the finished orders plus \sum \max(0, t + p_j - d_j) over the waiting ones. SST breaks ties by due date.

    Solution

    SST paints A, C, B, D. At t = 0 every t + p - d is negative: E = 0. At t = 20 (A on time) B has 20 + 10 - 25 = 5 and C, D are negative: E = 5. At t = 35 (C on time) B has 35 + 10 - 25 = 20 and D 35 + 25 - 60 = 0: E = 20. B ends at 55 (30 late) and D at 80 (20 late): total 50. Rewards -5, -15, -30, sum -50. FIFO paints A, B, C, D: E = 0, then 5; at t = 40 B is finished 15 late and D has 40 + 25 - 60 = 5: E = 20. C ends on time at 65 and D at 100, 40 late: total 55. Rewards -5, -15, -35, sum -55.

  2. Q-learning by hand. In the two-decision example of Part 4 (\gamma = 1), start from Q = 0 everywhere and apply the Q-learning update with \alpha = 0.5 to these transitions, in order: (s_0, a, -2, s_1), (s_1, a, -6, \text{end}), (s_0, b, -4, \text{end}), (s_1, b, -3, \text{end}), (s_0, a, -2, s_1). Give the table after each step. Which first move does the greedy policy pick at the end, and where do repeated passes lead?

    Hint

    Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \alpha\bigl(r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a') - Q(s, a)\bigr), with the max taken as 0 when the run ends.

    Solution

    Step 1: target -2 + \max(0, 0) = -2, so Q(s_0, a) = -1. Step 2: Q(s_1, a) = -3. Step 3: Q(s_0, b) = -2. Step 4: Q(s_1, b) = -1.5. Step 5: target -2 + \max(-3, -1.5) = -3.5, so Q(s_0, a) = -1 + 0.5(-3.5 + 1) = -2.25. The table is now Q(s_0, \cdot) = (-2.25, -2) and Q(s_1, \cdot) = (-3, -1.5): greedy picks b in s_0, already the right move, but every value is still too optimistic, the zero start half corrected. After 20 passes through the four transitions the table is (-5, -4) and (-6, -3), the Q^\star of Part 4: each update halves the distance to a target that itself settles.

  3. The bias of a maximum. For independent X_1, X_2 \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2) show \mathbb E[\max(X_1, X_2)] = \sigma/\sqrt\pi, and that the double estimator \mathbb E[Y_{\hat a}], \hat a = \arg\max_a X_a, Y an independent copy, is 0.

    Hint

    \max(x, y) = \frac{x + y}{2} + \frac{|x - y|}{2}, and \mathbb E|Z| = s\sqrt{2/\pi} for Z \sim \mathcal N(0, s^2).

    Solution

    X_1 - X_2 \sim \mathcal N(0, 2\sigma^2), so \mathbb E[\max] = 0 + \tfrac12 \sigma\sqrt2\sqrt{2/\pi} = \sigma/\sqrt\pi \approx 0.564\,\sigma. Given \hat a, Y_{\hat a} has mean 0 because Y is independent of X, so the double estimator has mean 0. The numbers script checks both by simulation (0.566 and 0.000).

Part 12

The chapter on one page

  1. The MDP: decide when a machine is free and two or more jobs wait; 29 features; four rules as actions; the simulator as transition; about 80 decisions a day; only approximately Markov.
  2. The reward: minus the increase of the committed tardiness E(t), nondecreasing and ending at \sum_j T_j; the rewards telescope to -(\sum_j T_j - E(t_0))/c. Discounting with \gamma = 0.97 changes the objective.
  3. Bellman and Q-learning: Q^\star(s,a) = \mathbb E[r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^\star(s',a')]; Q-learning moves Q towards one sample of it, a gradient step on a squared error; \varepsilon-greedy, off-policy.
  4. The toy: 18 states; 79.1 against 88.0 for SST; perfectly switched rules would reach 66.1, the optimum is 65.7: the coarse state holds the agent back.
  5. DQN: 29 \to 64 \to 64 \to 4, replay, a soft target network, Huber loss, learning rate 5 \times 10^{-4}, batch 64; Double DQN against \mathbb E[\max] \ge \max \mathbb E.
  6. Training: no trend on validation; the kept checkpoint is the luckiest of 60.
  7. Results: +0.4 [−0.7, +1.5] and +1.3 [−2.1, +5.1] min against the best rule, no measurable difference; the policy class, credit assignment and noise explain it.

The vocabulary, in one table

Word Meaning
Markov decision process States, actions, transitions, rewards, discount; the future depends on the present only.
Semi-Markov Decisions irregularly spaced in time; k counts decisions.
Committed tardiness E(t) Lateness already certain.
Telescoping reward Rewards that are differences of one quantity, so they sum to its total change.
Return, effective horizon \sum_k \gamma^k r_{k+1}; 1/(1-\gamma) decisions.
Q^\pi, Q^\star, Bellman equation Value of an action in a state; Q^\star = \mathbb E[r + \gamma \max Q^\star].
TD error, off-policy \delta = r + \gamma \max Q(s',\cdot) - Q(s,a); learning greedy values while exploring.
Replay, target network A buffer sampled at random; a slow copy for the targets.
Overestimation, Double DQN \mathbb E[\max] \ge \max \mathbb E; choose with one network, evaluate with the other.

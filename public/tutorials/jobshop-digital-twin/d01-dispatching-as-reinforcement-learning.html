Part 1

A rule selector that learns

Chapter B2 found that the best rule depends on the day: most urgent first (EDD) wins in a calm shop, shortest setup first (SST) under frequent breakdowns. A human dispatcher would switch: the urgent order is lost anyway, stay on this colour, or nothing is urgent, take the short job. Can a program learn such judgement by trial and error on the simulator of chapter B1?

That is reinforcement learning: an agent takes decisions, the environment answers with a new situation and a number, the reward, and the agent adjusts its behaviour to collect as much reward as possible over a run. No decision is labelled right or wrong. The repository's self-learning agent (DQN) does this on the workshop; this chapter builds it up from the hero's toy.

The toy. A paint-like machine (chapter A1) with six orders waiting. Whenever it is free and two or more orders wait, the agent sees a coarse situation, picks FIFO, SPT, EDD or SST (Pinedo [6]), and is charged the lateness that decision made certain (Part 3). It learns on fresh random instances, one run (an episode) after another. Every 100 episodes its choices are scored, without exploring, on 200 validation instances, and the best snapshot, the checkpoint, is kept; 200 other test instances give its honest score.

Read the hero. Untrained, all rules look equal and ties go to FIFO: the curve starts at FIFO's 133.8 minutes. The checkpoint kept comes at episode 4000 with 77.2. On the test instances:

FIFO 137.0

Optimal sequence of each instance (all 720 orders) 65.7

The agent beats the best single rule by 8.9 minutes, 10 %; training seeds 1 to 5 land between 79.1 and 79.4. A state-dependent choice of rule can beat every single rule. What it sees limits it more than its four rules: the best of the 4^5 = 1024 successions of rules (one per decision), chosen in hindsight for each instance, averages 66.1, within 0.4 minutes of the optimum; the agent stops at 79.1 because its 18 coarse situations force one rule onto queues that need different ones. The room depends on the problem: at a 10-minute changeover the agent gains 3.2 minutes (exercise 4), in the workshop nothing measurable.

Part 2

The dispatcher as a Markov decision process

What is a Markov decision process?

In plain words: a game against chance played in turns. At each turn the player sees the situation, chooses a move, receives a score and lands in a new situation that depends, at random, only on the current situation and the move.

More precisely: states s, actions a, transition probabilities \Pr(s' \mid s, a), rewards r and a discount \gamma \in [0, 1]. A policy \pi maps states to actions. The Markov property: s_{k+1} and r_{k+1} depend on the past only through (s_k, a_k).

Decision index
k counts decisions, at clock times t_k (as on the notation page); not the operation index.
Episode
one run: a simulated day, or one toy instance. The agent never sees \Pr, only sampled transitions.

In the toy. There are five decisions per instance; the order the chosen rule picks is painted, after a changeover if its colour differs. The state is one of 18 situations: how many orders wait (2, 3–4, 5–6); the colour situation (every order needs a change; the most urgent has the current colour; the most urgent needs a change but another does not); and whether the most urgent order is tight, its slack d_j - t - p_j less its changeover being below one changeover. Training visited 17. In the first two colour situations EDD and SST pick the same order (SST breaks ties by due date), so they compete only in the third column of the hero's table.

In the workshop. The agent decides when a machine is free and at least two jobs wait in its queue. Decisions are irregular in time (a semi-Markov process): k and the discount count decisions, not minutes. On 20 scenarios generated like the training days (seeds 100000–100009, both levels), under a random policy, a day held 17 to 184 decisions, 80 on average. The chosen rule's job starts; routing stays the earliest-expected-finish rule of chapter B2. The agent is a rule selector, a policy over four heuristics, not over jobs; Part 8 turns on this.

State. jobshop/dqn.py:features returns 29 scaled numbers (slacks divided by 100 minutes, clipped at ±3). With P_j(t) the remaining work of job j by its fastest route, the slack is \mathrm{slack}_j(t) = d_j - t - P_j(t) (in the toy P_j = p_j):

  • for each job the four rules would pick (20): processing time here, changeover, slack, share of operations left, time waited;
  • for the queue (5): length, share late (negative slack), smallest and mean slack, share needing no changeover;
  • for the shop (4): jobs present, share late, share of machines down, pending transport requests.

Is it Markov? Not exactly. The transition runs the simulator of chapter B1 to the next decision anywhere, so s_{k+1} often belongs to another machine, and the features omit other queues, carts and failure clocks. The process is partially observed; the theory below approximates it.

Part 3

A reward that adds up to the tardiness

A reward of 0 at every decision and -\sum_j T_j at the end is correct but nearly useless: with 80 decisions a day, which one caused the lateness? Idea: charge lateness as soon as it is certain.

What is the committed tardiness?

In plain words: the minutes of lateness the shop is already sure to deliver: finished jobs at their real lateness, every other job at the lateness it would have if nothing delayed it further.

More precisely: with \mathcal F_t the jobs finished at time t and \mathcal O_t the other jobs present,

\Theta(t) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal F_t} T_j + \sum_{j \in \mathcal O_t} \max\bigl(0,\ t + P_j(t) - d_j\bigr).

The reward of decision k is r_{k+1} = -\bigl(\Theta(t_{k+1}) - \Theta(t_k)\bigr)/c with c = 50 minutes per unit; the last decision K receives r_{K+1} = -\bigl(\sum_j T_j - \Theta(t_K)\bigr)/c.

By hand, in the toy (P_j = p_j, c = 1), test instance 0 of the widget below. Orders, painting time / due date: A 5/13, B 7/49, C 13/47, D 20/77, E 21/53, F 22/27; B and D red, the others blue. The agent applies EDD, SST, EDD, SPT, SST. At t = 40, A, F and C are done on time; of the waiting orders, B has t + p - d = 40 + 7 - 49 = -2 and D 40 + 20 - 77 = -17, both counting 0, and E 40 + 21 - 53 = 8: \Theta(40) = 8. SPT paints B, which after a 5-minute changeover ends at 52, 3 minutes late, and E's term grows to 52 + 21 - 53 = 20: \Theta(52) = 23. D ends on time at 72, E at 98, 45 minutes late. \Theta is 0, 0, 0, 8, 23 at the five decisions and the total tardiness 48: the rewards 0, 0, -8, -15, -25 sum to -48.

Lemma: \Theta never decreases, and ends at the total tardiness. Follow one job's term.

  • At its arrival r_j, P_j = W_{f(j)}, its fastest-route work, and d_j - r_j \ge 2 W_{f(j)}, so the term enters at 0.
  • While it waits for a machine or a cart, or rides one, P_j is constant and t grows.
  • When an operation starts, P_j jumps from this operation's fastest time to its time on the chosen machine, at least as long. During a changeover or a breakdown P_j is constant; during processing P_j falls as fast as t rises.
  • When its last operation ends at C_j, its term becomes \max(0, C_j - d_j) = T_j, the same value, now frozen.

Each term is nondecreasing, so \Theta is, and once every job is finished \Theta = \sum_j T_j. In particular every reward is \le 0. \square

Theorem: the rewards telescope. For a run with decisions k = 0, \dots, K,

\begin{aligned} \sum_{k=0}^{K} r_{k+1} &= -\frac1c \Bigl[\bigl(\Theta(t_1) - \Theta(t_0)\bigr) + \dots + \bigl(\textstyle\sum_j T_j - \Theta(t_K)\bigr)\Bigr] \\ &= -\frac{\sum_j T_j - \Theta(t_0)}{c}, \end{aligned}

since every intermediate \Theta(t_k) appears once with each sign. \square

The fine print. The sum is exactly -\sum_j T_j/c only when \Theta(t_0) = 0, as in the toy (due dates at least the painting time). On the 20 workshop scenarios above, \Theta(t_0) = 0 on 17, and 1, 5 and 12 minutes on the others: lateness committed before any machine had a choice, the same for every policy, so maximising the expected sum of rewards still exactly minimises the expected total tardiness. On the repository's test scenario (seed 91, 40 jobs, breakdowns, 79 decisions) the rewards sum to -2056/50, the tardiness over c.

A known construction. This reward is the sparse one, -\sum_j T_j/c at the end, plus \Phi(s_{k+1}) - \Phi(s_k) with \Phi = -\Theta/c. Ng, Harada and Russell [4] proved that such potential-based shaping, \gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s), never changes the optimal policy: with \gamma = 1, a second proof.

What discounting breaks. The agent maximises the discounted return \sum_k \gamma^k r_{k+1} with \gamma = 0.97: lateness committed k decisions ahead counts \gamma^k, a weight that halves every 22.8 decisions and is 0.090 at decision 79, the end of a typical day. The effective horizon is 1/(1-\gamma) = 33.3 decisions. On seed 91 the return is -578.3/50 against -2056/50. Maximising it is a different objective, and Ng's theorem would need \gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s) where the code uses \Phi(s') - \Phi(s). Part 5 says why the repository discounts anyway.

Discounted with \gamma = 0.97, the rewards of test instance 0 sum to -43.35; with \gamma = 0.9, to -33.82.

Part 4

Bellman's equation and Q-learning

The return from decision k is G_k = r_{k+1} + \gamma r_{k+2} + \gamma^2 r_{k+3} + \dots.

What is an action value?

In plain words: how good it is, on average, to apply a given rule in a given situation and then go on deciding in a given way, scored over the rest of the run.

More precisely: Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb E[G_k \mid s_k = s, a_k = a, \text{then } \pi], and Q^\star(s, a) = \max_\pi Q^\pi(s, a). A policy picking \arg\max_a Q^\star(s, a) in every state is optimal.

Greedy policy
\pi(s) = \arg\max_a Q(s, a) for an estimate Q.
Bootstrapping
estimating a value from other estimated values (unrelated to the statistical bootstrap of chapter D2).

Bellman's equation, derived. Factoring \gamma out of all terms after the first gives G_k = r_{k+1} + \gamma G_{k+1}. Take expectations given (s_k, a_k) = (s, a), with \pi followed afterwards:

Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb E\bigl[\, r_{k+1} + \gamma\, G_{k+1} \bigm| s_k = s, a_k = a \bigr].

Average G_{k+1} in two stages (the tower rule): over what follows the next state s_{k+1} = s', then over s'. By the Markov property the future after s' ignores how the run got there, and from s' the policy takes \pi(s'), so \mathbb E[G_{k+1} \mid s_{k+1} = s', \text{history}] = Q^\pi(s', \pi(s')) and

Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb E\bigl[\, r_{k+1} + \gamma\, Q^\pi\bigl(s', \pi(s')\bigr) \bigm| s_k = s, a_k = a \bigr].

For the optimal values, the best continuation from s' is to act optimally from there, worth \max_{a'} Q^\star(s', a'): this is the principle of optimality, which we cite from Sutton and Barto [1]. So

\boxed{Q^\star(s, a) = \mathbb E\bigl[\, r_{k+1} + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^\star(s', a') \bigm| s_k = s, a_k = a \bigr]},

with the max taken as 0 when the run ends. It has a unique solution in a finite problem when \gamma < 1 or every run ends (Sutton and Barto, Sections 3.6 and 4.1); for \gamma < 1 the right-hand side is a contraction, and we cite the proof from Puterman [13].

By hand (\gamma = 1). In s_0, rule a costs 2 minutes and leads to s_1; rule b costs 4 and ends the run. In s_1, a costs 6 and b costs 3, both ending it. Backwards: Q^\star(s_1, a) = -6, Q^\star(s_1, b) = -3; Q^\star(s_0, a) = -2 + \max(-6, -3) = -5 and Q^\star(s_0, b) = -4. The best first move is b, although a has the better immediate reward: what a reward-by-reward dispatcher cannot see. With \gamma = 0.5, Q(s_0, a) = -3.5 and a becomes optimal: the discount changes the answer.

Q-learning: the equation from samples. Each transition (s, a, r, s') is one sample of what is inside the expectation. Q-learning moves the estimate a little towards it:

\boxed{Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \alpha\,\delta}, \qquad \delta = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a') - Q(s, a).

(This \delta, the temporal-difference (TD) error, is not the cart allowance.) At Q = Q^\star it has mean zero: Q^\star is the fixed point the updates hover around, a stochastic approximation. Watkins and Dayan [5] proved convergence in a finite Markov problem if every pair is updated infinitely often with steps satisfying \sum \alpha = \infty, \sum \alpha^2 < \infty. The toy's 18 states are not Markov, two different queues sharing a state, so nothing guarantees convergence; this aliasing and the constant \alpha = 0.1 make the hero's curve wobble, hence the best checkpoint is kept, not the last.

Exploring. The \varepsilon-greedy behaviour takes a random rule with probability \varepsilon; in the toy \varepsilon falls linearly from 1, towards 0 at 60 % of the episodes, floored at 0.05 from 57 % on. Q-learning is off-policy: its target's \max_{a'} learns the greedy policy while behaving otherwise. Starting at Q = 0 when rewards are \le 0 makes untried rules look best (optimistic initialisation).

What the toy agent learned (the hero's table). In the conflict column, with three or more orders waiting and the urgent order tight, it takes the short job (SPT): clearing short orders first limits how many others become late. Nobody programmed this.

Part 5

From a table to a network: Double DQN

The workshop's state is 29 real numbers: no table can hold it. The deep Q-network of Mnih et al. [2] replaces the table by a fitted function.

What is Q_\theta?

In plain words: a function with adjustable numbers \theta that takes the 29 features and returns four scores, one per rule, fitted to targets by nudging the numbers downhill on an error (gradient descent).

More precisely: a neural network 29 \to 64 \to 64 \to 4: each layer multiplies its input by a weight matrix and adds biases, and all but the last replace negative values by 0 (ReLU); 6,340 parameters. The steps are taken by Adam, a gradient method with a step size per parameter.

Online network
Q_\theta, the one being trained.
Target network
Q_{\theta^-}, a slow copy of it used to build the targets.

Why fitting is Q-learning. For a table, take the error \tfrac12\bigl(y - Q(s, a)\bigr)^2 with the target y = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a') held fixed. Its derivative in Q(s, a) is -\delta, so a gradient step of size \alpha is exactly the Q-learning update. The network does the same through \theta:

y = r + \gamma\,(1 - \text{end}) \max_{a'} Q_{\theta^-}(s', a'), \qquad \min_\theta\ \mathbb E\bigl[h\bigl(y - Q_\theta(s, a)\bigr)\bigr],

where end = 1 if the transition ends the run, else 0, and h is the loss below. The target is held constant in the gradient although built from estimates: a semi-gradient method. Three devices make it stable:

  • Experience replay (Lin [7]): each step trains on a random batch of past transitions, breaking the correlation of successive decisions.
  • A target network \theta^-, so the target does not move with every step: the soft update of Lillicrap et al. [8], \theta^- \leftarrow (1 - \beta)\theta^- + \beta\theta (the code's tau_soft_update; \tau is the travel time here), with \beta = 0.005, halves an old weight's influence every 139 steps.
  • A robust loss: the Huber loss [9], h(u) = \tfrac12 u^2 for |u| \le 1 and |u| - \tfrac12 beyond, so one disastrous day does not give a huge gradient.

Why discount? The targets are built from the network's own estimates; with \gamma < 1 an error shrinks by \gamma at each step back and they stay bounded: Part 3's change of objective, traded for stability.

The overestimation bias. For any random variables, \mathbb E[\max_a X_a] \ge \max_a \mathbb E[X_a]: for each fixed a', \max_a X_a \ge X_{a'}, so \mathbb E[\max_a X_a] \ge \mathbb E[X_{a'}] for every a', the best one included. Two actions by hand: both rules are worth 0 and each estimate is +1 or -1 with probability \tfrac12, independently; the four cases give maxima 1, 1, 1, -1, so \mathbb E[\max] = 0.5 while \max \mathbb E = 0. For two actions of equal value with independent \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2) errors the bias is \sigma/\sqrt\pi = 0.564\,\sigma (exercise 3). The DQN target takes this max over noisy estimates, and bootstrapping copies the bias backwards (Thrun and Schwartz [10]).

Double DQN (van Hasselt [11]; van Hasselt, Guez and Silver [3]) chooses with one network and evaluates with the other:

\boxed{y = r + \gamma\,(1 - \text{end})\; Q_{\theta^-}\bigl(s', \arg\max_{a'} Q_\theta(s', a')\bigr)}.

If the two estimates had independent errors, the value read for the chosen \hat a would be unbiased given \hat a, with mean \mathbb E[Q^\star(s', \hat a)] \le \max_a Q^\star(s', a): no upward bias (exactly 0 in the coin example). The two networks are not independent, one lagging the other, so the bias is reduced, not removed.

The configuration used (DQNConfig, the better of two sets compared on validation), besides the above: Adam with learning rate 5 \times 10^{-4}; batches of 64 from a buffer of 50,000, learning from 1,000 transitions on; gradient norm clipped at 10; \varepsilon = 0.05 + 0.95\, e^{-N/30000} after N decisions, 0.399 at 30,000 and 0.068 at the end (seed 3); 1,500 days of 60 jobs, validated every 25.

Part 6

The code: the reward and the training step

jobshop/dqn.py · 105-115

def estimated_tardiness(shop: Shop) -> int:
    """Tardiness already certain at this time: finished jobs count in full, an unfinished job
    counts for the lateness it would have if all its remaining work started now, without waiting.
    It never decreases and equals the total tardiness of the run when every job is finished."""
    total = 0
    for j in shop.jobs.values():
        if j.completion is not None:
            total += max(0, j.completion - j.due)
        else:
            total += max(0, shop.now + remaining_work(shop, j) - j.due)
    return total

\Theta(t) term by term: a finished job adds \max(0, C_j - d_j), any other job \max(0, t + P_j(t) - d_j), with remaining_work returning P_j(t): what is left of the operation in progress plus the fastest times of the later ones. Changeovers are not counted, so a job's term grows with t during one; counting a queued job's future changeover would let \Theta drop when its machine changes family.

jobshop/dqn.py · Learner.act

    def act(self, shop: Shop, state: np.ndarray) -> int:
        """Epsilon-greedy action; closes the previous transition with the tardiness added since."""
        accrued = estimated_tardiness(shop)
        if self._pending is not None:
            prev_state, prev_action, prev_accrued = self._pending
            reward = -(accrued - prev_accrued) / self.config.reward_scale
            self.buffer.push(prev_state, prev_action, reward, state, False)
            self.optimize()
        if self.rng.random() < epsilon_by_step(self.steps_done, self.config):
            action = self.rng.randrange(len(ACTIONS))
        else:
            action = greedy_action(self.policy_net, state)
        self.steps_done += 1
        self._pending = (state, action, accrued)
        return action

accrued is \Theta(t_k). A pending decision's transition is now complete: reward -(\Theta(t_{k+1}) - \Theta(t_k))/c with reward_scale = 50, next state the one just observed; it goes to the buffer and one gradient step follows. Then the \varepsilon-greedy action, and (state, action, \Theta(t_k)) waits for the next decision, perhaps on another machine. end_episode closes the last one with the true total tardiness and a terminal flag: the telescoping theorem in code, checked by the test test_rewards_of_an_episode_sum_to_minus_the_total_tardiness.

jobshop/dqn.py · compute_td_targets

def compute_td_targets(rewards, next_states, terminals, policy_net, target_net, gamma: float, double_dqn: bool):
    """y = r + gamma * (1 - terminal) * Q_target(s', a*), a* chosen by the online net in Double DQN."""
    with torch.no_grad():
        next_q_target = target_net(next_states)
        if double_dqn:
            best_actions = policy_net(next_states).argmax(dim=1, keepdim=True)
        else:
            best_actions = next_q_target.argmax(dim=1, keepdim=True)
        return rewards + gamma * next_q_target.gather(1, best_actions) * (1.0 - terminals)

The boxed target. best_actions is the argmax of the online network in Double DQN (of the target network otherwise); the value is read from the target network. The tests check both branches: with online values (0, 9, 0, 0) and target values (4, 1, 7, 0), Double DQN returns 1 and plain DQN 7. Learner.optimize feeds it batches of 64, applies smooth_l1_loss (Huber) between Q_\theta(s, a) and the targets, clips, steps Adam and soft-updates the target network: one step per decision, 117,671 per training seed on average.

Part 7

Training curves and checkpoint selection

scripts/train_dqn.py trains five agents in parallel (training seeds 0–4), each on 1,500 days of 60 jobs alternating the breakdown levels, in 19–20 minutes; the scenario seeds, 100001 to 105500, give 5,500 distinct days, since agent k's days 1001–1500 are agent k+1's days 1–500. Every 25 days the greedy network is scored on 20 validation runs (seeds 5000–5009, both levels) by mean tardiness per job; each seed keeps its best checkpoint, and the best seed is kept: seed 3, 21.98 at day 1150. On the same runs the rules score FIFO 47.54, SPT 32.96, EDD 26.99 and SST 24.29.

Selection finds lucky networks. The validation line does not trend: seed 3's 60 checks wander between 21.98 and 33.81. Yet four of the five kept checkpoints beat SST on validation, while only 3 % of 300 checks do: the minimum of 60 noisy scores is biased low, \mathbb E[\min] \le \min \mathbb E, Part 5's inequality turned around. The kept agent's 2.31-minute margin over SST should vanish on new days; chapter D2 treats this optimism; exercise 6 shows it.

Going further: what the training histories show

Over the five seeds, training-day tardiness falls from 28.9 (first 100 days) to 24.0 (last 100), mostly because \varepsilon falls to about 0.07: the agent explores less, not chooses better. The last 20 validation scores of a seed have a standard deviation of 2.32 minutes on average.

Both intervals contain zero: no measurable difference from the best rule, and no gain over it; they allow the agent to be up to 1.5 and 5.1 minutes worse. It does stay close to the best rule at both levels although that rule changes (EDD, then SST), which no single rule manages; and the "best rule" is picked on the test itself, which flatters it slightly.

The policy class. At each decision the agent chooses among at most four jobs, at one machine, now. It cannot pick a fifth job, keep a machine idle for a job about to arrive, coordinate machines so that each runs one family, or route; the re-planning controller of chapter C3 does all that. Changeover time per calm day: 635 minutes for CP-SAT, 918 for SST, 1,025 for EDD, 988 for the agent. The hindsight row gains only 0.38 and 2.15 minutes, but it is a lower bound on the room (in the toy, 13.0 where successions left 21.9), and the 4^{80} or so successions of a day cannot be enumerated.

Credit assignment and noise. The reward between two decisions mixes this decision's consequences with other machines', routing's and breakdowns'; in a calm 60-job day under an untrained agent, 36 of 67 rewards are exactly 0. Deciding which decision deserves the blame is the credit assignment problem. And validation scores wander by as much as the effect sought.

What could work better. Change the policy class, not the budget: choose the job directly, scoring every waiting job; read a graph, the disjunctive graph of chapter A3, with a graph neural network as in Zhang et al. [12]; or decide when to re-plan, keeping the controller of chapter C3. Judge them against a stronger baseline, the ATCS rule of chapter B2. The result concerns this agent, with this state, these actions and this budget, not reinforcement learning in general.

Part 9

Common pitfalls

  1. "The rewards always sum to minus the total tardiness." Only undiscounted, and only up to \Theta(t_0), the same for every policy.
  2. "Discounting is a technicality." With \gamma = 0.97 lateness committed at the end of a day counts 0.090, and a discount can change the best action (Part 4).
  3. "A Q-value predicts the tardiness in minutes." It is a discounted sum of scaled rewards, learned through a biased max from incomplete features. Only the ranking is used.
  4. "The best validation score is the agent's performance." It is the minimum of 60 noisy checks per seed over five seeds, biased low. The test measures.
  5. "A rule selector finds the best schedule if trained long enough." In the toy, perfectly switched rules would reach 66.1, yet the agent stops at 79.1 whatever the seed: what an agent sees bounds it as much as what it may do.
Part 10

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question before opening it.

1. In the two-decision example of Part 4, with γ = 0.8, which first move is optimal?

Q^\star(s_0, a) = -2 + 0.8 \times (-3) = -4.4, below Q^\star(s_0, b) = -4, so b. The two tie at \gamma = 2/3; below that, a wins.

2. Why is the agent's problem only approximately Markov?

The features omit much of the shop and successive states belong to different machines: equal states can have different futures.

3. Why does the committed tardiness never decrease?

Each job's t + P_j(t) is constant while processed, grows otherwise, and jumps up on a slower machine; finished jobs are frozen at T_j.

4. State the telescoping identity precisely.

\sum_{k=0}^{K} r_{k+1} = -\bigl(\sum_j T_j - \Theta(t_0)\bigr)/c, undiscounted; -\sum_j T_j/c when \Theta(t_0) = 0.

5. What does Q-learning do with one transition (s, a, r, s')?

It moves Q(s, a) a fraction \alpha towards r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a'), one sample of the right-hand side of Bellman's equation.

6. Why is \mathbb E[\max_a X_a] \ge \max_a \mathbb E[X_a], and why does it matter?

Because \max_a X_a \ge X_{a'} for each a' and expectation preserves it. The DQN target maximises over noisy estimates, so it is biased upwards, and bootstrapping spreads the bias.

7. What does Double DQN change?

The action in the target is chosen by the online network and evaluated by the target network: Q_{\theta^-}(s', \arg\max_{a'} Q_\theta(s', a')).

8. The agent beats SST on validation (21.98 against 24.29) but not on test. Contradiction?

No. The 21.98 is the minimum of 300 noisy checks, selected on those very runs; only 3 % of checks beat SST. On unseen days the margin disappears.

  3. The bias of a maximum. For independent X_1, X_2 \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2) show \mathbb E[\max(X_1, X_2)] = \sigma/\sqrt\pi, and that the double estimator \mathbb E[Y_{\hat a}], \hat a = \arg\max_a X_a, Y an independent copy, is 0.

    Hint

    \max(x, y) = \frac{x + y}{2} + \frac{|x - y|}{2}, and \mathbb E|Z| = s\sqrt{2/\pi} for Z \sim \mathcal N(0, s^2).

    Solution

    By symmetry, \mathbb E|Z| = \frac{2}{s\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_0^\infty z e^{-z^2/2s^2} dz = \frac{2s^2}{s\sqrt{2\pi}} = s\sqrt{2/\pi}.     Solution

    The whole solution is that block of the numbers script, which runs it. With pick(a, wait, f) the order rule a picks and state(t, f, wait) the situation, its heart is:

    def run(inst, policy, learn=None):     # returns the total tardiness
    t, f, wait, done, prev = 0, 0, list(inst), 0, None
    while wait:
        o = wait[0]
        if len(wait) >= 2:
            s = state(t, f, wait)
            theta = done + sum(max(0, t + q[2] - q[3]) for q in wait)   # committed tardiness
            if prev and learn:
                learn(prev[0], prev[1], -(theta - prev[2]), s)
            a = policy(s)
            prev, o = (s, a, theta), pick(a, wait, f)
        wait.remove(o)
        t, f = t + setup(f, o[1]) + o[2], o[1]
        done += max(0, t - o[3])
    if learn:
        learn(prev[0], prev[1], -(done - prev[2]), None)
    return done

def train(seed=1, episodes=5000, alpha=0.1, gamma=1.0):
    r, Q, best = rng(seed), [[0.0] * 4 for _ in range(18)], (math.inf, None, 0)
    def behave(s):
        eps = max(0.05, 1 - ep / (0.6 * episodes))
        return math.floor(r() * 4) if r() < eps else greedy(Q)(s)
    def learn(s, a, rew, s2):
        target = rew + (0.0 if s2 is None else gamma * max(Q[s2]))
        Q[s][a] += alpha * (target - Q[s][a])
    for ep in range(episodes + 1):
        if ep % 100 == 0:              # checkpoint
            v = score(greedy(Q), VAL)
            if v < best[0]:
                best = (v, [q[:] for q in Q], ep)
        if ep < episodes:
            run(instance(r), behave, learn)
    return best

Part 12

The chapter on one page

  1. The MDP: decide when a machine is free and two or more jobs wait; 29 features; four rules as actions; only approximately Markov.
  2. The reward: minus the increase of the committed tardiness \Theta(t); the rewards telescope to -(\sum_j T_j - \Theta(t_0))/c. Discounting changes the objective.
  3. Q-learning: one sample of Bellman's equation at a time, a gradient step on a squared error; in the toy 79.1 against 88.0, with 66.1 within reach of its rules.
  4. Double DQN: a network, replay, a soft target network, Huber loss, against \mathbb E[\max] \ge \max \mathbb E; on test +0.4 [−0.7, +1.5] and +1.3 [−2.1, +5.1] min from the best rule: the policy class, credit assignment and noise.

The vocabulary, in one table

Word Meaning
Markov decision process States, actions, transitions, rewards, discount; the future depends on the present only.
Committed tardiness \Theta(t) Lateness already certain.
Telescoping reward Rewards that are differences of one quantity, so they sum to its total change.
Effective horizon 1/(1-\gamma) decisions.
Q^\pi, Q^\star, Bellman equation Value of an action in a state; Q^\star = \mathbb E[r + \gamma \max Q^\star].
TD error, off-policy \delta = r + \gamma \max Q(s',\cdot) - Q(s,a); learning greedy values while exploring.
Overestimation, Double DQN \mathbb E[\max] \ge \max \mathbb E; choose with one network, evaluate with the other.
Rule succession, aliasing One rule per decision in order; different situations sharing one state.
Credit assignment, policy class Which decision caused an outcome; the policies an agent can represent.

Satellite Pass Planning from Scratch · Chapter 04 of 10

Seeing the ground

A satellite can listen to a ship only while the ship is inside its sensor's cone and the satellite is high enough above the ship's horizon. This chapter turns those two sentences into geometry: one triangle between the Earth's centre, the satellite and the target, three angles that always add up to 90°, and the two dot products of the package's visibility test. From them follow the size of the footprint, which of the package's two limits actually decides visibility at 550 km, how long a pass lasts and why it gets shorter off the track, and what is lost when visibility is checked only every 10 seconds. The windows computed here are the raw material of every opportunity the planner chooses from.

Part 1

One triangle

Two points of view. Stand on the deck of a ship and look for the satellite: it is somewhere above your horizon, at an angle called its elevation. Near the horizon the line of sight crosses a lot of atmosphere, and hills or the ship's own superstructure may be in the way, so a receiver usually ignores satellites below some minimum elevation, the elevation mask. Now sit on the satellite and look down: straight down is nadir, and the ship is at some angle from it, the off-nadir angle. An antenna or a sensor covers a cone around nadir; a ship outside the cone cannot be heard, however high the satellite is in its sky. The scenario uses both limits for targets, a 55° cone and a 10° mask; for ground stations it uses no cone and a 5° mask, as suits a steerable station antenna.

Both angles live in one plane: the plane through the Earth's centre O, the satellite S and the target P. The three points make a triangle, and everything in this chapter is read off it.

What are the angles of visibility?

In plain words: how high the satellite stands in the target's sky (elevation), how far from straight down the satellite must look (off-nadir), and how far apart the two are over the Earth's surface, as an angle at the centre (central angle).

More precisely: with R the Earth's radius, h the altitude and \rho = \lVert P - S\rVert the slant range,

Elevation \varepsilon
The angle at P between the line of sight to S and the local horizontal plane: \sin\varepsilon = \hat u_P \cdot (S - P)/\rho, with \hat u_P = P/R the local up.
Off-nadir angle \eta
The angle at S between nadir -S/\lVert S\rVert and the line of sight to P: \cos\eta = \hat n_S \cdot (P - S)/\rho.
Earth central angle \lambda
The angle at O between S and P; the ground distance from the sub-satellite point to the target is R\lambda.
Footprint
The set of ground points visible from the satellite at one instant: a circle of central angle \lambda_{\max} around the sub-satellite point.

The angles add up to 90°. Look at the interior angles of the triangle OSP. At O it is \lambda. At S the side SO points to nadir, so the angle is \eta. At P, the side PO points straight down and the side PS points \varepsilon above the horizontal; from straight down to the horizontal is 90°, so the interior angle is 90^\circ + \varepsilon. The angles of a triangle add up to 180°:

\lambda + \eta + (90^\circ + \varepsilon) = 180^\circ \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \eta + \varepsilon + \lambda = 90^\circ .

The law of sines. In any triangle each side is proportional to the sine of the opposite angle. The side OP = R is opposite the angle \eta at S; the side OS = R + h is opposite the angle 90^\circ + \varepsilon at P, whose sine is \cos\varepsilon; the side SP = \rho is opposite \lambda. So

\frac{\sin\eta}{R} = \frac{\cos\varepsilon}{R + h} = \frac{\sin\lambda}{\rho} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sin\eta = \frac{R}{R + h}\,\cos\varepsilon, \quad \rho = \frac{R\sin\lambda}{\sin\eta}.

Two equations, three angles: fix any one and the other two follow. At 550 km, R/(R+h) = 6371/6921 = 0.92053. On the horizon (\varepsilon = 0) it gives \eta = \arcsin 0.92053 = 67.00^\circ and \lambda = 23.00^\circ: from 550 km the whole visible Earth fits in a cone of half-angle 67°, and its edge is 23.00° of arc, 2557 km, away. Move the target with the widget's \lambda slider and check that the readout of the sum never leaves 90°.

Part 2

The visibility test, line by line

The package never computes \lambda or uses the law of sines. It works with vectors, which is simpler and has no special cases. Here is the whole test (satplan/geometry.py):

def visibility_mask(sat_pos, ground_pos, max_off_nadir_deg, min_elevation_deg) -> np.ndarray:
    """Boolean (T, J): the ground point is within the sensor cone and above the elevation mask.

    sat_pos: (T, 3); ground_pos: (T, J, 3).
    max_off_nadir_deg = None disables the sensor-cone test (used for ground-station contacts).
    """
    los = ground_pos - sat_pos[:, None, :]  # satellite -> ground
    rng = np.linalg.norm(los, axis=-1)
    up = ground_pos / np.linalg.norm(ground_pos, axis=-1, keepdims=True)
    sin_elev = -np.einsum("tjk,tjk->tj", los, up) / rng
    ok = sin_elev >= np.sin(np.radians(min_elevation_deg))
    if max_off_nadir_deg is not None:
        nadir = -sat_pos / np.linalg.norm(sat_pos, axis=-1, keepdims=True)
        cos_off = np.einsum("tjk,tk->tj", los, nadir) / rng
        ok &= cos_off >= np.cos(np.radians(max_off_nadir_deg))
    return ok

Both positions are inertial: the satellite's from position_eci (chapter 02), the targets' from ground_points_eci (chapter 03). Line by line:

  • Line of sight. los = ground_pos - sat_pos[:, None, :] is the vector from the satellite to each target, P - S, for every time t and target j; [:, None, :] lets one satellite position serve all J targets. Its length rng is the slant range \rho.
  • Local up. On a sphere centred at the origin, the vertical at a point is the point's own direction: up = P/\lVert P\rVert.
  • Elevation. The sine of the elevation is the component of the unit vector from target to satellite along the local up. That vector is -los / rng, hence the minus sign: sin_elev = -(P - S)\cdot\hat u_P/\rho. The einsum("tjk,tjk->tj", ...) is a dot product over the last axis for every pair (t, j).
  • First test. \sin is increasing on [-90^\circ, 90^\circ], so \varepsilon \ge \varepsilon_{\min} is the same as \sin\varepsilon \ge \sin\varepsilon_{\min}, and no arcsine is needed.
  • Nadir. nadir = -S/\lVert S\rVert, the unit vector from the satellite toward the Earth's centre.
  • Off-nadir. cos_off is the cosine of the angle between the line of sight and nadir; the subscripts "tjk,tk->tj" pair each target's line of sight with the satellite's one nadir at that time. Cosine is decreasing on [0^\circ, 180^\circ], so \eta \le \eta_{\max} becomes \cos\eta \ge \cos\eta_{\max}.
  • Both. ok &= ... keeps a point only if it passes both tests; with max_off_nadir_deg=None only the elevation test runs, the rule for stations.

The elevation test alone also removes everything behind the Earth, since a point that cannot see the satellite has it below its horizon, \varepsilon < 0. The package's test test_point_below_satellite_is_visible_and_antipode_is_not checks exactly that: a point under an equatorial satellite is visible, its antipode is not. The page's port does the same computation for one pair, returning the angles instead of a Boolean (Sat.lookAngles, which the hero demo and the triangle widget call).

Checking the triangle against the code. Put an equatorial satellite above longitude 0 at t = 0 and run visibility_mask on points along the equator every 0.001°. The last visible longitude is 7.856° with the targets' limits, 14.967° with the 10° mask alone, 18.504° with the stations' 5° mask: the central angles the law of sines gives in the next part, 7.86°, 14.97° and 18.50°.

Part 3

Which limit binds

A target must pass both tests, so its footprint is the smaller of two circles: the one the cone allows and the one the mask allows. Put each limit into the law of sines, at 550 km.

The 55° cone. At its edge \eta = 55^\circ, so \cos\varepsilon = \sin 55^\circ / 0.92053 = 0.81915/0.92053 = 0.88987, \varepsilon = 27.14^\circ, and \lambda = 90 - 55 - 27.14 = 7.86^\circ. A target at the edge of the cone sees the satellite 27.14° above its horizon, far above the 10° mask.

The 10° mask. Alone, it would allow \sin\eta = 0.92053 \cos 10^\circ, \eta = 65.03^\circ, \lambda = 14.97^\circ: a footprint almost twice as wide. But that wider circle is outside the cone. So for targets the cone binds, and the 10° mask never does: whenever a target is inside the cone, its elevation is at least 27.14°.

The footprint radii are ground distances R\lambda from the sub-satellite point. The targets' footprint covers 2.39 million km² at a time; a station's 13.19 million km², more than five times as much. That is why contacts are long and frequent compared with target windows: a station is reached from a wider circle. In the small instance, the 19 contacts (merged over the four stations, per satellite) last from 200 to 1000 s, median 560 s, while no target window exceeds 240 s.

When would the mask bind? The elevation at the cone's edge falls as the satellite climbs, because the Earth looks smaller from further away. Setting \varepsilon = 10^\circ at \eta = 55^\circ gives (R+h)/R = \cos 10^\circ/\sin 55^\circ, so h = 1288 km. Below that the cone binds; above it the mask. At 400 km the elevation at the cone's edge is 29.5° (footprint 615 km), at 800 km 22.8° (1359 km), at 1200 km 13.2° (2420 km). Try it with the altitude slider of the triangle widget: the caption says which limit closes the window.

What this means for the model. At the scenario's altitude, the 10° elevation mask for targets is redundant: removing it would not change a single window. It is not wrong to keep it, since it states a physical requirement and would matter for a higher orbit or a wider cone; but whoever tunes the scenario should know that the cone, not the mask, sets the footprint. The hero demo shows it: drag the elevation mask from 0° up to 27° and the window does not change; the readout "limit that closes the window" stays "sensor cone".

Part 4

How long a pass lasts

A target is visible while the sub-satellite point is within the central angle \lambda_{\max} = 7.86^\circ of it. The footprint slides along the ground track with the satellite, so the window is the time the target spends inside a moving circle.

Overhead. If the track passes right over the target, the circle's whole diameter crosses it: an arc of 2\lambda_{\max} at the orbital rate n = 2\pi/T,

\Delta t_{\max} \approx \frac{2\lambda_{\max}}{n} = \frac{2 \times 7.86^\circ}{360^\circ} \times 5730.1\ \text{s} = 250.1\ \text{s}.

Off the track. If the target is at a cross-track central angle \beta from the track, it meets only a chord of the circle. In the spherical right triangle formed by the target, its closest point on the track and the sub-satellite point at an along-track angle \Delta u, the spherical Pythagoras theorem says \cos\lambda = \cos\beta\cos\Delta u. The target is visible while \lambda \le \lambda_{\max}, so

\Delta t(\beta) \approx \frac{2}{n}\,\arccos\frac{\cos\lambda_{\max}}{\cos\beta}, \qquad \beta < \lambda_{\max}.

The duration falls slowly at first (a chord near the centre is almost a diameter) and steeply near the edge. The table compares the formula with the exact window of the hero demo's geometry, which includes the Earth's rotation, and with the window recorded on the 10 s grid.

The formula is about 1 % long: it ignores the Earth turning under the orbit, which for a retrograde orbit adds about 1 % to the speed of the footprint over the ground and makes passes slightly shorter. The Earth's rotation also makes a pass depend on the side of the track: at 300 km to the left the exact window is 232.2 s, at 300 km to the right 233.1 s. A target within 600 km of the track, more than two thirds of the footprint's 874 km half-width, still gets about three minutes (179.5 s at 600 km); at the very edge a window of 22.5 s is worth nothing to a task of 30 s.

Stations. With no cone and a 5° mask, the station's footprint is 18.50° of central angle, and a station directly under the track sees the satellite for 583.2 s, 9.7 minutes; at 300 km from the track, 576.1 s. Press "station limits" in the hero demo to see it.

Part 5

Windows on a 10 s grid

Discretisation. The exact edges of a window are the roots of \varepsilon(t) = \varepsilon_{\min} or \eta(t) = \eta_{\max}; the package does not solve for them. It evaluates the mask on a grid of times t_i = 10\,i s, times = np.arange(0, horizon + 1, cfg.time_step_s) with time_step_s = 10, and reads windows off the Boolean sequence: a window is a maximal run of consecutive True samples.

def boolean_runs(mask) -> list[tuple[int, int]]:
    """Index ranges [i0, i1] (inclusive) of consecutive True values in a 1-D mask."""
    m = np.concatenate([[False], np.asarray(mask, dtype=bool), [False]])
    d = np.diff(m.astype(np.int8))
    starts = np.flatnonzero(d == 1)
    ends = np.flatnonzero(d == -1) - 1
    return list(zip(starts.tolist(), ends.tolist()))

Padding with False at both ends guarantees that every run has a rise and a fall. The difference of consecutive values is +1 where a run starts and -1 just after it ends. Because of the padding, position k of d compares padded samples k and k+1, that is original samples k-1 and k: a rise at k means sample k is the first True, a fall at k means sample k-1 was the last. Hence starts as is and ends minus one. The test boolean_runs([0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1]) == [(1, 2), (4, 4), (6, 8)] covers a run at the end and a run of one sample. _windows then records each run as the window [t_{i_0}, t_{i_1}].

What the grid loses. The true window starts somewhere between the last invisible sample t_{i_0 - 1} and the first visible one t_{i_0}, and ends between t_{i_1} and t_{i_1+1}. The recorded window [t_{i_0}, t_{i_1}] is inside the true one, and shorter by between 0 and 10 s at each end, between 0 and 20 s in total. Shift the grid in the hero demo and watch the two losses trade off: at the default the recorded window is 220 s against 232.2 s, 3.1 s lost at the start and 9.1 s at the end.

Measured on the small instance. code/ch04_numbers.py bisects the true edges of every window to a fraction of a microsecond. For the 169 windows not cut by the ends of the horizon (24 windows start at t = 0 or end at 21 600 s), the grid loses 5.20 s at the start and 5.39 s at the end on average, at most 9.97 s and 9.94 s. The total underestimate has mean 10.58 s, median 10.42 s, and ranges from 0.86 s to 18.91 s: a median of 5.4 % of the window. Every recorded window lies inside its exact window, as the argument above says.

Why 10 s is acceptable. Three reasons, in order of importance.

  • It is conservative. A task placed inside the recorded window is inside the true window. The grid can lose opportunities, never invent infeasible ones. A plan that the checker of chapter 06 accepts on the grid is feasible in continuous time as far as visibility goes.
  • It is small against the tasks and windows. Tasks last 30 to 120 s and a typical window about 200 s; an error of about 10 s shifts what fits by a fraction of a task. Of the 193 windows, only 2 are wrongly dropped: target 89 (110 s recorded, 124.9 s in truth, needs 120 s) and target 30 (110 s recorded, 120.4 s, needs 120 s).
  • A finer grid buys little. Rebuilding the small instance with time_step_s = 1 gives 169 opportunities instead of 168, 115 reachable targets instead of 114, and a mean window of 195.3 s instead of 186.7 s; 5 s gives 169 and 191.6 s. The mask is ten times larger for ten times less error, cheap at this size but a real cost on the large instance (12 satellites, 600 targets).

An alternative that keeps the grid's speed and loses nothing is to refine only the edges: bisect between t_{i_0 - 1} and t_{i_0}, as the script does. The package does not, and the grid has a second merit: integer window edges, with transition times rounded up to whole seconds (chapter 06), keep every start time and every precedence of chapters 06–09 in integers, which CP-SAT requires. Refined edges would have to be rounded inward to keep that property.

Visibility is one triangle and two dot products. At 550 km the 55° cone decides it, the 10° mask never does, and passes last at most about four minutes; sampling them every 10 s costs about 10 s per window, always on the safe side.

Part 6

Check your understanding

1. Why is the interior angle of the triangle at the target 90^\circ + \varepsilon and not 90^\circ - \varepsilon?

The two sides at the target are PO, straight down, and PS, \varepsilon above the horizontal. From straight down to the horizontal is 90°, and from there up to the line of sight another \varepsilon.

2. At 550 km, what elevation does a target at the edge of the 55° cone have, and what follows for the 10° mask?

\cos\varepsilon = \sin 55^\circ/0.92053, \varepsilon = 27.14^\circ. Every target inside the cone sees the satellite at least 27.14° high, so the 10° mask never removes a target the cone keeps: it does not bind.

3. Why does the code compare \sin\varepsilon and \cos\eta instead of the angles?

It saves an arcsine and an arccosine per pair, and it is exact because sine is increasing on [-90^\circ, 90^\circ] and cosine decreasing on [0^\circ, 180^\circ]. Note the reversed inequality for the cosine.

4. Why do ground stations have much longer contacts than targets have windows?

No cone and a 5° mask give a footprint of 18.50° of central angle (2058 km) against 7.86° (874 km) for targets: the satellite crosses a circle more than twice as wide, 583.2 s overhead against 247.6 s.

5. A target is 700 km from the ground track. Roughly how long is its window, and which tasks fit?

The formula gives \tfrac{2}{n}\arccos(\cos 7.86^\circ/\cos 6.30^\circ) \approx 150 s (the exact window in the hero geometry is 147.8 s, recorded as 140 s): any of the 30–120 s tasks fits, but a 120 s task has little room to move.

6. What does boolean_runs([1, 1, 0, 1]) return, and why does it need the padding?

[(0, 1), (3, 3)]. Without the leading False the first run would have no rise in the difference; without the trailing one the last run would have no fall.

7. Can a window recorded on the 10 s grid be longer than the true window?

No. Its first and last samples are visible, so they lie inside the true window, and the recorded window is between them. It is shorter by up to 10 s at each end; measured on the small instance, 10.58 s on average.

8. Two windows of the small instance were dropped although their task would have fitted. Is the planner's optimum wrong?

It is the optimum of the discretised problem, which is slightly more restrictive than the continuous one. Two windows out of 193, both for 120 s tasks with 0.4 s and 4.9 s to spare, could only increase the value if they were the best choice for their targets. A 1 s grid recovers the second (target 89 on satellite 0: 169 opportunities, 115 reachable targets); the first still records less than 120 s.

Exercises

  1. The footprint from scratch. Write a function footprint(h, max_off_nadir, min_elev) returning the central angle and ground radius of the footprint, taking the smaller of the two limits. Check it against the table of Part 3 and against visibility_mask along the equator.
    Hint

    Each limit gives a \lambda through \sin\eta = \tfrac{R}{R+h}\cos\varepsilon and \eta + \varepsilon + \lambda = 90^\circ. If \sin\eta_{\max} > R/(R+h), the cone is wider than the Earth and only the mask counts.

    Solution 
    def footprint(h, max_off_nadir=None, min_elev=0.0):
    k = R_EARTH / (R_EARTH + h)
    lam_mask = 90 - min_elev - np.degrees(np.arcsin(k * np.cos(np.radians(min_elev))))
    lam = lam_mask
    if max_off_nadir is not None and np.sin(np.radians(max_off_nadir)) <= k:
        eps = np.degrees(np.arccos(np.sin(np.radians(max_off_nadir)) / k))
        lam = min(lam, 90 - max_off_nadir - eps)
    return lam, np.radians(lam) * R_EARTH

Part 9

Scope and further reading

Scope. The chapter covers the package's visibility model exactly: a spherical Earth, a point target, a sensor that is a perfect cone around nadir, and a binary visible/not visible. It leaves out the Earth's flattening and terrain, atmospheric refraction near the horizon, antenna gain patterns (a real receiver hears better near the centre of its beam than at its edge), the link budget that decides whether a signal at 1063 km of slant range is strong enough, and the satellite's own pointing limits beyond the cone. Its reconfiguration between targets is chapter 06's subject. The scenario is synthetic: the 55° cone, the masks and the targets describe no real sensor or system. Next, chapter 05 turns the windows of this chapter into the planning problem's opportunities, with their revolutions and memory segments.

  • [1]
    J. R. Wertz and W. J. Larson (eds.). Space Mission Analysis and Design, 3rd edition. Microcosm Press and Kluwer, 1999. Its chapter on space mission geometry relates elevation, nadir angle, Earth central angle and slant range through the same triangle, and gives the pass-duration formulas of Part 4.
  • [2]
    H. D. Curtis. Orbital Mechanics for Engineering Students, 3rd edition. Butterworth-Heinemann, 2014. Topocentric coordinates, elevation and azimuth of a satellite seen from the ground.
  • [3]
    D. A. Vallado. Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications, 4th edition. Microcosm Press, 2013. Look angles and visibility, including the ellipsoidal Earth and refraction left out here.
  • [4]
    M. Lemaître, G. Verfaillie, F. Jouhaud, J.-M. Lachiver and N. Bataille. Selecting and scheduling observations of agile satellites. Aerospace Science and Technology, 6(5), 2002. A classic formulation of the selection-and-scheduling problem that the later chapters of this course solve, with visibility windows as its input.

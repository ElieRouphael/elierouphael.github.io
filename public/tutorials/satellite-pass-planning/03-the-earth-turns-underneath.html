Satellite Pass Planning from Scratch · Chapter 03 of 10

The Earth turns underneath

Chapter 02 placed an orbit in a frame fixed among the stars. The targets of the planning problem, though, are ships in the Gulf of Guinea and the North Sea, and they ride on a planet that turns once every 23 hours 56 minutes. This chapter puts the two frames together: how a point on the ground moves in the inertial frame, where the satellite is above the map, why its track slides 23.94° west on every revolution, why it never climbs above 82.4°, and why a polar station sees each satellite on almost every orbit while a target near the equator waits hours. These patterns decide which targets compete for the same satellite, which is what the rest of the course optimises. The chapter ends with the effect the package leaves out, J2, measured in kilometres.

Part 1

Two frames, one Earth

The picture to hold. Newton's law of chapter 01 holds in a frame that does not rotate: its axes point at fixed directions among the distant stars. In that inertial frame a circular orbit is a circle whose plane keeps its direction, and chapter 02 described it with three angles: the inclination i, the right ascension of the ascending node \Omega (RAAN) and the argument of latitude u. The Earth sits at the centre of that circle and spins inside it. A ship does not move in the inertial frame along a fixed point: it is carried round the Earth's axis once a day. So the satellite's plane stays put, the ground slides underneath it, and every new revolution passes over a different strip of the Earth.

Two frames are needed to say this precisely. The inertial frame (ECI, for Earth-centred inertial) has its z axis along the Earth's rotation axis and its x axis toward a fixed direction in the equatorial plane. The Earth-fixed frame (ECEF, Earth-centred Earth-fixed) has the same z axis, but its x axis goes through the Greenwich meridian on the equator and turns with the Earth. Latitude and longitude, the coordinates of the targets, are Earth-fixed.

What is a rotating frame?

In plain words: a set of axes glued to something that turns, here the Earth. A point at rest on the ground has constant coordinates in it, and moves on a circle in the inertial frame.

More precisely: the Earth-fixed frame is the inertial frame rotated about the common z axis by the angle \theta(t), the angle from the inertial x axis to Greenwich. The package's convention is the simplest possible: Greenwich lies on the inertial x axis at t = 0, and the Earth turns at a constant rate,

\theta(t) = \omega_E\, t, \qquad \omega_E = 7.2921159 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{rad/s} = 15.0411^\circ/\text{h}.
Inertial frame (ECI)
Non-rotating; Newton's law holds as written. Orbits and the satellite's position live here (position_eci).
Earth-fixed frame (ECEF)
Turns with the Earth; targets and stations have constant coordinates. Latitude and longitude are angles in this frame.
Sidereal rotation
One turn of the Earth relative to the stars: 2\pi/\omega_E.

From one frame to the other. A rotation by \theta about z is the matrix of chapter 02,

r_{\mathrm{ECI}} = R_z(\theta)\, r_{\mathrm{ECEF}}, \qquad R_z(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},

and the way back is the transpose, r_{\mathrm{ECEF}} = R_z(-\theta)\, r_{\mathrm{ECI}}. A ground point at latitude \varphi and longitude \lambda on a sphere of radius R has Earth-fixed position R(\cos\varphi\cos\lambda,\ \cos\varphi\sin\lambda,\ \sin\varphi). Rotating it by \theta only adds \theta to the longitude, because a rotation about z is a shift of the azimuth angle:

r_{\mathrm{ECI}}(t) = R\,\big(\cos\varphi\cos(\lambda + \omega_E t),\ \cos\varphi\sin(\lambda + \omega_E t),\ \sin\varphi\big).

That is exactly the package's ground_points_eci (satplan/geometry.py), written for many points and many times at once:

OMEGA_EARTH = 7.2921159e-5  # rad / s

def ground_points_eci(lat_deg, lon_deg, t) -> np.ndarray:
    """Inertial positions (T, J, 3) of ground points (J,) at times t (T,)."""
    lat = np.radians(np.asarray(lat_deg, dtype=float))[None, :]
    lon = np.radians(np.asarray(lon_deg, dtype=float))[None, :]
    theta = lon + OMEGA_EARTH * np.asarray(t, dtype=float)[:, None]
    return R_EARTH * np.stack(
        [np.cos(lat) * np.cos(theta), np.cos(lat) * np.sin(theta), np.sin(lat) * np.ones_like(theta)],
        axis=-1,
    )

Read it as the inverse view of the problem. The package never converts the satellite into Earth-fixed coordinates; it leaves the satellite in the inertial frame, where its motion is a clean circle, and moves the targets instead: theta is the target's inertial longitude, its Earth longitude plus the angle the Earth has turned. The shapes [None, :] and [:, None] make a table of T times by J points. Both views give the same geometry, since the angle between two vectors does not depend on the frame they are written in; chapter 04 needs only such angles.

Sidereal day and solar day. 2\pi/\omega_E = 86\,164.09 s, that is 23 h 56 min 4.1 s, is the time for one turn relative to the stars: the sidereal day. The clock's day of 86 400 s, the solar day, is 235.9 s longer, because in one day the Earth also moves about a 365th of its way round the Sun, and must turn a little further, 360/365.2422 = 0.9856^\circ, to bring the Sun back to the same meridian. In one solar day the Earth turns 360.9856°. A model of satellite visibility needs the rotation relative to the stars, because the orbit is fixed relative to the stars.

Does the difference matter? Using 2\pi/86\,400 s instead of \omega_E would misplace every target by 0.2464° of longitude after 6 h, 27.4 km at the equator, about 3 % of the 874 km radius of a satellite's footprint (chapter 04), and the error grows linearly with time. The package uses the sidereal rate; so does every chapter of this course.

Part 2

The point under the satellite

The sub-satellite point is where the line from the Earth's centre to the satellite meets the ground. A map of where the satellite has been is a curve of sub-satellite points: the ground track. To draw it we need the latitude and the Earth-fixed longitude of the satellite's position r = (x, y, z) at time t.

Latitude. The angle between r and the equatorial plane: \sin\varphi = z/\lVert r\rVert. The rotation about z does not touch z, so the latitude is the same in both frames.

Longitude. The inertial azimuth of r, \operatorname{atan2}(y, x), minus the angle the Earth has turned, brought back into (-180^\circ, 180^\circ]:

\varphi = \arcsin\frac{z}{\lVert r\rVert}, \qquad \lambda = \operatorname{wrap}\big(\operatorname{atan2}(y, x) - \omega_E t\big).

The package has no function for this, because the planner never needs a map; its test of the maximum latitude computes \varphi inline, and the course's port interactive/sat.js adds the longitude for the figures:

function subPoint(r, t) {
  const lat = Math.asin(r[2] / norm(r)) * DEG;
  let lon = (Math.atan2(r[1], r[0]) - OMEGA_EARTH * t) * DEG;
  lon = ((lon + 180) % 360 + 360) % 360 - 180;
  return { lat, lon };
}

The double modulo is there because JavaScript's % keeps the sign of its left operand: -200 \bmod 360 is -200 in JavaScript, and adding 360 before the second modulo makes it 160.

Which latitude? On a sphere, the angle at the centre (the geocentric latitude, above) and the angle of the local vertical (the geodetic latitude, the one on a map or a GPS receiver) are the same thing. On the real, slightly flattened Earth they differ by up to about 0.19° near 45° of latitude, roughly 21 km on the ground. The package uses a sphere of mean radius 6371 km, so its latitudes are geocentric and its targets' latitudes are read the same way; for a synthetic scenario this is consistent, and it is one of the simplifications chapter 05 lists.

The highest latitude. Chapter 02's position_eci gives z = a \sin u \sin i, so \sin\varphi = \sin u \sin i. Its largest value, at u = 90^\circ, is \sin i, and

\varphi_{\max} = \arcsin(\sin i) = \begin{cases} i, & i \le 90^\circ, \\ 180^\circ - i, & i > 90^\circ. \end{cases}

At i = 97.6^\circ the track turns back at \varphi_{\max} = 82.4^\circ. The orbit is retrograde: an inclination above 90° means the satellite's eastward velocity component points west, against the Earth's rotation, by \cos 97.6^\circ = -0.13 of its speed. The package checks this formula with a test (tests/test_planning.py):

def test_max_latitude_equals_inclination_supplement():
    orb = CircularOrbit(550e3, 97.6, 30.0, 0.0)
    p = orb.position_eci(np.linspace(0, orb.period_s, 4000))
    lat = np.degrees(np.arcsin(p[:, 2] / np.linalg.norm(p, axis=1)))
    assert lat.max() == pytest.approx(180 - 97.6, abs=0.1)

The package's number. On 4000 samples of one revolution the largest latitude is 82.4000°, equal to 180^\circ - 97.6^\circ to four decimals; the test allows 0.1°. The latitude does not depend on the Earth's rotation, so the test can stay in the inertial frame.

Part 3

Ground tracks and the westward shift

The map. The hero demo draws the ground track on an equirectangular map: longitude along the horizontal axis and latitude along the vertical, both linear, so a degree is the same length everywhere. It is the simplest map and it distorts: a degree of longitude is 111 km on the equator and 111 \cos\varphi km at latitude \varphi, yet it is drawn the same width, so everything near the poles is stretched sideways. The grey lines, every 30°, are the graticule. The shaded boxes are the ten generic maritime areas of satplan/scenario.py, the triangles its four ground stations; no coastline is drawn, because the scenario has none.

One revolution. At 550 km the period is T = 5730.127 s, 95.50 min (chapter 01). Over one revolution the track goes from the equator up to 82.4° N, down across the equator to 82.4° S, and back. Seen from the ground it is a wave, not a circle, because the ground moves under it at up to 465 m/s at the equator, while the sub-satellite point moves at 6986 m/s.

The shift. When the satellite returns to its ascending node after one period, the orbit plane has not moved, but the Earth has turned by \omega_E T. The node is now over a meridian that many degrees further west:

\Delta\lambda = \omega_E T = 7.2921159 \times 10^{-5} \times 5730.127 = 0.41785\ \text{rad} = 23.94^\circ,

or R\,\Delta\lambda = 2662 km along the equator. The numbered circles in the hero map are these crossings; sampling the track every second and finding where its latitude changes sign gives successive nodes at -23.94^\circ and -47.88^\circ, as the formula says. The shift does not depend on the inclination at all, only on the period: try the inclination slider and watch the readout stay at 23.94°; then the altitude slider, which changes T.

The satellite makes 15.078 revolutions per solar day, and 360/23.94 = 15.04 shifts fill the full circle of longitude. The two numbers are close but not equal, so after a day the tracks do not repeat: they land a little to the side of the first day's. Orbits whose numbers match exactly (a repeat ground track) exist, but the scenario does not use one, and over the 6 to 12 hours of a planning horizon it would make no difference.

The track's tilt. On the ascending node the satellite climbs northward at 97.6° from east, that is 7.60° west of north in the inertial frame. The ground moving eastward under it adds an apparent westward drift, and the track crosses the equator 11.34° west of north on the map. Both angles are small: the track is nearly a meridian, which is why polar orbits are said to "scan" the Earth in north–south strips.

The orbit is fixed among the stars; the Earth turns inside it. Every pattern on the map, the westward shift, the strips, the revisit, comes from one product: the Earth's rotation rate times the time spent.

Part 4

Revisit

A target can only be listened to when a satellite sees it: chapter 04 turns this into an exact test, with a half-width of the footprint of 7.86° of Earth central angle, 874 km on the ground, for the targets' limits. So a track serves a strip about 1747 km wide. The revisit of a point is how often some strip covers it.

At the equator, rarely. Neighbouring tracks of one satellite are 2662 km apart at the equator, more than the 1747 km strip: there are gaps between the strips of consecutive revolutions, and they are only filled over several days as the tracks drift. A point on the equator sees a given satellite once or twice a day, sometimes not at all.

Near the poles, every orbit. The spacing of the tracks along a parallel shrinks like 2662 \cos\varphi km, because the meridians converge, while the strip keeps its width. The two are equal near \varphi = 49^\circ; beyond that the strips of consecutive revolutions overlap, and above 75° nearly every revolution passes within reach. A polar ground station is placed there for this reason: in the small instance, GS-Arctic at 78.2° N has 12 contacts in 6 h with the four satellites: two or four per satellite in 3.77 revolutions, so satellites 1 and 3 see it on every revolution. That is against 5 for GS-Europe at 48.1° N and 3 each for GS-South and GS-Indian. (Contacts use a wider limit than targets, a 5° elevation mask and no sensor cone, chapter 04.)

Passes per day over a point, against its latitude (one satellite, 550 km, 24 h)

At 97.6° the chart climbs from 1.58 passes per day at the equator (between 0 and 3, depending on the longitude) to 2.00 at 40° and 7.75 from 75° up: the pole is covered on about half of the 15 revolutions, because only the revolutions whose turning point is on the point's side of the pole come close enough. The low-latitude bars wobble, from 1.17 to 1.58, because one day and twelve longitudes are a small sample of a pattern that repeats only after many days. Set the inclination to 53° and the picture flips: points from 65° up are never seen (the track turns at 53°, and the footprint reaches 7.86° beyond), and the passes pile up at 45–50°, near the turning points: 4.50 a day at 45°.

Every area gets one to three passes in the six hours (three for the Gulf of Guinea, the Bay of Biscay approaches and the North Sea; one for the South China Sea), and the gaps come in two sizes only: eight gaps of 42 to 44 minutes and four of 4.3 to 5.1 hours. Both sizes come from the constellation layout of chapter 02, two planes 90° of RAAN apart with two satellites in each, half a revolution apart.

  • About 43 minutes. While the Earth carries an area through one orbit plane, the area stays within reach of that plane for an hour or so. The two satellites of the plane follow each other at half a period, 47.75 min, so the area sees one, then the other, with about 43 minutes between the end of one pass and the start of the next. In the North Sea, at 56° N, the strips overlap and satellite 3 comes back on its next revolution as well: three passes.
  • About five hours. The next plane is 90° of RAAN away, and the Earth needs 90/15.04 \approx 6 h to turn that far. Six hours minus the length of a burst gives the long gaps.

Why the planner cares. Opportunities on the same satellite that fall in the same pass compete for the same few minutes: they are the conflict pairs of chapter 06. The bursts above are where the planning problem is hard: in the Gulf of Guinea, the third pass carries 15 opportunities in 4.3 minutes, for one satellite that must slew between them. The long gaps are where it is easy, and where a target that misses its pass waits five hours.

Part 5

What the model leaves out

The package's docstring is explicit: "spherical Earth, circular two-body orbits (no J2 drift)". The Earth is not a sphere. It bulges at the equator: its equatorial radius is 6378.137 km, about 21 km more than its polar radius. The extra mass in the bulge pulls an inclined orbit's plane toward the equator, and like a spinning top under gravity, the plane does not fall but precesses: the ascending node slides slowly round the equator. The size of the bulge's effect on gravity is the coefficient J_2 = 1.08263 \times 10^{-3}, the largest correction to a spherical Earth's field.

Nodal regression. For a circular orbit of radius a, mean motion n = 2\pi/T and inclination i, the node moves at the average rate [2]

\dot\Omega = -\frac{3}{2}\, J_2 \left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{eq}}}{a}\right)^2 n \cos i .

For a prograde orbit (\cos i > 0) the node moves west, the classic "regression"; for a retrograde one it moves east; for an exactly polar orbit it does not move. With the package's a = 6921 km (550 km above the mean radius), n = 1.096518 \times 10^{-3} rad/s and i = 97.6^\circ:

\dot\Omega = 2.0001 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{rad/s} = 0.9901^\circ/\text{day, eastward}.

Sun-synchronous. The Sun's apparent direction moves east along the ecliptic by 360/365.2422 = 0.9856^\circ a day, the same 0.9856° that separates the solar and sidereal days in Part 1. An orbit whose node moves at that rate keeps the same angle to the Sun all year, so it crosses each latitude at the same local solar time every day: a sun-synchronous orbit, prized for constant lighting and for predictable power on solar panels. Solving \dot\Omega = 0.9856^\circ/day for i gives 97.57° at a = 6921 km (97.59° if the 550 km are counted above the equatorial radius). The scenario's 97.6° is the sun-synchronous inclination for its altitude, which is why scenario.py comments it "sun-synchronous at 550 km". The package uses the inclination for that reason and then leaves out the drift that gives it the name. The hero demo's J2 readout shows how quickly this changes: at 53° the node would move 4.505° a day westward, at 0° 7.486°.

Does leaving it out matter? Over the planning horizons of the benchmark:

At the end of a 12-hour plan, a track the model puts over a target would in reality pass 55 km to the east of it. That changes a window's length by a few seconds and its timing barely, and it shifts every window of a satellite in the same direction; the conflicts between targets, which are what the planner optimises, are almost unchanged. Over a year the drift adds up to 361.6°, a full turn: it is essential for mission design, and negligible for a 6–12 h plan.

A second J2 effect, larger over a plan. J2 also changes how fast the satellite moves along its orbit. For a circular orbit the argument of latitude advances at n\,\big(1 + \tfrac{3}{4} J_2 (R_{\mathrm{eq}}/a)^2 (8\cos^2 i - 2)\big), the sum of the first-order perigee and mean-anomaly rates of [2]. At 97.6° the bracket is 1 - 1.283 \times 10^{-3}: for the same mean orbit radius, a real satellite would fall behind the Kepler model by 1.74° of its orbit after 6 h, 210 km along the track, or 27.7 s of timing (55.4 s after 12 h). That is comparable to the shortest tasks (30 s). It is also the easiest thing to fix: an operator plans with the measured period of the orbit, not with Kepler's law, and the package could do the same by using a period of 5737.49 s instead of 5730.13 s.

The judgement. For a synthetic study of planning algorithms the simplification is sound: it moves windows by tens of kilometres and tens of seconds, nearly uniformly per satellite, while the planning problem is decided by the minutes between neighbouring windows. For planning a real satellite it would not be: real planners use an orbit propagated with J2 and higher terms, updated from tracking data, and real scenarios would need geodetic latitudes too.

Part 6

Check your understanding

1. Why does the package move the targets into the inertial frame instead of moving the satellite into the Earth-fixed frame?

Because the satellite's motion is simplest in the inertial frame (a circle of fixed plane), and the visibility test only needs angles between vectors, which are the same in either frame. Rotating a ground point is one added angle, \lambda + \omega_E t, which is all ground_points_eci does.

2. Why is \omega_E based on 23 h 56 min and not on 24 h?

The orbit is fixed relative to the stars, so what matters is the Earth's rotation relative to the stars: one sidereal day, 86 164.09 s. The 24-hour solar day includes the extra 0.9856° the Earth turns to catch up with the Sun's apparent motion. Using it would misplace targets by 27.4 km at the equator after 6 h.

3. A satellite crosses the equator northward at longitude 10° E. Where does it cross next time, and where on the following revolution?

One period later the Earth has turned \omega_E T = 23.94^\circ, so the next ascending crossing is at 10 - 23.94 = -13.94^\circ, that is 13.94° W, and the one after at 37.88° W.

4. What is the highest latitude of the ground track at i = 53^\circ? At i = 97.6^\circ?

\arcsin(\sin i): 53° for the first, and 180 - 97.6 = 82.4^\circ for the second. A point at 85° is still seen from the second orbit if it is within the footprint, 7.86° of central angle, of the track's turning point.

5. Does the westward shift per revolution depend on the inclination?

No. It is \omega_E T, and the period depends only on the orbit radius. The inclination changes the shape of the track and its tilt at the equator, not the spacing of its nodes.

6. Why is a polar ground station contacted on nearly every revolution while an equatorial target can wait a whole day?

Every revolution of a near-polar orbit passes within about 7.6° of each pole, and a footprint of 7.86° of central angle reaches the station on most of them. At the equator successive tracks are 2662 km apart and the footprint strip is 1747 km wide, so a point falls between strips on many revolutions. GS-Arctic has 12 contacts in 6 h with four satellites; an equatorial point sees one satellite 1.58 times a day on average.

7. In the small instance, gaps between passes over an area are either about 43 minutes or about 5 hours. Where does each come from?

43 minutes: the two satellites of one plane are half a period (47.75 min) apart, and both reach the area while the Earth carries it through that plane. Five hours: the other plane is 90° of RAAN away, and the Earth needs about 6 h to turn 90°.

8. Why can the package call its orbit "sun-synchronous" if it has no J2?

Sun-synchronism is a property of the inclination and altitude under J2: at 550 km, 97.6° makes the node drift 0.9901°/day, the Sun's 0.9856°/day. The package keeps the inclination that a real sun-synchronous orbit would have, so its tracks look like a real one's, but does not move the node. Over 6 h the missing drift is 0.248°, 27.5 km at the equator.

    code/ch03_numbers.py does exactly this and prints -23.94^\circ. Split the plotted line where abs(np.diff(lon)) > 180 to avoid a horizontal line across the map at the date line.

  2. A repeat ground track. Find the altitude at which a circular orbit makes exactly 15 revolutions per sidereal day, so that its track repeats every day relative to the stars-and-Earth geometry (ignoring J2). How far is it from 550 km?
    Hint

    Fifteen revolutions in a sidereal day means T = 86\,164.09/15 s. Invert Kepler's third law, a = (\mu T^2 / 4\pi^2)^{1/3}.

    Solution

    T = 5744.27 s, a = (3.986 \times 10^{14} \times 5744.27^2/4\pi^2)^{1/3} \approx 6932 km, about 561 km above the mean radius: about 11 km higher than the scenario's orbit. Then \omega_E T = 24^\circ exactly and fifteen shifts close the circle. (Strictly, a track repeats when the Earth has turned a whole number of times relative to the orbit plane; with J2 the plane moves too, and real repeat orbits are computed with the nodal period and the node drift.)

  3. Sun-synchronous inclination against altitude. Using the nodal regression formula, compute the sun-synchronous inclination for altitudes from 300 to 1200 km. Is it always retrograde?
    Hint

    Solve \cos i = -\dot\Omega_\odot / \big(\tfrac{3}{2} J_2 (R_{\mathrm{eq}}/a)^2 n\big) with \dot\Omega_\odot = 0.9856^\circ/day converted to rad/s.

    Solution

    The right-hand side is negative, so \cos i < 0: always retrograde. With 550 km counted above the mean radius as in the package, the required inclination grows with altitude: 96.65° at 300 km, 97.57° at 550 km, 98.57° at 800 km, 100.38° at 1200 km, because the J2 effect weakens like a^{-7/2} and a larger |\cos i| has to compensate. Above about 5990 km of altitude even i = 180^\circ is not enough. (code/ch03_numbers.py prints these.)

  4. Add J2 to the package, in a copy. Make a subclass of CircularOrbit whose position_eci uses \Omega(t) = \Omega_0 + \dot\Omega t, rebuild the small instance with it, and compare the opportunities: how many windows appear, disappear or move by more than 10 s?
    Hint

    CircularOrbit is a frozen dataclass; override position_eci and compute the RAAN as an array over t. build_instance calls make_constellation, so monkeypatch that function in your script, not in the package.

    Solution

    code/ch03_numbers.py does this for the windows. With the node drift, the small instance has 169 opportunities instead of 168: one disappears and two appear (windows close to their task's duration that cross the threshold), 125 keep exactly the same edges on the 10 s grid, and no edge moves by more than 10 s, one grid step. The drift grows linearly from 0 to 27.5 km over the 6 h, so the later windows are the ones that move. Re-solving with chapter 08's CP-SAT tells you whether the optimum moves. If you also add the along-track rate of Part 5, window times move by up to about 28 s, and many more edges change.

Part 8

The chapter on one page

  1. Two frames: orbits live in the inertial frame, targets in the Earth-fixed frame; one rotates relative to the other by \theta = \omega_E t, with Greenwich on the inertial x axis at t = 0.
  2. Sidereal rate: \omega_E = 7.2921159 \times 10^{-5} rad/s, one turn in 86 164.09 s (23 h 56 min 4.1 s), 235.9 s shorter than the solar day.
  3. The package's view: ground_points_eci moves the targets into the inertial frame by adding \omega_E t to their longitude.
  4. Sub-satellite point: \varphi = \arcsin(z/r) (geocentric, on a sphere), \lambda = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x) - \omega_E t, wrapped.
  5. The track: highest latitude 180^\circ - i = 82.4^\circ (tested in the package); shift per revolution \omega_E T = 23.94^\circ, 2662 km at the equator; 15.078 revolutions per day.
  6. Revisit: a 1747 km strip against 2662 km between tracks at the equator (1.58 passes a day per satellite), overlapping beyond about 49°; 12 contacts in 6 h at the Arctic station, every revolution for two of the satellites; the maritime areas see bursts 42–44 min apart and gaps of 4.3–5.1 h.
  7. Left out: J2 turns the node by 0.9901°/day at 97.6° (the Sun's 0.9856°/day: sun-synchronous), 27.5 km at the equator over 6 h and 55.0 km over 12 h; the along-track effect is 27.7 s over 6 h at fixed mean radius, and is removed by using the measured period.

Part 9

Scope and further reading

Scope. This chapter covers only what the package models: a uniform rotation about a fixed axis, a spherical Earth and circular orbits, plus J2's two first-order secular effects as a measured omission. It leaves out the slow motions of the Earth's axis (precession, nutation, polar motion), the difference between UT1 and the clock time, the ellipsoid and geodetic coordinates, the higher terms of the gravity field, air drag (which slowly lowers an orbit at 550 km), and the orbit determination that real operators use to keep their predictions honest. Everything in the scenario is synthetic; nothing here describes a real satellite or operator. Chapter 04 now turns the sub-satellite geometry into the exact visibility test, and chapter 05 turns visibility windows into a planning problem.

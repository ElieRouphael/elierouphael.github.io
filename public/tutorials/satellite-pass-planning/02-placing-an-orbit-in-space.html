Placing an orbit in space

Chapter 01 flew a satellite on a flat circle. A real constellation needs that circle tilted and turned in space, a different way for each satellite, so that together they cover the oceans the planner cares about. This chapter introduces the frame in which orbits are described, the three angles that place a circular orbit in it, and the rotation matrices that turn those angles into coordinates. It then derives, term by term, the three lines of the course's package that compute every satellite position, and shows how the package lays out its constellations.

Part 1

A frame that does not turn

A position is three numbers, and three numbers mean nothing until we say which origin and which axes they are measured from: a reference frame. Chapter 01 used one without naming it. The origin was the Earth's centre, and the axes were fixed while the satellite went round. That second property matters. Newton's law m\ddot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf F holds in an inertial frame, one that neither accelerates nor rotates. In a rotating frame, such as one glued to the turning Earth, the same motion picks up extra terms (centrifugal and Coriolis accelerations) that have nothing to do with gravity. Orbits are therefore described in a frame whose axes point at fixed directions among the stars.

What is the ECI frame?

In plain words: Earth-Centred Inertial. Origin at the Earth's centre; axes that do not turn with the Earth. The z axis points along the Earth's rotation axis, to the north; x and y lie in the equatorial plane.

More precisely: the Earth's centre does accelerate, around the Sun, but the Sun pulls on the Earth and on a nearby satellite almost equally, so for a satellite's motion relative to the Earth the frame is inertial to a very good approximation. Standard ECI frames point x at the vernal equinox, the direction from the Earth to the Sun at the March equinox [2]. The package simplifies: its x axis is wherever the Greenwich meridian points at t = 0, and the Earth turns about z from there (chapter 03). Both the RAANs of the orbits and the Earth's orientation at t = 0 are measured from this same x axis, so the model is self-consistent; it simply does not tie t = 0 to a real date.

Equatorial plane
The xy plane: z = 0.
Right ascension
An angle in the equatorial plane measured from x towards y; the inertial cousin of longitude.
Latitude
The angle above the equatorial plane, \arcsin(z/|\mathbf r|) for a spherical Earth.

In the hero demo the three arrows are the ECI axes, and the grid on the sphere is the Earth's latitude and longitude lines as they are at t = 0. The orbit is a fixed circle in this frame: the satellite goes round it and, in chapter 03, the Earth turns beneath it. No coastline is drawn because the package has none; its Earth is a sphere with targets and ground stations as points.

Part 2

Three angles for a circle

A circular orbit around the Earth's centre is fixed by its radius, which chapter 01 set to a = 6{,}921.0 km, and by the plane it lies in. Its plane passes through the Earth's centre because gravity pulls towards the centre and never out of the plane. A plane through the origin needs two angles. Where the satellite is on the circle needs a third.

The line of nodes. Unless the orbit lies in the equatorial plane, it crosses that plane at exactly two points, the nodes. At the ascending node the satellite crosses going north (z increasing); at the descending node, half a revolution later, going south. The line through the two nodes, which passes through the Earth's centre, is the line of nodes. It is the hinge about which the orbit plane is tilted relative to the equator.

The angles that place a circular orbit

In plain words: start with a circle in the equatorial plane. Tilt it about a hinge in the equatorial plane by the inclination. Turn the hinge about the polar axis to the right ascension of the ascending node. Then say how far along the circle, from the ascending node, the satellite is: its argument of latitude.

More precisely [1]:

Inclination i
The angle between the orbit plane and the equatorial plane, between 0° and 180°: equivalently, between the orbit's angular-momentum vector \mathbf r \times \mathbf v and the z axis.
RAAN \Omega
The right ascension of the ascending node: the angle from the x axis to the ascending node, in the equatorial plane, between 0° and 360°.
Argument of latitude u
The angle from the ascending node to the satellite, in the orbit plane and in the direction of motion. On an ellipse it is split into the argument of perigee plus the true anomaly; a circle has no perigee, so u alone is used.

In the package, inclination_deg and raan_deg are fields of CircularOrbit, and phase_deg is the argument of latitude at t = 0. After that, as chapter 01 showed, u grows at the constant rate 2\pi/T:

        u = np.radians(self.phase_deg) + 2.0 * np.pi * t / self.period_s

Prograde, polar, retrograde

An inclination below 90° gives a prograde orbit. Seen from above the North Pole the satellite goes anticlockwise, like the Earth turns, and at the ascending node it heads north-east. At exactly 90° the orbit is polar and passes over both poles. Above 90° it is retrograde: the satellite goes round the other way and crosses the equator heading north-west, against the Earth's rotation.

The course's 97.6°. Every satellite of the package has i = 97.6°: a retrograde orbit, 7.6° past polar. The orbit normal's z component is \cos i = -0.1323; at the ascending node the velocity has an eastward component of -0.1323 (per unit speed): it heads mostly north and a little west. The highest latitude the satellite reaches is 180° - i = 82.4°, so it passes close to both poles but not over them. Why this odd number? At 550 km it is the inclination at which the Earth's equatorial bulge turns the orbit plane at the same rate as the Earth goes around the Sun, the "sun-synchronous" condition, which chapter 03 explains. The package keeps the inclination but ignores the bulge.

Set the hero to step 3, try i = 30°, 90° and 150°, and watch where the ascending node is and which way the satellite's arrow points. Use "along the nodes" to look straight down the hinge: the orbit becomes a line tilted by exactly i from the equator.

Part 3

Rotation matrices

"Tilt about the hinge, then turn the hinge" is an instruction for a person. A computer needs it as arithmetic on coordinates, and rotations about the coordinate axes are linear maps, so they are matrices.

Rotation about z. Turn a point (x, y, z) about the z axis by an angle \theta, anticlockwise when seen from the tip of z. Its height z does not change. In the xy plane, write the point as (\rho\cos\phi, \rho\sin\phi); after the turn it is (\rho\cos(\phi + \theta), \rho\sin(\phi + \theta)). Expanding with the angle-sum formulas, \rho\cos(\phi + \theta) = x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta and \rho\sin(\phi + \theta) = x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta. In matrix form:

R_z(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad R_x(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}.

Rotation about x is the same construction with the roles moved along: x is unchanged, and (y, z) turn as (x, y) did, anticlockwise when seen from the tip of x. Under R_x(\theta) the y axis tips up towards z. Each matrix's columns are the images of the three axes, and they stay unit length and mutually perpendicular. Such matrices are orthogonal, R^\top R = I, so undoing a rotation is just transposing it: R_z(\theta)^{-1} = R_z(\theta)^\top = R_z(-\theta).

What is a rotation matrix?

In plain words: a 3×3 table of cosines and sines that turns any vector about an axis by an angle, without stretching it.

More precisely: an orthogonal matrix with determinant +1. Applying two rotations one after the other is multiplying their matrices: B(A\mathbf p) = (BA)\mathbf p, where the rightmost matrix acts first.

The order matters

Numbers commute, 3 \times 5 = 5 \times 3; matrices in general do not, and rotations about different axes almost never do (the exceptions are special cases, such as an angle that is a multiple of 360°, or two half-turns about perpendicular axes). The cleanest example is 90° each, applied to the unit vector along x:

R_z(90°)\,R_x(90°)\,\mathbf e_x = (0, 1, 0), \qquad R_x(90°)\,R_z(90°)\,\mathbf e_x = (0, 0, 1).

On the left, R_x acts first and leaves \mathbf e_x alone, then R_z turns it to y. On the right, R_z turns it to y first, and then R_x tips y up to z. Same two rotations, different result. With the course's angles, i = 97.6° and \Omega = 40°, the two products are

R_z(\Omega)R_x(i) = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7660 & 0.0850 & 0.6371 \\ 0.6428 & -0.1013 & -0.7593 \\ 0 & 0.9912 & -0.1323 \end{pmatrix}, \quad R_x(i)R_z(\Omega) = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7660 & -0.6428 & 0 \\ -0.0850 & -0.1013 & -0.9912 \\ 0.6371 & 0.7593 & -0.1323 \end{pmatrix}.

Their entries differ by up to 0.7278. Press "wrong order" in the hero: at u = 60° the wrong product puts the satellite at (−1,202, −901, 6,756) km, 5,103 km from the right place. The reason is visible in the 3D view. Turning a flat circle about z first does not move the circle at all; it only slides the satellite along it by \Omega. The tilt then happens about the x axis, so the wrong orbit's ascending node stays on x whatever \Omega says.

Part 4

Deriving position_eci

Now build the orbit as the hero demo does, one step at a time, and keep track of where each step leaves the line of nodes.

Step 1: the circle in its own plane. Use coordinates in which the orbit is the unit circle of the xy plane scaled by a, with the ascending node on the x axis. The satellite at argument of latitude u is at

\mathbf p_1 = (a\cos u,\; a\sin u,\; 0)^\top .

Step 2: tilt by i about x. The node is on x, so x is the hinge. R_x(i) leaves it fixed and tips the rest of the circle out of the equatorial plane. The satellite moves through u = 90° at its highest point, and at u slightly positive it is just above the equator, so the node on x is indeed the ascending one:

\mathbf p_2 = R_x(i)\,\mathbf p_1 = (a\cos u,\; a\sin u\cos i,\; a\sin u\sin i)^\top .

Step 3: turn by \Omega about z. R_z(\Omega) carries the ascending node from the x axis to right ascension \Omega, and the tilted circle with it. The tilt is preserved because R_z does not change heights or the angle with the equatorial plane:

\mathbf r = R_z(\Omega)\,R_x(i)\,(a\cos u, a\sin u, 0)^\top = a\begin{pmatrix} \cos\Omega\cos u - \sin\Omega\,\sin u\cos i \\ \sin\Omega\cos u + \cos\Omega\,\sin u\cos i \\ \sin u\sin i \end{pmatrix}.

Each component is the first row (second, third) of R_z(\Omega) times \mathbf p_2: for x, \cos\Omega \cdot a\cos u - \sin\Omega \cdot a\sin u\cos i + 0; for y, \sin\Omega \cdot a\cos u + \cos\Omega \cdot a\sin u\cos i; and z is unchanged from step 2. Here is the package's code once more:

        cu, su = np.cos(u), np.sin(u)
        x = a * (np.cos(raan) * cu - np.sin(raan) * su * np.cos(inc))
        y = a * (np.sin(raan) * cu + np.cos(raan) * su * np.cos(inc))
        z = a * (su * np.sin(inc))
        return np.stack([x, y, z], axis=-1)

Term for term, it is the vector above: cu and su are \cos u and \sin u, raan is \Omega, inc is i. The code never builds a matrix; it is the product written out by hand, which is faster and works on whole arrays of times at once. The comment in the JavaScript port, sat.js, says the same in words: rotate the in-plane point by the inclination about x, then by the RAAN about z.

Checked. code/ch02_numbers.py builds the two matrices, multiplies them onto (a\cos u, a\sin u, 0), and compares with CircularOrbit.position_eci for 10,000 random inclinations, RAANs, phases and times: the largest coordinate difference is 1.86 \times 10^{-9} m, rounding. At the hero's default, i = 97.6°, \Omega = 40°, u = 60°, the three steps give (3,460.5, 5,993.8, 0.0) km, then (3,460.5, −792.7, 5,941.1) km, then (3,160.4, 1,617.1, 5,941.1) km, which is what position_eci returns. The satellite is at latitude 59.14°.

Reading the product right to left. R_z(\Omega)R_x(i)\mathbf p_1 applies R_x(i) first. The rule that makes it right is that each rotation must be about the axis where its hinge is at that moment. The inclination's hinge, the line of nodes, starts on x, so the tilt is about x; only afterwards is the node turned to \Omega. The same matrix also gives the orbit's normal, the image of the in-plane z axis: its third column, (\sin\Omega\sin i, -\cos\Omega\sin i, \cos i). Its z component \cos i is negative for a retrograde orbit.

A circular orbit is a flat circle moved into place by two rotations, and the package's three lines of trigonometry are those rotations multiplied out. The order is part of the definition: first tilt about the line of nodes while it lies on x, then turn the node to its right ascension.

Part 5

A constellation

One satellite sees a strip of the Earth at a time. To revisit the oceans often, the package flies several, all at 550 km and 97.6°, in different planes and at different points along them. This is the whole layout, make_constellation in satplan/scenario.py:

def make_constellation(cfg: ScenarioConfig) -> list[CircularOrbit]:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(cfg.seed + 1000)
    n_planes = max(1, int(round(np.sqrt(cfg.n_sats))))
    orbits = []
    for k in range(cfg.n_sats):
        plane = k % n_planes
        slot = k // n_planes
        per_plane = int(np.ceil(cfg.n_sats / n_planes))
        raan = 180.0 * plane / n_planes + rng.uniform(-5, 5)
        phase = 360.0 * slot / per_plane + 360.0 * plane / (n_planes * per_plane) + rng.uniform(-3, 3)
        orbits.append(CircularOrbit(cfg.altitude_km * 1e3, cfg.inclination_deg, raan, phase))
    return orbits

Read it in four pieces.

  • How many planes. \mathrm{round}(\sqrt n), so planes and satellites per plane are roughly balanced: 2 planes for the small instance's 4 satellites, 3 for the medium's 8 (\sqrt 8 = 2.828) and 3 for the large instance's 12 (\sqrt{12} = 3.464). Satellite k goes to plane k \bmod P, slot \lfloor k/P \rfloor, with \lceil n/P \rceil slots per plane: 2, 3 and 4. When P does not divide n the last plane is short: the medium constellation has 3 + 3 + 2 satellites.
  • RAAN over 180°. Plane p has its ascending node at 180° \cdot p/P: 0° and 90° for the small layout, 0°, 60° and 120° for the medium and large ones. Why not 360° \cdot p/P? See below.
  • Phasing. Within a plane the slots are 360°/\lceil n/P \rceil apart: 180° (small), 120° (medium), 90° (large). Each plane is also shifted by 360°/(P\lceil n/P \rceil), 90°, 40° and 30°, so that the satellites of neighbouring planes are staggered along their orbits instead of side by side: in the small layout the four satellites start at about 0°, 90°, 180° and 270°. In time, two satellites 120° apart in one medium plane pass the same point of their orbit 1,910 s (31.8 min) apart; 180° apart in a small plane, 2,865 s (47.8 min).
  • Jitter. A random generator seeded with seed + 1000, so that it is independent of the targets' generator, adds up to ±5° to each RAAN and ±3° to each phase. The code does not say why; the effect is a layout that is less regular than an ideal one, as a real constellation never is, with fewer exact ties between the passes of different satellites. One consequence: the satellites of a "plane" are in slightly different planes, a few degrees apart. Another: the draws come in the same order for every size, so satellite k gets the same jitter in the small, medium and large layouts.

Why the RAAN is spread over 180°, not 360°

A near-polar orbit passes every latitude below its highest twice per revolution: once going north, on its ascending half, and once going south, on its descending half, half a world away. Now compare a plane with node \Omega and one with node \Omega + 180°. For a polar orbit, i = 90°, they are the same plane: the second one's ascending half is the first one's descending half. For i = 97.6° they are nearly the same. Their normals, from Part 4, are (\sin\Omega\sin i, -\cos\Omega\sin i, \cos i) and (-\sin\Omega\sin i, \cos\Omega\sin i, \cos i), whose angle works out to 180° - 2(i - 90°). The two planes are only 2(i - 90°) = 15.2° apart, flown in opposite directions.

So spreading near-polar planes over 360° wastes them. With two planes the effect is dramatic: nodes at 0° and 180° give two planes 15.2° apart (the same with the small layout's jitter), where 0° and 90° give planes that are 89.0° apart, or 86.6° at least with the jitter. With three planes, 0°, 120° and 240° happen to make nearly the same set of planes, as sets of points, as 0°, 60° and 120°: for the medium layout the smallest angle between planes is 57.1° against 55.8° as built. But the plane at 240° is then nearly the plane at 60° flown backwards, so neighbouring planes fly in opposite directions; spreading over 180° keeps every plane flying the same way. Spreading over 180° puts all the ascending halves side by side on one side of the Earth and all the descending halves on the other. Toggle "spread over 360°" in the widget below to see both cases.

Part 6

Check your understanding

1. Why are orbits described in an inertial frame rather than one fixed to the Earth?

Newton's second law in the simple form m\ddot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf F holds only in a frame that does not accelerate or rotate. In a frame turning with the Earth, the same motion acquires centrifugal and Coriolis terms. The orbit is a fixed circle in ECI; in the Earth-fixed frame its ground track keeps shifting.

2. What do the inclination, the RAAN and the argument of latitude each fix?

The inclination is the tilt of the orbit plane relative to the equator. The RAAN is the direction, in the equatorial plane, of the ascending node: where the hinge of the tilt points. The argument of latitude is where the satellite is on the circle, measured from the ascending node in the direction of motion.

3. Is an orbit with i = 97.6° prograde or retrograde, and what is its highest latitude?

Retrograde, because i > 90°: it goes round against the Earth's rotation and crosses the equator heading slightly west. Its highest latitude is 180° - 97.6° = 82.4°.

4. Why is R_x used for the inclination and not R_y?

Because the in-plane coordinates put the ascending node on the x axis (u = 0 gives (a, 0, 0)), and the tilt is about the line of nodes. With the node on y the formula would use R_y and a different phase convention.

5. What goes wrong with R_x(i)R_z(\Omega) instead of R_z(\Omega)R_x(i)?

The rightmost matrix acts first. Turning the flat circle about z first only slides the satellite along it by \Omega; the tilt then happens about the x axis, so the node never reaches \Omega. At the default angles the satellite ends up 5,103 km from the right place.

6. In position_eci, where does the term -\sin\Omega\,\sin u\cos i in x come from?

After the tilt, the satellite's y coordinate is a\sin u\cos i. The first row of R_z(\Omega), (\cos\Omega, -\sin\Omega, 0), multiplies it by -\sin\Omega. The other term, \cos\Omega\cos u, is the same row acting on the unchanged x = a\cos u.

7. Why does make_constellation spread the RAANs over 180° and not 360°?

For a near-polar orbit, the plane with node \Omega + 180° is nearly the plane with node \Omega, flown in the other direction: only 2(i - 90°) = 15.2° apart at 97.6°. Over 360°, two planes would nearly coincide; over 180° they are 89.0° apart (86.6° with the jitter). With three planes the set of planes is about the same either way, but over 360° neighbouring planes would fly in opposite directions.

8. The medium constellation has 8 satellites. How are they arranged, and why is one plane short?

\mathrm{round}(\sqrt 8) = 3 planes at RAAN 0°, 60°, 120° (±5°), with \lceil 8/3 \rceil = 3 slots per plane, 120° apart, and a 40° shift between planes (±3°). Satellites go round-robin to the planes, k \bmod 3, so planes 0 and 1 get three satellites and plane 2 gets two.

Part 7

Exercises

  1. The velocity. Differentiate position_eci with respect to time and write the satellite's inertial velocity as R_z(\Omega)R_x(i) times an in-plane vector. Check that its length is the circular speed of chapter 01, 7589.0 m/s.
    Hint

    Only u depends on time, with \dot u = 2\pi/T = n. The rotation matrices are constant.

    Solution

    \dot{\mathbf r} = R_z(\Omega)R_x(i)\,(-a n\sin u,\; a n\cos u,\; 0)^\top. Rotations preserve length, so |\dot{\mathbf r}| = a n = 2\pi a/T = \sqrt{\mu/a}, the circular speed. At u = 0 the in-plane velocity is (0, an, 0), and its image's z component an\sin i is positive: the satellite is indeed heading north at the ascending node.

  2. Recover the angles. Given only an inertial position \mathbf r and velocity \mathbf v on a circular orbit, compute i, \Omega and u. Test it on the hero's default point.
    Hint

    The normal \mathbf h = \mathbf r \times \mathbf v is parallel to (\sin\Omega\sin i, -\cos\Omega\sin i, \cos i). The ascending node is in the direction \mathbf z \times \mathbf h.

    Solution 
    import numpy as np

def angles(r, v):
    h = np.cross(r, v); h /= np.linalg.norm(h)
    inc = np.degrees(np.arccos(h[2]))
    raan = np.degrees(np.arctan2(h[0], -h[1])) % 360
    node = np.array([np.cos(np.radians(raan)), np.sin(np.radians(raan)), 0.0])
    # u: angle from the node to r, measured in the direction of motion
    u = np.degrees(np.arctan2(np.dot(np.cross(node, r), h), np.dot(node, r))) % 360
    return inc, raan, u

    For the satellite at (3,160.4, 1,617.1, 5,941.1) km with the velocity of exercise 1, it returns 97.6°, 40° and 60°. The formula for \Omega fails when \sin i = 0: an equatorial orbit has no line of nodes, a singularity of these angles, not of the orbit.

  3. A constellation of your own. Call make_constellation for 6 and for 10 satellites. How many planes and slots does each get, and how many satellites end up in the short plane?
    Hint

    ScenarioConfig(n_sats=6) and ScenarioConfig(n_sats=10); then follow \mathrm{round}(\sqrt n), \lceil n/P \rceil and k \bmod P.

    Solution

    Six satellites: \sqrt 6 = 2.449, so 2 planes of \lceil 6/2 \rceil = 3 slots, 3 + 3, RAAN 0° and 90°, slots 120° apart, a 60° shift between planes. Ten satellites: \sqrt{10} = 3.162, so 3 planes of \lceil 10/3 \rceil = 4 slots; round-robin gives 4 + 3 + 3, RAAN 0°, 60°, 120°, slots 90° apart, a 30° shift between planes. In both, the first satellites' jitter is the same as in the tables above, since the generator is drawn in the same order.

  4. Rotations as a chain. Write position_eci as an explicit matrix product in NumPy, Rz(raan) @ Rx(inc) @ p, for an array of 2,161 times, and time it against the package's version. Then explain why the package writes the product out by hand.
    Hint

    Build p with shape (3, T) so that one matrix product handles every time. Use timeit.

    Solution

    Both are fast at this size; the explicit product builds a 3×3 matrix once and multiplies it onto a 3×T array, the package's version evaluates three expressions elementwise. They agree to rounding (the script finds 1.86 \times 10^{-9} m at most). The hand-written form has advantages beyond speed: it works for any array shape of t without reshaping, and each line can be checked against the derivation, which is exactly what Part 4 did.

Part 8

The chapter on one page

  1. The frame: ECI, origin at the Earth's centre, z to the north, axes fixed among the stars; Newton's law holds there. The package puts x on Greenwich at t = 0.
  2. Three angles: inclination i (tilt), RAAN \Omega (where the ascending node points), argument of latitude u (where the satellite is, from the node). The course's i = 97.6° is retrograde, 7.6° past polar, reaching latitude 82.4°.
  3. Rotations: R_z and R_x are orthogonal matrices; their order matters (R_z(90°)R_x(90°)\mathbf e_x = \mathbf e_y but R_x(90°)R_z(90°)\mathbf e_x = \mathbf e_z).
  4. The formula: \mathbf r = R_z(\Omega)R_x(i)(a\cos u, a\sin u, 0)^\top, multiplied out, is position_eci, checked to 1.86 \times 10^{-9} m over 10,000 random cases. The wrong order misplaces the default satellite by 5,103 km.
  5. The constellation: \mathrm{round}(\sqrt n) planes with RAAN over 180° (the plane at \Omega + 180° is only 15.2° away, flown backwards), slots evenly spaced, planes shifted to interleave, ±5° and ±3° jitter. Small: 2 planes × 2; medium: 3 planes, 3 + 3 + 2; large: 3 planes × 4.

Part 9

Scope and further reading

Scope. The chapter places circular orbits only, with three angles; elliptical orbits need two more elements, the eccentricity and the argument of perigee. It ignores the slow turning of the orbit planes caused by the Earth's bulge, the difference between the package's simplified inertial frame and the standard ones (equinox, precession, nutation), and constellation design beyond what make_constellation does: coverage analysis and optimised phasing are standard topics of mission design [3]. The Earth is drawn as at t = 0 and does not turn; chapter 03 makes it turn.

Satellite Pass Planning from Scratch · Chapter 06 of 10

Constraints and an independent checker

Chapter 05 turned the geometry into a list of 168 opportunities. A plan picks some of them and gives each a start time; this chapter says exactly when a plan is allowed. Four rules, each a few lines of satplan: stay inside the window, collect a target at most once, leave each satellite time to turn between tasks, and respect the power and memory budgets. We write each one down, measure the approximations behind it, see how satplan prepares the rules for a solver (the conflict-pair sweep), and build the piece of code that every plan of every method must pass: the independent checker.

    Part 1

    The decision

    A plan answers two questions for every opportunity o of chapter 05: do we use it, and if so, when does the listening start? In symbols, a binary x_o \in \{0, 1\} and, when x_o = 1, a start time t_o. In satplan a plan is simply a dictionary from opportunity id to start time; the opportunities not in the dictionary are not used.

    What is a feasible plan?

    In plain words: a choice of tasks and start times that a satellite could actually carry out under the model's rules.

    More precisely: a map plan: {opportunity id: start} such that every start lies in its window, no target appears twice, any two tasks on the same satellite are separated by the reconfiguration time, and no power or memory segment exceeds its budget. Its value is the sum of the weights of the chosen opportunities. Planning means finding a feasible plan of maximum value.

    Constraint
    A rule that a plan must satisfy; a plan that breaks one is infeasible, whatever its value.
    Feasibility
    Satisfying all the constraints. Checking it for a given plan is easy; finding the best feasible plan is the hard part (chapters 07 to 10).
    Checker
    A function that takes a plan and lists every violated constraint.

    How many plans are there? Each of the 168 opportunities is in or out, 2^{168} \approx 3.7 \times 10^{50} subsets, and each selected one has a start time on top. Almost all of them are infeasible. The greedy plan of chapter 07 has 54 tasks and value 193; the optimal plan of chapter 08 has 55 tasks and value 219. The difference is not in how many tasks fit but in which ones and in what order. The hero demo opens on the optimal plan.

    Part 2

    The rules, one by one

    Rule 1: the task fits in its window. A task of length d_o must be entirely inside the window [a_o, b_o] where the target is visible:

    a_o \;\le\; t_o \;\le\; b_o - d_o .

    This is the earliest and latest start of chapter 05. In the hero demo, drag a task beyond the end of its start range and the checker reports it as outside.

    Rule 2: each target at most once. A target seen on two passes has two opportunities, but collecting it twice brings nothing (and the weight would count twice). If O_j is the set of opportunities of target j,

    \sum_{o \in O_j} x_o \;\le\; 1 \qquad \text{for every target } j.

    Rule 3: one thing at a time, and time to turn. A satellite points its antenna at one target at a time. Between two tasks it must slew from one direction to the other and let the structure settle. satplan models the time this takes as

    s(a, b) \;=\; \left\lceil\, \sigma + \frac{\theta(a, b)}{\omega} \,\right\rceil, \qquad \theta(a, b) = \arccos(u_a \cdot u_b),

    with a settle time \sigma = 10 s, a slew rate \omega = 2°/s, and u_a, u_b the unit pointing vectors stored in the opportunities (chapter 05). The ceiling keeps everything in integer seconds, which CP-SAT needs. The code is a direct transcription:

    def transition(self, a: int, b: int) -> int:
    """Reconfiguration time (integer seconds) from opportunity a to opportunity b."""
    oa, ob = self.opportunities[a], self.opportunities[b]
    angle = pointing_angle_deg(np.asarray(oa.pointing), np.asarray(ob.pointing))
    return int(math.ceil(self.settle_s + angle / self.slew_rate_deg_s))

def max_transition(self) -> int:
    return int(math.ceil(self.settle_s + 180.0 / self.slew_rate_deg_s))

    The longest possible turn, 180°, takes 10 + 90 = 100 s. If tasks a and b are on the same satellite and both selected, one must finish and the satellite must turn before the other starts:

    t_b \;\ge\; t_a + d_a + s(a, b) \quad \text{or} \quad t_a \;\ge\; t_b + d_b + s(b, a).

    The "or" is what makes the problem hard: it is a disjunction, and the solver must decide which side holds. This is a sequence-dependent setup time in scheduling vocabulary [2]: the gap needed after a task depends on which task comes next. Here s(a, b) = s(b, a) for every pair (the angle is symmetric), but the total time a sequence takes still depends on the order, because the angles along the sequence change with it. Over the 3,832 pairs of opportunities that share a satellite, s ranges from 11 to 100 s, median 39 s.

    In the hero demo, the grey bar after each task in the top lane is the reconfiguration time it needs before the next one. In the optimal plan of satellite 3's third pass, four of the five gaps between consecutive tasks are exactly the reconfiguration time: 19, 22, 13 and 14 s, no slack at all. The solver packs the pass as tightly as the rule allows. Drag any of those tasks one second earlier and the checker objects.

    Rule 4: budgets. Per satellite and revolution, at most 300 s of listening (power); per satellite and memory segment, at most 600 s (memory). With the segment labels r_o and m_o of chapter 05,

    \sum_{o:\,\mathrm{sat}(o)=s,\ r_o = r} d_o\, x_o \;\le\; 300, \qquad \sum_{o:\,\mathrm{sat}(o)=s,\ m_o = k} d_o\, x_o \;\le\; 600.

    Part 5 returns to these. The objective, finally, is \max \sum_o w_o x_o.

    Part 3

    Two properties of s(a, b)

    Pointing at the midpoint. The pointing vector u_o is taken at the middle of the window, and s(a, b) is therefore one fixed number per pair. In reality the direction from the satellite to the target changes all through the pass: the satellite moves at 7.6 km/s, and the target goes from ahead of it to behind it. The widget below follows that direction for one opportunity. For the first one in the list (the default), a 230 s window of satellite 0, the direction is 58.4° away from the midpoint direction when the window opens, 57.3° when it closes, and it turns 115.7° from start to end.

    The script code/ch06_numbers.py does the same for all 168 opportunities. Between the start and the end of a window the direction turns by 14.2° to 123.7°, median 96.1°. The largest angle between the true direction and the midpoint direction, within a window, has a median of 49.0° and a maximum of 64.2°: at 2°/s, up to 32 s of slewing.

    Does it matter for the plans? Take the optimal plan, and for each pair of consecutive tasks on a satellite recompute the reconfiguration time with the directions at the moments that count: where the first task ends and where the second begins. The true-time transition differs from the midpoint one by −30 to +59 s (median +4 s), and, with the plan's start times unchanged, 21 of the 51 gaps in the optimal plan would be too short under the model's own slew rule (23 of 50 for the greedy plan). That so many fail is partly because the plan is packed: 26 of its 51 gaps have no slack at all in the model, so any increase breaks them. Moving the starts does not rescue it: keeping each satellite's order of tasks and trying every whole-second start inside the windows, only one of the four satellites' sequences (two of four for the greedy plan) can meet the true-time rule even between consecutive tasks. Two tasks of one pass turn together with the pass, which is why the error goes both ways.

    A strong simplification. With fixed per-pair transitions, the plans of this course are feasible for the model, not for a satellite slewing between true directions. The README lists this among the limitations. The literature on agile satellites uses time-dependent transition times, which depend on both start times [1]; the problem keeps its structure (choose, order, separate), but the separation becomes a function of the two start times instead of a constant, and each solver has to be adapted. Everything in the following chapters is about the model as it stands.

    The triangle inequality. Angles between directions obey \theta(a, c) \le \theta(a, b) + \theta(b, c): turning from a to c directly is never longer than going via b. Does s inherit it? The settle time appears once on the left and twice on the right, and the ceiling satisfies \lceil x + y \rceil \le \lceil x \rceil + \lceil y \rceil, so

    s(a, c) = 10 + \lceil \theta_{ac}/2 \rceil \;\le\; 10 + \lceil \theta_{ab}/2 \rceil + \lceil \theta_{bc}/2 \rceil \;=\; s(a, b) + s(b, c) - 10.

    (The settle time is an integer, so it comes out of the ceiling.) The ceiling does not break the inequality and the double settle time strengthens it, by at least 10 s. The script checks all 377,304 ordered triples of distinct opportunities on a common satellite: the smallest slack s(a,b) + s(b,c) - s(a,c) is exactly 10 s, and the angles themselves satisfy the inequality up to rounding (smallest slack 1.25 \times 10^{-8} degrees).

    The consequence: if a plan respects the rule between consecutive tasks a, b, c, then

    t_c - t_a \;\ge\; d_a + s(a, b) + d_b + s(b, c) \;\ge\; d_a + s(a, c) + 10 + d_b,

    and the rule holds between a and c too, with room to spare. Checking consecutive tasks would be enough. The checker of Part 6 nevertheless checks every pair on a satellite. A checker must not rely on a property of the function it checks: the time-dependent transitions above, or any other slew model, need not satisfy the triangle inequality, and a checker that silently assumed it would then pass infeasible plans. Checking every pair costs 22 \cdot 21 / 2 = 231 comparisons on the busiest satellite of the optimal plan (22 tasks).

    Part 4

    The conflict-pair sweep

    Rule 3 is a statement about pairs. A solver model needs one constraint (or more) per pair that can actually interfere, and most pairs cannot: a task in the first pass of a satellite and a task in its last pass are hours apart. build_pairs finds the pairs that matter with a sweep over each satellite's windows, sorted by start:

    def build_pairs(self) -> None:
    """Enumerate interfering pairs with a sweep over each satellite's windows."""
    s_max = self.max_transition()
    pairs = []
    for ids in self.by_satellite().values():
        ids = sorted(ids, key=lambda i: self.opportunities[i].window_start)
        for p, a in enumerate(ids):
            oa = self.opportunities[a]
            for b in ids[p + 1 :]:
                ob = self.opportunities[b]
                if ob.window_start >= oa.window_end + s_max:
                    break  # b is always after a, with any transition
                if oa.target == ob.target:
                    continue  # at most one per target already forbids both
                s_ab, s_ba = self.transition(a, b), self.transition(b, a)
                # 'a then b' always satisfied: latest end of a + s_ab <= earliest start of b
                if oa.window_end + s_ab <= ob.earliest_start:
                    continue
                if ob.window_end + s_ba <= oa.earliest_start:
                    continue
                a_first = oa.earliest_start + oa.duration + s_ab <= ob.latest_start
                b_first = ob.earliest_start + ob.duration + s_ba <= oa.latest_start
                pairs.append(ConflictPair(a, b, s_ab, s_ba, a_first, b_first))
    self.pairs = pairs

    When a pair can be skipped. A task on a ends by b_a at the latest, and a task on b starts at a_b at the earliest. If b_a + s(a, b) \le a_b, every placement of the two puts b after a with enough time to turn: the pair can never violate rule 3 and needs no constraint. The break is the same test with the largest possible transition, s_{\max} = 100 s, in place of s(a, b): since the windows are sorted by start, once one b is that far away, all the following ones are too, and the inner loop can stop. That is what makes the sweep fast: it never looks at pairs further apart than one window plus 100 s. Pairs of the same target are skipped as well, because rule 2 already forbids selecting both. (The second continue, "b then a always satisfied", can never fire: b starts no earlier than a, so b's window cannot end before a's starts. It is there for symmetry.)

    The three cases. For a pair that is kept, two flags say which orders are possible at all, by placing the first task at its earliest start and asking whether the second can still start by its latest:

    a \text{ first possible} \iff a_a + d_a + s(a, b) \le b_b - d_b, \qquad b \text{ first possible} \iff a_b + d_b + s(b, a) \le b_a - d_a.
    • both orders possible: the model must choose an order when both are selected (an order variable and two conditional precedences);
    • one order possible: if both are selected, that order is forced (one conditional precedence);
    • neither: the two cannot both be selected, x_a + x_b \le 1. The pair is mutually exclusive.

    Chapters 08 and 09 turn each case into solver constraints. The widget shows one pair at a time, both orders placed as early as possible.

    The default pair is the first "both" pair of the list: opportunities 15 and 7 on satellite 0, windows [580, 820] and [590, 810] s, 30 and 60 s long, 20 s apart in reconfiguration. Either can go first: 580 + 30 + 20 = 630 is before 7's latest start of 750, and 590 + 60 + 20 = 670 is before 15's latest start of 790. Switch to "neither" and the first pair is 2 and 10, windows [0, 100] and [0, 120] s, 30 and 90 s long, 18 s apart: 30 + 18 = 48 is after 10's latest start of 30, and 90 + 18 = 108 after 2's latest start of 70. Too much work for the time available.

    Testing the sweep by brute force. A sweep with a break is the kind of code where an off-by-one loses pairs silently, and a lost pair is a missing constraint: the solver could then return a plan that breaks rule 3. The test suite compares it with the obvious quadratic loop:

    def test_pair_sweep_skips_only_free_pairs(small):
    listed = {(p.a, p.b) for p in small.pairs} | {(p.b, p.a) for p in small.pairs}
    opps = small.opportunities
    for ids in small.by_satellite().values():
        for a, b in itertools.combinations(ids, 2):
            if (a, b) in listed or opps[a].target == opps[b].target:
                continue
            free_ab = opps[a].window_end + small.transition(a, b) <= opps[b].earliest_start
            free_ba = opps[b].window_end + small.transition(b, a) <= opps[a].earliest_start
            assert free_ab or free_ba

    Every pair the sweep did not list must be free in one direction. The test runs on a 3-satellite, 40-target instance; ch06_numbers.py repeats it on the course's instance, where all 2,951 unlisted pairs of different targets are free.

    Part 5

    Budgets as knapsacks

    Each budget inequality of rule 4 has the form "sum of the sizes of the chosen items \le a capacity": a knapsack constraint, the constraint of the classic problem of filling a bag of limited capacity with items of given sizes [3]. The items are the opportunities of one segment, their sizes the listening times. Because chapter 05 assigned each opportunity to exactly one segment of each kind, the groups are fixed sets, built once:

    def budget_groups(self) -> list[tuple[str, int, list[int]]]:
    """(kind, limit, opportunity ids) for every duty-cycle and memory segment."""
    duty: dict[tuple, list[int]] = {}
    mem: dict[tuple, list[int]] = {}
    for o in self.opportunities:
        duty.setdefault((o.sat, o.revolution), []).append(o.idx)
        mem.setdefault((o.sat, o.memory_segment), []).append(o.idx)
    groups = [("duty", self.duty_budget_s, ids) for ids in duty.values()]
    groups += [("memory", self.memory_budget_s, ids) for ids in mem.values()]
    # A group whose opportunities cannot exceed the limit even if all are chosen is dropped.
    return [
        (k, lim, ids)
        for (k, lim, ids) in groups
        if sum(self.opportunities[i].duration for i in ids) > lim
    ]

    Why drop groups. If all the opportunities of a segment together ask for no more than the limit, then every plan, even one that selects all of them, respects the budget. The constraint is redundant: it can never cut off a plan, so it only costs the solver time. The small instance has 12 non-empty power segments and 14 memory segments, 26 in all; 7 can never be exceeded, and 19 remain, 10 for power and 9 for memory. The ones that remain are heavily oversubscribed: their opportunities ask for 1.45 to 7.1 times the limit. The worst is satellite 3's last revolution, 33 opportunities asking for 2,130 s against 300.

    In the optimal plan, six power groups are exactly full (300 of 300 s), and no memory group is: the largest memory use is 420 of 600 s. In the small instance, power and reconfiguration limit the plan and memory does not: solved again with the memory budget removed, CP-SAT proves the same optimum, 219. Chapter 10's memory sweep on the medium instance finds the same: memory binds only when it is cut to 300 s or less. In the hero demo, satellite 3's revolutions 2 and 3 are full; switch on any other task of the default pass and the power bar turns orange.

    The small instance's constraints. 168 windows (rule 1), 114 targets with at least one opportunity (rule 2, which binds only for the 47 targets with two or three), 876 conflict pairs: 356 two-way, 414 one-way, 106 exclusive (rule 3), 19 knapsacks: 10 power, 9 memory (rule 4).

    Part 6

    The independent checker

    Four methods produce plans in this course: a greedy heuristic, a CP-SAT model, a rolling-horizon CP-SAT and a MILP. Each one encodes the rules in its own way: precedences on the conflict pairs, order literals, big-M inequalities. Each encoding can be wrong: a sign, a missing pair, a big-M too small, a fixed task forgotten between blocks. A wrong encoding does not crash; it returns a plan, with a good value, that breaks a rule. The only defence is to check every plan against the rules themselves, with code that does not reuse the encodings:

    def check_plan(inst: PlanningInstance, plan: dict[int, float]) -> list[str]:
    """Return a list of violations (empty list = feasible). plan maps opportunity id -> start time."""
    errors = []
    opps = inst.opportunities
    seen_targets: dict[int, int] = {}
    per_sat: dict[int, list[int]] = {}
    for i, t in plan.items():
        o = opps[i]
        if t < o.earliest_start - TOL or t > o.latest_start + TOL:
            errors.append(f"opp {i}: start {t} outside [{o.earliest_start}, {o.latest_start}]")
        if o.target in seen_targets:
            errors.append(f"target {o.target} collected twice (opps {seen_targets[o.target]}, {i})")
        seen_targets[o.target] = i
        per_sat.setdefault(o.sat, []).append(i)

    for s, ids in per_sat.items():
        ids.sort(key=lambda i: plan[i])
        for p, a in enumerate(ids):
            for b in ids[p + 1 :]:
                gap_needed = opps[a].duration + inst.transition(a, b)
                if plan[b] - plan[a] < gap_needed - TOL:
                    errors.append(...)

    for kind, limit, ids in inst.budget_groups():
        used = sum(opps[i].duration for i in ids if i in plan)
        if used > limit + TOL:
            errors.append(f"{kind} budget exceeded: {used}s > {limit}s")
    # groups dropped by budget_groups() cannot be exceeded by construction
    return errors

    Read it rule by rule: one loop for windows and targets, one double loop over every pair of tasks on a satellite (sorted by start, so the earlier one must be followed by the gap), one loop over the budgets. TOL is 10^{-6}, because the MILP of chapter 09 returns real-valued start times.

    What "independent" means here. The checker does not use the conflict pairs, the order flags or any solver variable. It recomputes transition for every pair of scheduled tasks, including pairs the sweep skipped, so a bug in build_pairs or in a model shows up as a violation. What it does share with the solvers is the problem's definition: the opportunities, transition(), and budget_groups() (which is safe to share, because the groups it drops can never be exceeded). A mistake in those would be invisible to it, which is why they are tested separately and kept short. The rule of thumb is to share the specification and nothing of the method.

    What it catches. The tests in tests/test_planning.py:

    • test_checker_catches_violations: on a hand-made two-task instance, a plan over the power budget ("budget"), two tasks without the reconfiguration gap ("need") and a start after the latest start ("outside");
    • test_checker_catches_double_collection: one target collected by two satellites ("twice");
    • test_cpsat_and_milp_agree_and_beat_greedy: on three generated instances of about 170 opportunities, the greedy, CP-SAT and MILP plans all pass the checker and their values match plan_value;
    • test_rolling_horizon_is_feasible_and_beats_greedy: the rolling-horizon plan passes it too.

    The benchmark script goes further: it calls the checker on every plan and stops with an error if one is infeasible, so no number in the results table comes from a plan that was not checked. On the course's instance, the greedy and optimal plans both return an empty list. Break the optimal plan on purpose and it speaks: start one task 10 s past its latest start and it reports the start outside its window and, as a consequence, a reconfiguration gap of 20 s where 75 s are needed; add a second opportunity of a target already collected and it reports the double collection, a gap of 0 s where 117 s are needed and a power budget of 330 s over 300. Adding every unused opportunity at its earliest start yields 646 violations.

    The hero demo runs the same checker (Sat.checkPlan, a line-by-line port) on the whole plan at every change. Try three things on the default pass: drag the task of opportunity 130 one second to the left (reconfiguration); switch on opportunity 164 (reconfiguration, power, and a double collection: its target 115 is already collected by opportunity 75 on satellite 1); load the greedy plan, which is feasible too, and compare the gaps.

    A solver is a search procedure; a checker is the definition. Keep them apart, and let no plan reach a table, a figure or a satellite without passing the definition.

    Part 7

    Check your understanding

    1. What exactly does a plan decide?

    Which opportunities are used (a subset) and, for each used one, a start time. In satplan, a dictionary from opportunity id to start time.

    2. Two tasks point 40° apart. What is the reconfiguration time, and how long can it be at most?

    \lceil 10 + 40/2 \rceil = 30 s. At most 10 + 180/2 = 100 s, for opposite directions.

    3. Why is rule 3 harder for a solver than rules 1, 2 and 4?

    Rules 1, 2 and 4 are single linear inequalities. Rule 3 is a disjunction: a before b with a gap, or b before a with a gap. The solver must choose the side, and with many pairs the choices interact.

    4. When can build_pairs skip a pair, and why does its break use s_{\max}?

    When even the latest end of a plus the transition is before the earliest start of b: every placement is then fine. The break must hold for all the following windows, whose transitions are not computed, so it uses the largest possible transition, 100 s.

    5. What do the three cases of a kept pair mean for a model?

    Both orders possible: choose an order, enforce the matching precedence if both are selected. One order: enforce it if both are selected. Neither: forbid selecting both. Small instance: 356, 414 and 106.

    6. The transition satisfies the triangle inequality. Why does the checker still compare every pair of tasks on a satellite?

    Because a checker must not depend on a property of the thing it checks. Consecutive checks suffice only for transitions that satisfy the inequality; a different slew model, such as time-dependent transitions, might not. Checking all pairs costs a few hundred comparisons.

    7. Why are 7 of the 26 budget segments dropped, and is that safe for the checker?

    All their opportunities together ask for no more than the limit, so no plan can exceed it: the constraint is redundant. It is safe for the checker for the same reason, as its closing comment says.

    8. How large is the error of taking pointing directions at window midpoints?

    Large. The direction turns a median 96° across a window, and recomputing the optimal plan's 51 consecutive transitions with true directions, at the plan's own start times, makes 21 of the gaps too short, and even retiming the tasks within their windows (same order) leaves three of the four satellites' sequences infeasible. The model's plans are feasible for the model; time-dependent transitions would be needed for a real satellite.

    Part 8

    Exercises

    1. Break the sweep. Change the break in build_pairs to use s_ab of the current pair instead of s_max. Run the brute-force test. What happens, and why?
      Hint

      After the break, the loop never looks at later windows of the same satellite. Is a later window always free just because the current one is?

      Solution

      The code is now wrong, and the test still passes. With s_ab the break says "this b is free of a", but a later window b' may start a little later and need a longer transition (a target in a very different direction): b_a + s(a, b') > a_{b'}. The sweep would stop before it and lose the pair, and a solver could then schedule a and b' too close. Yet ch06_numbers.py runs the faulty sweep on the test's instance and on the course's: it finds all 22 and all 876 pairs. The situation simply never arises in them. A passing test is evidence, not proof; the argument "s_{\max} bounds every transition" is what makes the original break correct. And if a lost pair ever did let a solver return a bad plan, the checker would still catch it.

    2. A consecutive-only checker. Write a checker that compares only consecutive tasks on each satellite. Show that it returns the same verdict as check_plan on the greedy and CP-SAT plans and on 1,000 random plans. Then replace transition by a function that violates the triangle inequality and find a plan where the verdicts differ.
      Hint

      A function that breaks the inequality: a large time between two particular directions and small times through a third, for example s(a, c) = 100 and s(a, b) = s(b, c) = 11, with short tasks.

      Solution

      With satplan's s, the verdicts agree on every plan (Part 3 proves it). With the made-up function, schedule a, then b right after its 11 s, then c right after its 11 s: consecutive checks pass, but t_c - t_a = d_a + 11 + d_b + 11, which is less than d_a + 100 for short tasks. Only the all-pairs checker sees it.

    3. Time-dependent transitions. Using ground_points_eci and position_eci, write true_transition(a, t_a, b, t_b) that takes the direction to a's target at t_a + d_a and to b's at t_b. Reproduce "21 of 51" for the CP-SAT plan.
      Hint

      The direction is (g - r)/\lVert g - r \rVert with g the target and r the satellite in the inertial frame, at the same time. The CP-SAT plan is in interactive/instance-small.js.

      Solution

      code/ch06_numbers.py does it in the section "pointing inside a window": for each satellite, sort the plan by start, and for each consecutive pair compare t_b - (t_a + d_a) with ceil(10 + angle / 2) at the true times. It finds 51 consecutive pairs and 21 that are too short (greedy: 23 of 50), with differences from −30 to +59 s against the midpoint transitions.

    4. A plan by hand. In the hero demo, empty satellite 3's third pass and build your own plan for it. What value can you reach for the pass without a violation? Compare with the optimal plan's 30.
      Hint

      Revolution 2 of satellite 3 is shared with the next pass, where the optimal plan spends 60 s; the pass can use at most 240 s of the power budget if that task is kept. Start with the two weight-8 targets (opportunities 130 and 156).

      Solution

      30 is the best possible for this pass alone: ch06_numbers.py solves the 15 opportunities of the pass on their own with CP-SAT and proves 30 optimal, with 240 s or with the full 300 s of power. The optimal plan takes 153, 130, 166, 152, 165 and 156 (weights 4, 8, 2, 4, 4, 8), 240 s of listening, with four of the five gaps at zero slack; for the pass alone other sets reach 30 too, for example with 164 in place of 153, but in the demo, with the rest of the optimal plan kept, 164 collects a target that opportunity 75 already has, and the optimal set is the only one that reaches 30. Whatever you reach by hand, the checker tells you at each step whether you are still allowed.

    Part 9

    The chapter on one page

    1. The decision: a subset of the 168 opportunities and a start for each; 2^{168} \approx 3.7 \times 10^{50} subsets.
    2. The rules: start in [a_o, b_o - d_o]; at most one opportunity per target; on a satellite, a gap of d + s(a, b) in one order or the other, with s = \lceil 10 + \theta/2 \rceil (11 to 100 s); 300 s per revolution, 600 s per memory segment.
    3. Approximations: pointing at window midpoints is a strong simplification (the direction turns a median 96° in a window; 21 of 51 gaps of the optimal plan too short with true directions); s satisfies the triangle inequality with at least 10 s to spare.
    4. Conflict pairs: a sorted sweep with a break at s_{\max}; of 3,832 same-satellite pairs, 876 kept: 356 both orders, 414 one, 106 neither; tested by brute force.
    5. Budgets: knapsacks over fixed groups; 26 segments, 19 can bind (10 power, 9 memory); in the optimal plan 6 power groups are full, no memory group.
    6. The checker: recomputes every rule for every pair of tasks, shares the problem definition but no solver encoding, and every plan of every method goes through it.

    The vocabulary, in one table

    WordMeaning
    Feasible planA plan that satisfies every constraint.
    Reconfiguration times(a, b): settle plus slew between the two pointing directions, rounded up.
    Sequence-dependent setupA gap between two jobs that depends on which job follows which.
    DisjunctionA constraint of the form "this or that"; here, the two orders of a pair.
    Triangle inequalitys(a, c) \le s(a, b) + s(b, c): a detour is never faster.
    Conflict pairTwo opportunities on one satellite whose tasks can interfere.
    Mutually exclusiveA pair with no feasible order: at most one of the two can be selected.
    Knapsack constraintA sum of fixed sizes of chosen items below a capacity.
    Redundant constraintOne that no plan can violate; dropping it changes nothing but the model size.
    Independent checkerCode that verifies a plan against the rules without reusing any solver's encoding of them.
    Part 10

    Scope and further reading

    Scope. This chapter states the rules and checks plans; it does not find good plans. Chapter 07 builds one greedily, chapter 08 encodes the rules for CP-SAT (optional intervals, AddNoOverlap, enforced precedences), chapter 09 as a MILP with big-M disjunctions, and chapter 10 splits the horizon into blocks. The physical slew model (a constant rate, no acceleration limits, no attitude constraints) is the simplest one that gives sequence-dependent times.

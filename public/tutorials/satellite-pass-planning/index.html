Satellite pass planning from scratch

A small constellation of satellites flies over the oceans, listening for radio emitters at maritime targets. Each satellite sees a target for a few minutes per pass, must turn its antenna between targets, can only listen so long per orbit before its battery runs low, and fills its memory until it next flies over a ground station. Which targets should each satellite listen to, on which pass, and when? This course answers that question from the very beginning: Newton's law, the orbit it gives, the frames that place the orbit in space, the Earth turning underneath, the geometry of seeing the ground, and then the planning problem itself, solved four ways and compared. Each chapter teaches one idea a student can reuse elsewhere, and ties it to the lines of the author's satellite-pass-planning repository that implement it.

One project, ten concepts

The repository is a compact modelling study: a synthetic constellation on circular sun-synchronous orbits at 550 km, ten generic ocean areas, four illustrative ground stations, and a planner that chooses listening tasks under three kinds of constraint. It is solved by a greedy rule, by constraint programming with OR-Tools CP-SAT, by a rolling-horizon decomposition and by a mixed-integer linear program with HiGHS, and every plan from every method is checked by an independent program. Everything is simplified and synthetic: the course says where each simplification matters and by how much, and makes no claim about any real system.

The course follows the data. Part one computes where the satellites are and when they can see each target. Part two describes what a plan is and what makes it feasible. Part three looks for the best plan and asks how we know it is the best. The whole model runs in your browser, ported line for line from the Python and checked against it: the same 876 conflicting pairs, the same greedy plan, the same verdicts from the checker.

Part one · Where the satellites are, and what they can see

Physics and geometry, from scratch. Needs high-school mechanics and vectors; each chapter introduces the mathematics it uses.

  1. 01 Circular Orbits from Newton Newton's law of gravitation, the speed and period of a circular orbit, Kepler's third law, and a trajectory built from scratch by stepping the equation of motion forward in time with three integrators. Why the repository can use the closed-form circle, and why a flight-dynamics tool still integrates. You find At 550 km a satellite flies at 7589 m/s and circles the Earth in 95.5 minutes. Stepped at 60 s for five revolutions, explicit Euler spirals out to 17 970 km from the centre, symplectic Euler keeps its energy within half a percent, and RK4 ends 269 m from the exact circle the repository uses.
  2. 02 Placing an Orbit in Space Reference frames and rotations: the inertial frame, inclination, the ascending node and the argument of latitude, rotation matrices and why their order matters, the repository's position formula derived term by term, and how the constellation's planes and phases are laid out. You find The repository's position formula is the product of two rotations, inclination then ascending node, and matches it to within 2 nanometres over 10 000 random cases; the other order lands 5103 km away. Why the planes are spread over 180° and not 360°: at 97.6° two planes with nodes 180° apart are only 15.2° apart.
  3. 03 The Earth Turns Underneath Rotating frames: Earth-fixed and inertial coordinates, the sidereal day, latitude and longitude of the point under the satellite, ground tracks and their westward shift, the highest latitude reached, revisits, and what the Earth's flattening would add to a model that ignores it. You find Each revolution's track lands 23.94° (2662 km) further west and never goes beyond 82.4° of latitude. The Earth's flattening, which the repository ignores, would turn each orbit plane by 0.9901° a day, close to the Sun's 0.9856°: that is what sun-synchronous means. Over the 6-hour horizon it would shift the tracks by 27.5 km.
  4. 04 Seeing the Ground Visibility geometry: elevation, off-nadir angle and Earth central angle, the footprint, which of the limits actually binds at 550 km, how long a pass lasts, and what sampling visibility on a ten-second grid does to the windows. You find At 550 km the 55° sensor cone, not the 10° elevation mask, decides what a satellite sees: it sees 874 km to each side, and an overhead pass lasts 247.6 s. The 10 s grid shortens every window by 10.6 s on average, and two windows long enough for their task are dropped because of it.

Part two · What a plan is

Modelling: from geometry to a combinatorial problem a solver can read, and the rules a plan must obey. Needs part one's windows, or just the idea that each target is visible in short windows.

  1. 05 From Geometry to a Planning Problem Abstraction: targets with priorities and listening times, opportunities (one satellite, one target, one window), ground contacts, orbital revolutions and memory segments, the statistics of the course's instance, and what the model keeps and drops. You find Of 193 visibility windows, 25 are too short for their task, leaving 168 opportunities. Six targets, all in the Bay of Bengal, can never be collected, so 316 of the 326 priority points are reachable. Assigning each window to a revolution and a memory segment by its midpoint puts no task on the wrong side of a boundary on the small instance, and could for 1.5% of windows on the medium one.
  2. 06 Constraints and an Independent Checker Feasibility: the decision, sequence-dependent reconfiguration times, the sweep that finds which pairs of tasks can interfere and its three cases, power and memory budgets as knapsack constraints, and a checker that shares no code with the solvers. You find 876 of the 3832 same-satellite pairs can interfere: 356 in either order, 414 in one, 106 never together. Measured here for the first time, the midpoint pointing direction is the model's largest simplification: rechecked at the real start and end times, 21 of the optimal plan's 51 back-to-back gaps would be too short.

Part three · Finding a good plan, and knowing how good it is

Optimisation: a heuristic, two exact formulations and a decomposition, on the same instances, and how to read the comparison. Needs part two.

  1. 07 A Greedy Plan and What It Leaves Behind Heuristics: the repository's greedy rule, highest value per second first and earliest feasible insertion, stepped through decision by decision; why it falls short of the optimum, with counterexamples; other orderings and local improvement. You find The greedy collects 193 against the proven optimum 219, 11.9% below, in a hundredth of a second. None of 1000 random orders does better than 171. A simple ruin-and-recreate search lifts the plan to 211 in 2000 iterations.
  2. 08 Constraint Programming with CP-SAT Exact search and proofs of optimality: Boolean and interval variables, at-most-one, no-overlap and enforced precedences, what the solver does with them, best solution against best bound, and a small branch-and-bound written in the page. You find On a 21-opportunity toy, the page's own branch and bound proves the optimum 28 in 1311 nodes with the weakest bound, or 50 with a budget-aware bound and a greedy start; every count matches Python. CP-SAT proves 219 optimal on the small instance in under a second.
  3. 09 The Same Problem as a MILP Formulation strength: linear programs and their relaxations, big-M disjunctions and the smallest valid M, why the relaxation of this model is weak, and how that shows up against CP-SAT as the instances grow. You find The LP relaxation bound is 229.3 on the small instance, and it is identical if every reconfiguration row is deleted: the relaxation sees the budgets and nothing of the reconfiguration times. HiGHS proves the same optimum, 219, 10 to 17 times slower than CP-SAT, and falls below greedy on the large instances.
  4. 10 Rolling Horizons and Reading a Benchmark Decomposition and experimental method: solving one hour at a time, myopia at block borders, the choice of block size, gaps against proven bounds, run-to-run noise, which constraint binds, and where the project could go next. You find Solving one hour at a time loses 1 point of 219 on the small instance, gained early and paid back in the last block. Five CP-SAT runs with different seeds on the medium instance spread over 5 points, so differences of that size are noise. A rerun seeded with the rolling plan reaches 560, placing the medium optimum between 560 and 575.

Running it yourself

The chapter pages run the model in the browser. To run the original code, download the code: the satplan package, the benchmark script, the tests and the benchmark results, and in code/ one script per chapter that reproduces every number quoted on that chapter's page. Or clone the repository. Then:

python -m venv .venv
.venv/Scripts/python -m pip install -r requirements.txt   # Linux/macOS: .venv/bin/python
.venv/Scripts/python -m pytest                            # 14 tests, about 35 s
.venv/Scripts/python code/ch04_numbers.py                 # every number on chapter 04's page
.venv/Scripts/python scripts/run_benchmark.py --quick     # a one-minute version of the benchmark

The full benchmark takes about twenty minutes on a laptop (CP-SAT with eight threads); its results ship in results/, so the chapters that quote it can be checked without rerunning it.

Scope

The scenario is synthetic: circular orbits around a spherical Earth with no perturbations, generic ocean boxes, illustrative ground stations and budgets. That is enough to produce realistic pass lengths, revisit patterns and contention between targets, which is what the planning problem needs, and it keeps every formula short enough to derive in a chapter. The course is a worked example of how to go from a physical system to a model, from a model to an optimisation problem, and from solver output to a claim one can defend.

Circular orbits from Newton

Before a planner can decide which target a satellite listens to, it must know where the satellite is, at every second of the day. This chapter builds that knowledge from one law of physics. Newton's gravitation gives the speed and the period of a circular orbit, 7.6 km/s and 95.5 minutes at the course's 550 km. Then we let the computer fly the satellite step by step, with three integration methods, and watch two of them fail in instructive ways. That explains why the course's Python package can skip integration altogether and use a closed-form circle, and why a real flight-dynamics tool cannot.

Part 0

The course in one table

The course is built on one small public project, satellite-pass-planning. A synthetic constellation of radio-listening satellites flies over the oceans, and a planner must decide which maritime targets each satellite listens to, on which pass, and when. Each satellite needs time to re-point between two targets, may listen only so long per orbit (power) and between two ground-station contacts (memory). The project solves this with a greedy rule, with constraint programming (CP-SAT) and with a mixed-integer linear program, and checks every plan with an independent checker. Everything in it is synthetic: circular orbits, a spherical Earth, generic ocean areas, illustrative budgets. It is a modelling exercise and makes no claim about any real satellite or operator.

The ten chapters climb from physics to optimisation, one concept each. The first four build the geometry: where the satellite is, and when it can see a target. The last six turn that geometry into a planning problem and solve it.

Part 1

Gravity and the circular orbit

Why a satellite does not fall down. It does fall, all the time. Throw a stone horizontally and it lands some distance away; throw it faster and it lands further. Throw it fast enough and the ground curves away beneath it as quickly as it falls, so it never lands. That is an orbit: free fall with enough sideways speed to keep missing the Earth. To make this precise we need the force that pulls the stone, and Newton's second law to say what the force does.

Newton's law of gravitation. Two bodies of masses M and m at distance d attract each other with a force of magnitude F = GMm/d^2, where G is the gravitational constant. For a satellite around the Earth, M is the Earth's mass and m the satellite's. Put the Earth's centre at the origin and call \mathbf r the satellite's position vector, so d = |\mathbf r|. The force points from the satellite to the Earth, along -\mathbf r/|\mathbf r|. Newton's second law, m\ddot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf F, then gives the satellite's acceleration (two dots mean the second derivative in time):

\ddot{\mathbf r} \;=\; -\frac{GM}{|\mathbf r|^2}\,\frac{\mathbf r}{|\mathbf r|} \;=\; -\,\mu\,\frac{\mathbf r}{|\mathbf r|^3}, \qquad \mu = GM.

Three things to notice. The satellite's own mass m has cancelled: a 1 kg satellite and a 1000 kg satellite follow the same path. G and M only appear as their product \mu, the gravitational parameter, which is known far more precisely than either factor because it is what satellite tracking measures. And we have treated the Earth as fixed, which is fine since it is more than 10^{19} times heavier than even a 420-tonne space station. This is the two-body problem: one attracting point mass (a spherical Earth attracts exactly like a point at its centre) and one body moving around it, nothing else.

What is the two-body problem?

In plain words: a small body moving under the gravity of one big spherical body, and nothing else: no air, no Moon, no Sun, no bulge at the Earth's equator.

More precisely: the solution of \ddot{\mathbf r} = -\mu\,\mathbf r/|\mathbf r|^3 from a given position and velocity. Its solutions are conic sections with the Earth's centre at one focus: circles, ellipses, parabolas, hyperbolas. Satellites that stay up follow circles or ellipses [1].

\mu
The Earth's gravitational parameter GM. The package uses 3.986004418 \times 10^{14} m³/s².
R_E
The Earth's radius. The package uses the mean radius, 6,371 km, for a spherical Earth.
Altitude
Height above the surface, |\mathbf r| - R_E. The course's satellites fly at 550 km.

These constants are the first three lines of the package's geometry module, satplan/geometry.py:

MU_EARTH = 3.986004418e14  # m^3 / s^2
R_EARTH = 6_371_000.0  # m, mean radius
OMEGA_EARTH = 7.2921159e-5  # rad / s

The third, the Earth's rotation rate, waits for chapter 03. With the first two, gravity at the surface is \mu/R_E^2 = 9.820 m/s², the familiar g. At 550 km it is \mu/a^2 = 8.321 m/s², still 84.7 % of the surface value. Astronauts in orbit float because they fall with their spacecraft, not because gravity is gone.

The circular orbit: a balance

Look for a solution in which the satellite moves on a circle of radius a at constant speed v. A body moving on a circle is accelerating even at constant speed, because its direction keeps turning. The acceleration points to the centre and has magnitude v^2/a (the centripetal acceleration; one line of proof: the position is a(\cos nt, \sin nt) with angular rate n = v/a, and differentiating twice gives -n^2 times the position, of magnitude n^2 a = v^2/a). Gravity points to the centre too, with magnitude \mu/a^2. The circle is a solution exactly when the two agree:

\frac{v^2}{a} = \frac{\mu}{a^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{a}}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi a}{v} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}}.

The period T is the circumference divided by the speed. Squaring it, T^2 = 4\pi^2 a^3/\mu: the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the radius. This is Kepler's third law, found by Kepler from planetary observations decades before Newton explained it. For an ellipse the same law holds with a the semi-major axis, half the longest diameter, which is why the package calls the radius semi_major_axis.

The course's orbit. At 550 km, a = 6371 + 550 = 6921.0 km. The circular speed is \sqrt{\mu/a} = 7589.0 m/s, about 27,320 km/h. The period is 2\pi\sqrt{a^3/\mu} = 5730.1 s, or 95.50 minutes, so a satellite goes round 15.08 times a day (86,400 s). Its angle along the orbit advances at n = 2\pi/T = 1.0965 \times 10^{-3} rad/s, 3.770° per minute. These are the package's numbers: CircularOrbit.period_s returns 5730.127 s.

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class CircularOrbit:
    altitude_m: float
    inclination_deg: float
    raan_deg: float
    phase_deg: float  # argument of latitude at t = 0

    @property
    def semi_major_axis(self) -> float:
        return R_EARTH + self.altitude_m

    @property
    def period_s(self) -> float:
        return 2.0 * np.pi * np.sqrt(self.semi_major_axis**3 / MU_EARTH)

The other two fields, inclination and right ascension of the ascending node, say how the circle is tilted in space; they are the subject of chapter 02. The phase says where on the circle the satellite is at time zero.

How speed and period change with height. Higher orbits are slower, since v \propto a^{-1/2}, and they are also longer, so the period grows faster than the radius: doubling a multiplies T by 2^{3/2} = 2.828. Move the slider below from a very low orbit to the geostationary one.

Geostationary, with this Earth. A satellite over the equator whose period equals the Earth's rotation period stays above the same point of the ground. The Earth turns once in a sidereal day, 2\pi/\omega_E = 86{,}164.1 s or 23.9345 h; this is shorter than the 24 h solar day because the Earth also moves around the Sun (chapter 03). Kepler's law solved for the radius gives a = (\mu T^2/4\pi^2)^{1/3} = 42{,}164.2 km. Textbooks quote the geostationary altitude as 35,786 km, measured from the equatorial radius of 6,378.137 km. The package's spherical Earth has the mean radius, 7.1 km smaller, so with its constants the same orbit is 35,793 km above the surface. The orbit is the same; only the height is measured from a different ground. Nothing in the course flies there; the point is that a model's constants change the numbers it prints, and a careful reader checks which constants were used.

Part 2

A trajectory from scratch

The circle solves the equation of motion for one special starting speed. Suppose we did not know that, or the start were not circular, or the force had extra terms that no formula solves. Then we would do what every orbit-propagation program does: start from a position and a velocity and let the computer advance them a small time step h at a time. This is numerical integration, and it is worth doing once by hand because it shows what can go wrong.

The equation as a first-order system. Integration methods advance a state whose rate of change is known. The acceleration involves a second derivative, so we carry the velocity \mathbf v = \dot{\mathbf r} as part of the state:

\dot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf v, \qquad \dot{\mathbf v} = \mathbf a(\mathbf r) = -\mu\,\frac{\mathbf r}{|\mathbf r|^3}.

The state (\mathbf r, \mathbf v) at time t determines its own rate of change, so knowing it at t = 0 determines the whole future. In the hero demo the start is \mathbf r_0 = (a, 0) and \mathbf v_0 = (0, k\,v_c): 550 km up, moving perpendicular to the radius at k times the circular speed. The motion stays in the plane of \mathbf r_0 and \mathbf v_0, because the force always points along \mathbf r, so two coordinates are enough.

Method 1: explicit Euler

The simplest idea: over a short step, pretend the rates of change stay what they are at the start of the step.

\mathbf r_{n+1} = \mathbf r_n + h\,\mathbf v_n, \qquad \mathbf v_{n+1} = \mathbf v_n + h\,\mathbf a(\mathbf r_n).

Its error per step is of order h^2 (the first neglected term of the Taylor series), and over a fixed time span, 1/h steps, the error is of order h. That makes it a first-order method. On an orbit it has a systematic flaw that the order alone does not reveal. Take the first step of the circular start, where \mathbf r_0 \perp \mathbf v_0 and \mathbf a_0 \perp \mathbf v_0. Pythagoras gives

|\mathbf r_1|^2 = |\mathbf r_0|^2 + h^2 |\mathbf v_0|^2, \qquad |\mathbf v_1|^2 = |\mathbf v_0|^2 + h^2 |\mathbf a_0|^2 .

Both the radius and the speed grow, whatever the sign of h. Each step moves the satellite along the tangent, and the tangent leaves the circle. With h = 60 s the satellite sweeps nh = 0.06579 rad per step, the radius grows by the factor \sqrt{1 + (nh)^2} = 1.002162, and the first step alone lifts the satellite by 14.96 km. The next steps do not start perpendicular any more, but the push stays outward. Energy is added at every step, and the orbit spirals out.

Method 2: symplectic Euler

Change one thing: update the velocity first, then move with the new velocity.

\mathbf v_{n+1} = \mathbf v_n + h\,\mathbf a(\mathbf r_n), \qquad \mathbf r_{n+1} = \mathbf r_n + h\,\mathbf v_{n+1}.

It costs the same, one evaluation of gravity per step, and it is still first-order. But it has a structural property that explicit Euler lacks: it is symplectic. For mechanics without friction this means that the map from one state to the next preserves areas in the space of positions and velocities, as the true motion does. The consequence, proved in the theory of geometric integration [4], is that symplectic Euler very nearly conserves a slightly modified energy (up to errors that stay negligible for exponentially long times), one that differs from the true energy by a term of order h. The true energy therefore cannot drift: it oscillates within a band of width proportional to h in general, for very long times. (On a nearly circular orbit the order-h term, which is proportional to \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf a, almost vanishes because the velocity is nearly perpendicular to gravity, so here the band shrinks like h^2: 4.3 \times 10^{-3} at 60 s, 1.1 \times 10^{-3} at 30 s.) The orbit is slightly misshapen and the satellite slowly gets ahead of or behind its true position, but it does not spiral away. This "kick, then drift" scheme, and its symmetric cousin leapfrog (Störmer–Verlet), are what long simulations of planets and molecules use.

Method 3: the classical Runge–Kutta method (RK4)

Instead of trusting the slope at the start of the step, sample it four times inside the step and average:

k_1 = f(y_n),\;\; k_2 = f(y_n + \tfrac h2 k_1),\;\; k_3 = f(y_n + \tfrac h2 k_2),\;\; k_4 = f(y_n + h k_3),\;\; y_{n+1} = y_n + \tfrac h6 (k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4)

Here y = (\mathbf r, \mathbf v) and f(y) = (\mathbf v, \mathbf a(\mathbf r)). The weights are chosen so that the step agrees with the Taylor series of the true solution up to the h^4 term: the error per step is of order h^5, the error over a fixed span of order h^4. Halve the step and the error falls about sixteen-fold. It costs four evaluations of gravity per step, and it is not symplectic: its energy error is tiny but drifts steadily in one direction.

What is a numerical integrator?

In plain words: a recipe that turns "here is the state now, and here is how fast it changes" into "here is the state a moment later", applied over and over to build a whole trajectory.

More precisely: a map y_{n+1} = \Phi_h(y_n) approximating the exact flow of \dot y = f(y) over a time h. A method has order p when its error over a fixed time span shrinks like h^p. Order says how fast the error shrinks; structure (symplectic or not) says what kind of error survives over long times.

Step h
The time advanced per update. Smaller is more accurate and costs more steps.
Local, global error
The error made in one step; the error accumulated at the end of a run. For order p: h^{p+1} and h^p.
Specific energy
E = \tfrac12|\mathbf v|^2 - \mu/|\mathbf r|, kinetic plus potential energy per kilogram. The exact motion conserves it, so its change measures the integrator's error without knowing the true solution. On the 550 km circle, E = -\mu/2a = -28.796 MJ/kg.

Here are the three methods as code/ch01_numbers.py writes them in NumPy; the page's script runs the same updates in JavaScript.

def accel(r):
    """Newton's gravity per unit mass: r'' = -mu r / |r|^3."""
    return -MU_EARTH * r / np.linalg.norm(r) ** 3


def step_euler(r, v, h):
    """Explicit Euler: both updates use the state at the start of the step."""
    return r + h * v, v + h * accel(r)


def step_symplectic(r, v, h):
    """Symplectic (semi-implicit) Euler: kick the velocity with the old position, then drift with the new velocity."""
    v = v + h * accel(r)
    return r + h * v, v


def step_rk4(r, v, h):
    """Classical fourth-order Runge-Kutta on the state (r, v)."""
    k1r, k1v = v, accel(r)
    k2r, k2v = v + h / 2 * k1v, accel(r + h / 2 * k1r)
    k3r, k3v = v + h / 2 * k2v, accel(r + h / 2 * k2r)
    k4r, k4v = v + h * k3v, accel(r + h * k3r)
    return r + h / 6 * (k1r + 2 * k2r + 2 * k3r + k4r), v + h / 6 * (k1v + 2 * k2v + 2 * k3v + k4v)

The two Euler methods differ by one character's worth of logic: whether the position update uses the old v or the new one. The script runs each method for a whole number of steps, \lceil 5T/h \rceil for five revolutions, and compares the end state with the exact position at the same time.

Part 3

What the three methods do

The hero demo starts on the default: a circular start, a 60 s step and five revolutions. A step of 60 s is 95.5 steps per revolution, each sweeping 3.77° of the orbit; five revolutions take 478 steps. That sounds fine-grained. Here is what each method makes of it.

Five revolutions at h = 60 s, from code/ch01_numbers.py; the hero demo shows the same numbers.

  • Explicit Euler's energy rises by 57.8 % of its starting magnitude |E_0| (relative error +5.8 \times 10^{-1}). Its spiral reaches 17,970 km from the Earth's centre, and it ends 8,383 km from where the satellite should be. Already after one revolution its energy error is 35.8 % and its radius has grown by 5,191 km.
  • Symplectic Euler keeps the energy error inside a band of 4.3 \times 10^{-3}, and returns to almost the starting energy after each revolution (9.9 \times 10^{-7} at the end). Its orbit is not a circle: the radius wanders between 6,708.2 and 7,163.8 km, up to 243 km off. And its satellite runs 666 km away from the true position along the orbit.
  • RK4's energy falls by 1.08 \times 10^{-6} of |E_0| (it drifts inwards, by 7 m in radius) and ends 0.269 km from the exact position, after 1,912 evaluations of gravity.

Explicit Euler spirals out. The Pythagoras argument of Part 2 applies at every step: the method always steps along the tangent and the tangent always leaves the orbit. Making the step smaller slows the spiral but does not remove it. At h = 1 s, 28,651 steps for five revolutions, the energy error still reaches 6.1 %. For a method whose error falls only like h and whose errors all push the same way, an orbit is the worst case. Try the "5 % slower" button: the exact orbit dips below the surface and RK4 crashes after 21 minutes, but explicit Euler's energy gain keeps it flying. A wrong integrator can be wrong in the reassuring direction.

Symplectic Euler stays bounded. Set the revolutions to 20: the energy band does not widen. The script runs 50 revolutions and finds the same largest error, 4.333 \times 10^{-3}, as after five. What does accumulate is the timing error: the satellite slowly gets ahead of or behind the true one, 6,407 km apart after 50 revolutions at 60 s. Bounded energy means the orbit keeps its size and shape on average, not that the satellite is where it should be.

RK4 is accurate but drifts. Its energy error after 50 revolutions, -1.077 \times 10^{-5}, is ten times the five-revolution value: the drift is linear in time. It is small because RK4 is fourth-order. At h = 10 s it ends five revolutions 9.5 cm from the exact position, at a cost of 11,464 evaluations.

Order shows when the step changes. The widget below repeats the run for nine step sizes and plots the final error on log scales, where a method of order p becomes a line of slope p. Halving the step from 60 s to 30 s divides symplectic Euler's position error by 4.00, from 666.5 to 166.6 km. Its position error is dominated by the drift along the orbit, which falls as h^2 here. The same halving divides RK4's error by 23.8, from 0.269 km to 11.3 m, a little more than the sixteen-fold asymptotic rate.

Equal cost. RK4 needs four evaluations of gravity per step, so a fair comparison gives the Euler methods four times as many steps. With about 1,912 evaluations each over five revolutions (1,911 for the Euler methods), RK4 at 60 s ends 0.269 km from the truth, symplectic Euler at 15 s 41.7 km, explicit Euler at 15 s 14,635 km. For short, accurate runs higher order wins. For very long runs where only the orbit's shape matters, as in simulating the solar system over millions of years, symplectic methods win, usually in higher-order versions.

An integrator has two kinds of quality: how fast its error shrinks with the step (order), and what kind of error it makes (structure). Explicit Euler is poor at both on an orbit. Symplectic Euler is low-order but keeps the energy bounded. RK4 is accurate but lets the energy drift. Before trusting a simulated trajectory, check a quantity the true motion conserves.

Part 4

The closed form, and why tools still integrate

The circle needs no integration at all. Its position at time t is the starting angle plus nt, placed on a circle of radius a. Here is the package's whole propagator, CircularOrbit.position_eci:

    def position_eci(self, t) -> np.ndarray:
        """Inertial position (..., 3) in metres at times t (seconds)."""
        t = np.asarray(t, dtype=float)
        a = self.semi_major_axis
        u = np.radians(self.phase_deg) + 2.0 * np.pi * t / self.period_s
        inc = np.radians(self.inclination_deg)
        raan = np.radians(self.raan_deg)
        cu, su = np.cos(u), np.sin(u)
        x = a * (np.cos(raan) * cu - np.sin(raan) * su * np.cos(inc))
        y = a * (np.sin(raan) * cu + np.cos(raan) * su * np.cos(inc))
        z = a * (su * np.sin(inc))
        return np.stack([x, y, z], axis=-1)

The line for u is the whole dynamics: the angle along the orbit grows at the constant rate 2\pi/T. The last three lines tilt the circle into space, which chapter 02 derives term by term. With zero inclination and zero right ascension they reduce to (a\cos u, a\sin u, 0), the circle of the hero demo. Within the two-body model it is exact: position_eci(5 * period_s) returns (6921.000, 0.000, 0.000) km, the starting point, to rounding.

It is also cheap, and the planner needs that. Chapter 04 checks visibility for every target on a 10-second grid: for the course's small instance that is 2,161 instants over six hours, for four satellites, against 120 targets and four ground stations. With the closed form each satellite position is a cosine and a sine, computed for all instants at once with NumPy, and any instant can be computed without the ones before it. An integrator would have to march through every step in order, and its error would have to be watched.

Why the package may skip integration. For the two-body model with a circular start, the closed form is the exact solution, so integrating could only add error and cost. The integrators in this chapter reach the same circle, RK4 to within 0.269 km after five revolutions at 60 s. The planning problem of chapters 05 to 10 only needs the geometry to be realistic in its pass lengths and revisit patterns, not to match any real satellite.

Not every orbit is a circle

Give the satellite a little more or less than the circular speed and the two-body solution becomes an ellipse. Starting perpendicular to the radius, the start is the lowest point (perigee) if it is faster than circular, the highest (apogee) if slower. The hero demo then compares the integrators with the exact ellipse, computed from Kepler's equation; the script prints its numbers.

  • 5 % faster (7,968 m/s): an ellipse from 550 km up to 2,131 km, eccentricity 0.1025, period 112.3 min. RK4 at 60 s ends five of these revolutions 0.192 km from the exact position.
  • 2 % slower: the lowest point is 23 km up, deep in the atmosphere. Below k = \sqrt{2R_E/(a + R_E)} = 0.9791, 159 m/s slower than circular, the ellipse dips below the surface.
  • 5 % slower: the ellipse would reach 680 km below the surface; RK4 shows the satellite hitting the ground after 21 minutes.
  • Escape: at \sqrt 2\,v_c = 10{,}732 m/s the ellipse opens into a parabola and the satellite leaves for good. The hero's slider stops at 1.2.

The course uses circles only. Real satellites keep slightly elliptical orbits, which the full set of six orbital elements describes [1]; at 550 km a nearly circular orbit is the usual choice, and a circle keeps the geometry of the following chapters simple.

Why a flight-dynamics tool still integrates

The two-body problem is a model. The real Earth is not a sphere: it bulges at the equator, and the term that describes this bulge in its gravity field, called J_2, turns the plane of a low orbit slowly around the Earth's axis. Chapter 03 quantifies that effect for the course's orbit and explains why the package's 97.6° inclination is the "sun-synchronous" one; the package itself ignores J_2. At 550 km the thin upper atmosphere also drags on the satellite and slowly lowers its orbit, by an amount that depends on the satellite's shape and on solar activity. The Moon, the Sun and the pressure of sunlight add smaller forces. None of these has a closed-form solution in general. A real tool writes the acceleration as two-body gravity plus all of these terms and integrates, with high-order methods, adaptive steps and the energy-style checks of Part 3 [3]. The skills of Part 2 are what those tools are made of; the package's closed form is what remains when every force but central gravity is dropped.

Part 5

Check your understanding

1. Why does the satellite's mass not appear in its orbit?

Gravity's force is proportional to the satellite's mass, and so is the force needed to accelerate it (Newton's second law). The mass cancels, leaving \ddot{\mathbf r} = -\mu\mathbf r/|\mathbf r|^3, which depends only on the Earth through \mu = GM.

2. Derive the circular speed at 550 km, and say why the period is not simply proportional to the radius.

Setting the centripetal acceleration v^2/a equal to gravity \mu/a^2 gives v = \sqrt{\mu/a} = 7589.0 m/s for a = 6921.0 km. The period is circumference over speed, 2\pi a/v \propto a \cdot a^{1/2} = a^{3/2}: a higher orbit is both longer and slower.

3. How many times a day does a satellite at 550 km go round, and why does the answer change slightly if "day" means a sidereal day?

86,400 s / 5730.1 s = 15.08 revolutions per solar day. A sidereal day, the Earth's rotation period relative to the stars, is 86,164.1 s, about four minutes shorter, so slightly fewer revolutions fit into it.

4. Why does the geostationary altitude come out as 35,793 km with the package's constants, not the textbook 35,786 km?

The radius of the orbit, 42,164.2 km, is fixed by \mu and the sidereal day. The altitude is that radius minus the Earth's radius: the package subtracts its mean radius, 6,371 km, textbooks subtract the equatorial radius, 6,378.137 km. The 7.1 km difference is in the ground, not in the orbit.

5. Why does explicit Euler always gain energy on a circular orbit, however small the step?

Each step moves along the tangent at the start of the step, and the tangent leaves the circle: on the first step |\mathbf r_1|^2 = |\mathbf r_0|^2 + h^2|\mathbf v_0|^2 and |\mathbf v_1|^2 = |\mathbf v_0|^2 + h^2|\mathbf a_0|^2. Both radius and speed grow, so the energy grows, step after step. A smaller step makes each push smaller (an energy error of 6.1 % after five revolutions at 1 s, against 57.8 % at 60 s) but never reverses it.

6. Symplectic Euler's energy error is bounded. Does that mean it predicts where the satellite is?

No. The bound keeps the orbit's size and shape close to right on average, but the satellite's position along the orbit drifts: 666 km off after five revolutions at 60 s, 6,407 km after fifty. Energy is a necessary check, not a sufficient one.

7. RK4 makes four gravity evaluations per step. When is it still the better choice than a cheaper method?

When accuracy over a limited time matters. At equal cost (about 1,912 evaluations over five revolutions), RK4 at 60 s ends 0.269 km from the truth against 41.7 km for symplectic Euler at 15 s. Over very long runs its steady energy drift accumulates, and symplectic methods become preferable.

8. If the closed form is exact and free, why would anyone integrate?

Because it is exact only for the two-body model with a circular orbit. The Earth's bulge (J_2), atmospheric drag, the Moon and Sun and radiation pressure add accelerations that have no closed-form solution in general, so real tools integrate the full equation of motion numerically. The package deliberately keeps only central gravity.

Part 6

Exercises

  1. Circular orbits elsewhere. Using only v = \sqrt{\mu/a} and T = 2\pi\sqrt{a^3/\mu}, find the altitude at which a circular orbit has a period of exactly 90 minutes with the package's constants. Then find how much faster than at 550 km the satellite must fly.
    Hint

    Invert Kepler's law: a = (\mu T^2 / 4\pi^2)^{1/3}, then subtract R_E.

    Solution 
    import numpy as np
MU, R = 3.986004418e14, 6_371_000.0
T = 90 * 60
a = (MU * T**2 / (4 * np.pi**2)) ** (1 / 3)
print((a - R) / 1e3, np.sqrt(MU / a) - np.sqrt(MU / (R + 550e3)))

    The altitude is about 282 km and the speed 7,741 m/s, 152 m/s faster than at 550 km. A 90-minute orbit is low enough that drag would bring it down quickly, which is one reason the course's satellites fly higher.

  2. Write the integrators yourself. Implement the three steppers of Part 2 in NumPy (do not copy them), run five revolutions at h = 60 s from \mathbf r_0 = (a, 0), \mathbf v_0 = (0, v_c), and reproduce the page's three relative energy errors.
    Hint

    Use \lceil 5T/h \rceil = 478 steps and compare E = \tfrac12|\mathbf v|^2 - \mu/|\mathbf r| with its starting value -\mu/2a. For symplectic Euler, track the largest error along the way, not only the final one.

    Solution

    You should get +0.578 for explicit Euler, a largest error of 4.33 \times 10^{-3} for symplectic Euler (and 9.9 \times 10^{-7} at the very end, because the oscillation is back near its start after a whole number of revolutions), and -1.08 \times 10^{-6} for RK4. code/ch01_numbers.py in the course archive is a reference implementation. If your symplectic Euler gains energy steadily, you are probably using the old velocity in the position update, which turns it back into explicit Euler.

  3. Leapfrog. The Störmer–Verlet (leapfrog) method takes half a kick, a full drift, and another half kick: \mathbf v_{n+1/2} = \mathbf v_n + \tfrac h2 \mathbf a(\mathbf r_n), \mathbf r_{n+1} = \mathbf r_n + h\mathbf v_{n+1/2}, \mathbf v_{n+1} = \mathbf v_{n+1/2} + \tfrac h2 \mathbf a(\mathbf r_{n+1}). Add it to your code. What is its order, and is its energy error bounded?
    Hint

    Run it at 60 s and 30 s and take the ratio of the final position errors, as Part 3 does. The last acceleration of one step is the first of the next, so it still costs one gravity evaluation per step.

    Solution

    Leapfrog is second-order: at 60 s it ends five revolutions 313 km from the truth, at 30 s 78.4 km, a factor of four. It is symplectic, so its energy error stays in a band, and the band is a thousand times narrower than symplectic Euler's (4.65 \times 10^{-6} against 4.33 \times 10^{-3} at 60 s, unchanged after 50 revolutions) because the method is symmetric in time. For the same single evaluation per step it is strictly better than symplectic Euler, which is why it is the workhorse of molecular dynamics and N-body codes [4].

  4. A step that adapts. On the 5 %-faster ellipse, the satellite moves fastest at perigee. Make RK4 choose its step so that each step sweeps the same angle, h = \theta\,|\mathbf r|^2/|\mathbf r \times \mathbf v| with \theta = 2\pi/100, and compare its accuracy with a fixed step of the same average length.
    Hint

    |\mathbf r \times \mathbf v|/|\mathbf r|^2 is the angular rate of the radius vector; dividing a fixed angle by it gives a time. Count the steps of one revolution to choose the fixed step for a fair comparison.

    Solution

    The adaptive version takes short steps near perigee, where the satellite is fastest, and longer steps near apogee. On this mildly eccentric orbit (eccentricity 0.1025) it does not pay: with 100 steps for one revolution either way, the equal-angle steps end 31.0 m from the exact position and the fixed 67.4 s step 28.3 m. Push the start to 20 % faster (eccentricity 0.44) and the picture reverses: 212 m for equal angles against 2,425 m for a fixed step, eleven times better. Adapting the step pays when the speed along the orbit varies a lot. Production integrators control the step from an error estimate instead (embedded Runge–Kutta pairs such as Dormand–Prince), which is what SciPy's solve_ivp does by default with RK45.

Part 7

The chapter on one page

  1. Gravity: \ddot{\mathbf r} = -\mu\mathbf r/|\mathbf r|^3 with \mu = GM = 3.986004418 \times 10^{14} m³/s²; the satellite's mass cancels.
  2. The circle: v^2/a = \mu/a^2 gives v = \sqrt{\mu/a} and T = 2\pi\sqrt{a^3/\mu} (Kepler's third law). At 550 km: 7589.0 m/s, 5730.1 s = 95.50 min, 15.08 revolutions a day.
  3. Altitude: higher orbits are slower and longer. Geostationary is 42,164.2 km from the centre, 35,793 km above the package's mean-radius Earth, 35,786 km above the equatorial radius.
  4. Integration: write the motion as \dot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf v, \dot{\mathbf v} = \mathbf a(\mathbf r) and step it. At 60 s for five revolutions: explicit Euler's energy error reaches 57.8 % and it spirals out; symplectic Euler keeps the error within 4.3 \times 10^{-3} but drifts 666 km along the orbit; RK4's error is -1.08 \times 10^{-6} and it ends 0.269 km from the truth.
  5. Order and structure: halving the step divides the error by 4.00 (symplectic Euler) and 23.8 (RK4); symplectic methods bound the energy, non-symplectic ones let it drift.
  6. The package: CircularOrbit.position_eci is the exact closed form for the two-body circle, one cosine and one sine per instant. Real tools integrate because J_2, drag and other forces have no closed form; chapter 03 quantifies J_2.

Part 8

Scope and further reading

Scope. The chapter stays in one plane, with one attracting body and circular or elliptical orbits started perpendicular to the radius. It does not treat the six classical orbital elements, Kepler's equation beyond its use in the demo, hyperbolic trajectories, or orbit determination from measurements. It names the perturbations real tools model (J_2, drag, third bodies, radiation pressure) without modelling them; chapter 03 quantifies the effect of J_2 on the course's orbit. The integrators are fixed-step; adaptive step control appears only in exercise 4.

Rolling horizons and reading a benchmark

On the large instance, CP-SAT given the whole twelve-hour horizon gains only one or two percent over greedy in two minutes. Cutting the horizon into one-hour blocks and solving them one after the other does better, in less time. This last chapter has two concepts. The first is decomposition: the repository's rolling.py line by line, and what it costs, measured, at the borders between blocks. The second is experimental method: what a gap against a bound means, why every method gets the same time limit but not the same threads, how much a second run of the same solver differs from the first, and how a sweep tells which constraint binds. It ends the course with its limitations, four capstone exercises and a recap of the ten chapters.

Part 1

Why decompose

Chapter 08's CP-SAT model proves the optimum of the small instance in under a second. On the large one (12 satellites, 600 targets, 12 hours, 4,826 opportunities and 145,039 conflict pairs on seed 0) the same model, given 120 s, reaches 1072 against greedy's 1060, and its bound stays at 1138. The search is not stuck because the problem is impossible; it is stuck because the model is large, and an exact search on a large model spends its time everywhere at once.

Most of that size is unnecessary for most decisions. A task at 01:00 and a task at 11:00 on the same satellite never interact through reconfiguration: chapter 06's sweep does not even create a pair for them. They interact only through the budgets, and only when they fall in the same revolution or the same memory segment. The idea of a rolling horizon is to exploit this locality: solve the first hour, freeze its decisions, then solve the second hour knowing what the first one did, and so on.

What is a rolling-horizon decomposition?

In plain words: cut the time line into blocks, plan each block in turn with an exact solver, and keep what earlier blocks decided as fixed facts for the later ones.

More precisely: the opportunities are partitioned into blocks B_1, \dots, B_K by the start of their window. For k = 1, \dots, K, the subproblem on B_k is solved with every decision of B_1 \cup \dots \cup B_{k-1} fixed: their targets are no longer available, their budget use is subtracted from the limits, and their tasks occupy their satellites. The union of the block plans is feasible for the whole problem by construction. It is a heuristic: it gives no bound, and its plan can be worse than the optimum because each block ignores what comes after it.

The repository's numbers (results.md, cited). On the two large instances, rolling CP-SAT with one-hour blocks reaches 1088 and 1079 in 76.6 and 57.0 s, against 1072 and 1066 for the whole-horizon CP-SAT in 122 s and 1060 and 1047 for greedy: the best plans of the benchmark. On medium the order is reversed: the whole horizon gives 557, 590 and 534 in 60 s, the rolling horizon 540, 570 and 522 in 1.4 to 3.6 s. On small it loses 1 to 4 points against the proven optimum, in under 0.4 s.

Part 2

rolling.py line by line

The outer loop. solve_rolling makes the blocks, solves them in time order and keeps two pieces of state, the plan so far and the budget used so far:

def solve_rolling(inst: PlanningInstance, block_s: int = 3600, time_limit_s: float = 120.0,
                  workers: int = 8, seed: int = 0) -> dict:
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    opps = inst.opportunities
    n_blocks = -(-inst.horizon_s // block_s)
    blocks = [[] for _ in range(n_blocks)]
    for o in opps:
        blocks[min(o.window_start // block_s, n_blocks - 1)].append(o.idx)
    plan: dict[int, float] = {}
    used: dict[tuple, int] = {}
    for ids in blocks:
        chosen = _solve_block(inst, ids, plan, used, time_limit_s / n_blocks, workers, seed)
        for i, t in chosen.items():
            plan[i] = t
            o = opps[i]
            for key in (("duty", o.sat, o.revolution), ("memory", o.sat, o.memory_segment)):
                used[key] = used.get(key, 0) + o.duration
  • -(-a // b) is a ceiling division in integers: a 6-hour horizon in 3600-second blocks gives 6 blocks, a 12-hour one 12.
  • An opportunity belongs to the block in which its window starts; a window that crosses a border stays in the earlier block, and the min keeps the last window inside the last block.
  • After each block, every chosen task adds its duration to two counters: the power budget of its revolution and the memory of its segment, the two keys of chapter 05.
  • The time limit is split evenly, time_limit_s / n_blocks per block. A block that is proved optimal before its share runs out simply stops, and the unused time is not passed on: that is why the benchmark's rolling runs often end far below the limit.

One block. _solve_block builds the CP-SAT model of chapter 08 restricted to the block, with three additions for what is already fixed. First, targets already collected are removed, which enforces "each target at most once" across blocks:

    opps = inst.opportunities
    taken = {opps[i].target for i in fixed}
    ids = [i for i in ids if opps[i].target not in taken]
    if not ids:
        return {}

Then come the variables, AtMostOne per target, NoOverlap per satellite and the pairwise precedences, exactly as in cpsat.py but only for pairs whose two opportunities are both in the block. Second, the budgets are tightened by what earlier blocks used:

    for kind, limit, group in inst.budget_groups():
        sub = [i for i in group if i in idset]
        if not sub:
            continue
        key = (kind, opps[sub[0]].sat, opps[sub[0]].revolution if kind == "duty" else opps[sub[0]].memory_segment)
        m.add(sum(opps[i].duration * x[i] for i in sub) <= limit - used.get(key, 0))

A revolution of 95.5 minutes straddles one-hour borders, so its 300 s of listening are shared between two or three blocks; whatever the first block takes is gone for the next. This line is where the decomposition's myopia lives.

Third, a new task must keep the reconfiguration time with every fixed task on its satellite. Let the fixed task f start at t_f with duration d_f. A new task i can go after f if it starts at or after \text{after} = t_f + d_f + s(f, i), and before f if it starts at or before \text{before} = t_f - d_i - s(i, f). Comparing these two numbers with the window of possible starts [e_i, \ell_i] gives four cases:

    for i in ids:
        oi = opps[i]
        for f in fixed_by_sat.get(oi.sat, []):
            tf, df = int(round(fixed[f])), opps[f].duration  # block plans have integer starts
            after = tf + df + inst.transition(f, i)  # earliest start of i if it comes after f
            before = tf - oi.duration - inst.transition(i, f)  # latest start of i if before f
            after_ok = after <= oi.latest_start
            before_ok = before >= oi.earliest_start
            if after <= oi.earliest_start or before >= oi.latest_start:
                continue  # no interference whatever the start
            if not after_ok and not before_ok:
                m.add(x[i] == 0)
            elif after_ok and not before_ok:
                m.add(s[i] >= after).only_enforce_if(x[i])
            elif before_ok and not after_ok:
                m.add(s[i] <= before).only_enforce_if(x[i])
            else:
                o = m.new_bool_var("")
                m.add(s[i] >= after).only_enforce_if([x[i], o])
                m.add(s[i] <= before).only_enforce_if([x[i], o.Not()])

It is chapter 06's three-case sweep again, with one side known: no interference (the whole window is clear of f), impossible (the whole window is inside the forbidden interval), one side only, or both sides with a fresh order literal. The widget lets you move the window of i around an illustrative fixed task.

case– what rolling.py adds–

Two details are worth noticing. The first block is solved with nothing fixed, and the last with nearly everything fixed, so each block's model is smaller than the whole and its precedences only involve its own pairs and the fixed tasks. And the block models carry no hint: unlike the whole-horizon run of the benchmark, they do not start from greedy's plan. The function returns no bound ("bound": None): the optimum of each block says nothing about the optimum of the whole.

Part 3

Myopia at block borders

Each block is solved to optimality for its own objective: the priority collected inside the block. It has no reason to leave anything for later. If a revolution's power budget can be spent on a priority-4 task now or on a priority-8 task in the next hour, the block takes the 4: the 8 is not in its model. The hero widget shows the result on the small instance. With 60-minute blocks (the default view, first run), the rolling plan collects 56 points in the first hour where the optimum collects 49, the same in hours two to four, 50 against 44 in the fifth, and then 54 against 68 in the last: 218 against 219 overall. The optimum of the course is one of possibly several optimal plans, so the per-block comparison is a picture, not a proof; but the shape, ahead early and behind late, appears for every block length shorter than the horizon.

The numbers per block (small instance, first run of code/ch10_numbers.py). 30-minute blocks: 53 against 46 in the first block, 34 against 39 in the last; total 213. 120-minute blocks: 76, 38, 103 against 69, 38, 112; total 217. 180-minute blocks: 82, 137 against 75, 144; total 219, so here the second block catches up completely. A whole-horizon block finds 219 as well. Every plan passes the checker, in Python and again in your browser.

A decomposition is exact inside each piece and blind across pieces. What it loses is decided at the borders: wherever a resource (a budget, a target, satellite time) can be spent on either side, the earlier block spends it.

Two standard remedies exist, and the repository's README lists both as next steps. Overlapping blocks solve a longer window than they commit: plan hours 1 and 2, keep only hour 1, then plan hours 2 and 3. The second hour is then seen twice, once as a look-ahead. Polishing gives the rolling plan to the whole-horizon CP-SAT as a hint, so the global model starts from a good plan and repairs the borders. Capstone exercise 1 measures the second.

Part 4

The block-size trade-off

Short blocks are easy to solve and see little; long blocks see more and are harder. At one extreme a single block is the whole-horizon model; at the other, blocks of a few minutes would hold one or two opportunities each and reduce the method to a greedy in time order. The chapter's script runs blocks of 30, 60, 120 and 180 minutes and a single block on seed 0 of the small and medium instances, with the benchmark's time limits (20 s and 60 s in total, split evenly between blocks, CP-SAT with 8 workers), twice each with identical settings.

small, seed 0 medium, seed 0

Reading the sweep. On small, every block length finishes in well under a second (each block is proved optimal at once), and only the value changes: 213 and 217 for 30-minute blocks in the two runs, 218 for 60, 217 and 216 for 120, and the optimum 219 from 180 minutes up. On medium, 30-minute blocks give 535 and 543 in about a second, 120-minute blocks 549 in 8 to 13 s, and the whole horizon 555 and 558 in the full 60 s. Three lessons:

  • The trend is real, the details are noise. Longer blocks are better on average, but 180-minute blocks on medium (545 and 544) do worse than 120-minute ones (549). Their blocks are larger and slower to solve (15.4 and 19.0 s per run, against 8.2 and 12.7 s), and their borders fall at different times.
  • The same settings do not give the same plan. The two runs with 30-minute blocks differ by 4 points on small and 8 on medium, although every block is solved by the same solver with the same seed. Among several optimal plans for a block, eight threads racing each other do not always return the same one, and a different choice in block 1 changes what blocks 2 to 12 can do.
  • The best block length depends on the instance and the time. On medium in 60 s, the whole horizon wins; on large in 120 s (results.md), one-hour blocks win. A block length is a parameter to tune, against a time budget, on instances like the ones it will meet.
Part 5

Gaps and bounds

The benchmark's table reports a gap for every method on every instance. results.md defines it in one line: "Gap = (upper bound − value) / upper bound, with the best upper bound proven by either exact solver". In symbols, for a plan of value v and the smallest upper bound U proved by CP-SAT or HiGHS on that instance,

\text{gap}_U(v) = \frac{U - v}{U}.

The quantity one would like is the true gap against the optimum v^*, (v^* - v)/v^*, but v^* is unknown except on small. What is known is a bracket. The optimum is at most the bound, v^* \le U, and at least the best plan anyone found, v^* \ge L. The true gap is therefore between two computable numbers:

\frac{L - v}{L} \;\le\; \frac{v^* - v}{v^*} \;\le\; \frac{U - v}{U}.

On small, L = U = the optimum and the bracket closes. On medium seed 0, the benchmark's U = 575 and its best plan is CP-SAT's 557, so CP-SAT's gap of 3.1% is an upper estimate: the true gap is anywhere between 0% and 3.1%. This chapter's script narrows the bracket with more runs on the same instance: its best plan is 560 (CP-SAT hinted with a rolling plan, capstone exercise 1) and no run proved a bound below 575, so the optimum lies in [560, 575], and the benchmark's 557 is between 0.5% and 3.1% below it.

The table is results.md's, values with their gap against the best bound underneath. Two remarks change how it reads. First, on one instance every method is measured against the same U, so the bound's looseness shifts all gaps on that row by roughly the same amount: comparisons within a row are fair, absolute gaps on medium and large are pessimistic. Second, a gap of 0% against the best known plan means "nobody found better", not "optimal": the rolling horizon on large is 0% against the best plan and 4.4% against the bound, and nothing in the benchmark says where in between it is. On the large instances the bounds come from the whole-horizon models (the rolling horizon proves nothing), and chapter 09 showed that they stay close to the root LP relaxation, which is blind to reconfiguration: the true gaps there may be much smaller than 4–6%.

Part 6

Same limits, unequal threads, and noise

Same time limit for all. The benchmark gives every method the same wall-clock limit per size: 20 s on small, 60 s on medium, 120 s on large. That is the right comparison for a planner who has a fixed amount of time before the plan must be uploaded. It is not a comparison of algorithms in the abstract: a different limit can change the ranking, as the medium instance shows (the rolling horizon is far ahead at 3 s, the whole horizon ahead at 60 s).

Unequal threads. CP-SAT ran with 8 workers on a 14-thread machine, HiGHS through SciPy on one. The README and results.md say so, which is the minimum a benchmark owes its reader. A careful reader then asks whether the conclusion survives the difference. For "CP-SAT beats the MILP" it does (chapter 09: 10 to 17 times faster on small at equal results, and on large the MILP is below greedy). For a close result it would not: a 2% difference between a 1-thread and an 8-thread run says nothing about the algorithms.

Run-to-run noise. CP-SAT's eight workers run different strategies in parallel and exchange plans and bounds as they go. Which worker finds what first depends on how the operating system schedules the threads, so two runs with the same seed and the same limit can stop with different plans. results.md warns that "CP-SAT is multi-threaded, so values can change slightly between runs". The chapter's script measures how much: five runs of the benchmark's CP-SAT configuration (greedy hint, 8 workers) on medium seed 0 with a 30-second limit, random seeds 0 to 4.

Measured. The five runs gave 549, 550, 545, 547 and 550: a spread of 5 points, mean 548.2, every one with the bound 575. The benchmark's memory sweep ran exactly this configuration once (memory 600 s, 30 s, seed 0) and recorded 549; this script's seed-0 run gave 549 too, this time, while seeds 1 to 4 gave values from 545 to 550. Running the script again gives other numbers: that is the point. The rolling horizon is not immune either: the two runs of Part 4 with 30-minute blocks differ by 4 points on small and 8 on medium.

One more caution about this measurement: the script's runs were made on a laptop that may have been busy with other work, and a busy machine lets a 30-second run explore less. The level of the five values (545 to 550) is therefore not comparable with the benchmark's 549 to 554; their spread is the number to carry away.

A single run is one draw from this distribution. A difference between two methods smaller than the spread, such as 553 against 549, cannot be attributed to the methods without repeated runs; a difference much larger than it, such as greedy's 518 against CP-SAT's 557, can. The honest report of a multi-threaded solver stopped at a time limit gives several runs, or at least the spread of a few. The benchmark gives one run per instance, which is enough for its large differences and not for its small ones.

Part 7

Which constraint binds

A constraint binds when relaxing it would improve the optimum. The memory budget of chapter 05, 600 s of recording between two ground contacts, is a design choice; the question an engineer asks is whether a bigger memory would buy more collection. The benchmark answers it with a sweep: the medium instance, seed 0, solved by greedy and by CP-SAT (30 s, greedy hint) for memories of 200, 300, 450, 600 and 900 s and for practically no limit (100,000 s).

CP-SAT, 30 s greedy CP-SAT bound measured spread, 5 runs at 600 s

Read the bound first, because it moves only for a reason. At 200 s it equals the value, 489, and CP-SAT proves it optimal: with so little memory the problem is small. At 300 s the bound is 566, still below the unconstrained 575, so memory still limits what any plan can collect. From 450 s on, the bound is 575 whatever the memory, and greedy is stuck at 516 to 518. The values 553, 549, 554 and 554 differ by at most 5 points, the size of the spread measured in Part 6, so they cannot be told apart. The conclusion of the README follows: at 300 s or less memory binds; above 450 s it does not, and what limits collection is the reconfiguration time and the power budget. As far as these runs can tell, a larger onboard memory than 600 s would buy nothing on this instance (the bound does not prove it, since the optimum may sit below 575 differently for each budget); a faster slew or a larger power budget might, and the same sweep would say how much.

Note the method: the claim rests on the bound, which is a proof, and on the measured noise, which says how much a change in value means. Values alone (549 at 600 s, 554 at 900 s) would suggest that more memory helps by 5 points, which is noise.

Part 8

Limitations, and the course in ten concepts

What the model leaves out. The README is explicit, and the course has quantified most of it along the way:

  • a synthetic, simplified scenario: circular orbits, a spherical Earth, no J2 (chapter 03 measures the effect), generic ocean areas, illustrative ground stations and budgets; it makes no claim about any real system;
  • reconfiguration times computed from the pointing directions at window midpoints, fixed per pair; budget segments assigned from window midpoints; each ground contact assumed long enough to empty the memory (chapter 05);
  • a static plan over a fixed horizon: no new requests, no replanning, no uncertainty on detections or on whether an emitter is active;
  • on medium and large instances the optimum is unknown and every gap is an upper estimate (Part 5).

Part 9

Check your understanding

1. Why is the union of the block plans always feasible for the whole problem?

Each block removes the targets already collected, subtracts the budgets already used, and forces its tasks to keep the reconfiguration times with every fixed task on the same satellite. So every constraint of the whole problem is respected between a new task and everything fixed before it, and inside the block by the block model.

2. Why does the rolling horizon give no bound?

Each block's optimum is conditional on what earlier blocks fixed. A different early choice could allow a better total, and nothing in the block models measures how much. The sum of block optima is the value of one plan, not a limit on all plans.

3. Where, in the code, does the myopia come from?

From the objective of each block (only its own priority) together with limit - used.get(key, 0): a block may spend a revolution's or a segment's budget, or a target, that a later block would have used better, because it does not see the later block.

4. A new task's window is [e_i, \ell_i] = [520, 600], and \text{after} = 700, \text{before} = 515. Which case?

\text{after} \le \ell_i is false and \text{before} \ge e_i is false, and neither "no interference" test holds, so the task cannot be selected: m.add(x[i] == 0).

5. CP-SAT's medium seed 0 gap is 3.1%. What is the most you can say about its true gap?

That it lies between the gap against the best plan known and the gap against the best bound. With the benchmark alone, between 0% and 3.1%; with this chapter's extra runs, between 0.5% and 3.1%.

6. Why do two CP-SAT runs with the same seed give different plans?

Its parallel workers exchange solutions and bounds as they find them, and the timing of those exchanges depends on the operating system's thread scheduling. With a wall-clock time limit, the search also stops at a slightly different point each time.

7. The memory sweep shows 549 at 600 s and 554 at 900 s. Does more memory help?

No conclusion can be drawn from the values: the difference is smaller than the measured run-to-run spread. The bound, which is the same 575 at both, says that no plan can use the extra memory to exceed it; the evidence is that memory does not bind above 450 s.

8. Why is "same time limit for all methods" fair, and in what sense is the benchmark still unfair?

It compares what each method delivers in the time a planner has. It remains unequal in resources: CP-SAT used 8 threads and HiGHS 1. A conclusion that a factor of eight in compute could reverse should not be drawn from it.

Part 10

Capstone exercises

The four next steps of the repository's README, as projects. Each uses everything the course built; each comes with a hint and the shape of a solution rather than a single answer.

  1. Seed CP-SAT with the rolling plan. Run solve_rolling with one-hour blocks, pass its plan as hint to solve_cpsat on the whole horizon, and compare with the greedy hint at the same total time. Does it help on medium? On large?
    Hint

    solve_cpsat(inst, time_limit_s, hint=rolling["plan"]) accepts any feasible plan. Give the combination the same total time as the alternative, and repeat each configuration a few times: Part 6 says why.

    Solution

    The chapter's script does it once on medium seed 0 with 60 s for CP-SAT: hinted with the one-hour rolling plan (544 in that run; the two runs of Part 4 gave 539 and 540), CP-SAT reaches 560 with bound 575, against 557 for the benchmark's greedy-hinted run and 555 and 558 for the two unhinted whole-horizon runs of Part 4. Those differences are of the size of the spread of Part 6, so one run does not show an effect. The case where seeding should matter is large, where the rolling plan (1088) is far ahead of what CP-SAT reaches from greedy (1072) in 120 s: start the whole-horizon search there and it can only improve on 1088. Measure it with several runs.

  2. Large neighbourhood search by satellite or area. Instead of blocks in time, repeatedly fix the current plan everywhere except on one satellite (or one maritime area, or one satellite over a few hours) and re-solve that part with CP-SAT. Accept the result if it is better.
    Hint

    The fixed-task machinery of _solve_block already handles "everything else is fixed" for one satellite's neighbours; budgets of the freed part are its limits minus the fixed tasks' use. For an area, the coupling is through the targets, which are freed together.

    Solution

    Each re-solve is small and exact, and the plan's value can only increase, so the method is anytime. Unlike the rolling horizon it can revisit the first hour after the last, which repairs border effects. The design questions are the neighbourhood's size (small enough to solve in a second, large enough to contain an improvement) and the order of neighbourhoods; CP-SAT's own LNS workers (chapter 08) do a generic version of this, and a problem-specific neighbourhood often does better.

  3. A Lagrangian bound. The only constraint that couples satellites is "each target at most once". Move it into the objective with a multiplier \lambda_j \ge 0 per target and show that the problem then splits into one independent problem per satellite. Use it to compute a bound.
    Hint

    Replace \max \sum_o w_o x_o s.t. \sum_{o \in j} x_o \le 1 by \max \sum_o (w_o - \lambda_{j(o)}) x_o + \sum_j \lambda_j with that constraint removed. Precedences and budgets are all per satellite.

    Solution

    For any \lambda \ge 0 the relaxed optimum

    L(\lambda) = \sum_j \lambda_j + \sum_{\text{sat}} \max \big(\text{that satellite's problem with weights } w_o - \lambda_{j(o)}\big)

    is an upper bound, since every plan satisfying the target rows scores at least as much in the relaxed objective. Each satellite's problem is small and keeps the reconfiguration times, which is exactly what chapter 09's LP throws away; solve each with CP-SAT. Minimise L by subgradient steps: raise \lambda_j for targets collected by several satellites, lower it (not below 0) for targets nobody collects. The open question for the project is whether this bound beats 575 on medium.

  4. Replanning. Halfway through the horizon an urgent target appears. Insert it into the running plan, keeping everything already executed and changing as few future tasks as possible.
    Hint

    Everything before "now" is fixed: that is _solve_block's fixed argument with its carried budgets. For "as few changes as possible", add a penalty for each future task of the old plan that is dropped or moved.

    Solution

    Build the model on the opportunities after "now", with the executed tasks fixed and the budgets they used carried over, exactly as in rolling.py. Give the urgent target a large weight, and subtract from the objective a cost c for every task of the old plan whose x becomes 0 (and optionally for moved starts, with |s - s_{\text{old}}| linearised by two inequalities). The weight of c against the priorities states how much stability is worth, which is an operational choice, not a mathematical one. Check the new plan with the checker before anything else.

Part 11

The chapter on one page

  1. Rolling horizon: opportunities in blocks by window start; each block solved by CP-SAT with earlier tasks fixed, their targets removed, their budget use carried, reconfiguration against fixed tasks in four cases; the time limit split evenly.
  2. Myopia: each block takes what it can; on small with one-hour blocks the rolling plan is 7 points ahead of the optimum after the first hour and 1 behind at the end (218 against 219).
  3. Block size: small 213–219, optimal from 180-minute blocks; medium 535–549 in 1 to 19 s, the whole horizon best in 60 s; on large (results.md) one-hour blocks win.
  4. Gaps: against the best proved bound, an upper estimate of the true gap; against the best known plan, a lower one. Medium seed 0's optimum is in [560, 575].
  5. Method: same limits, stated threads (8 against 1), and run-to-run noise measured: 5 points over five CP-SAT runs on medium, 8 between two rolling runs. Differences below the noise mean nothing.
  6. Which constraint binds: memory at 300 s or less (489 proved optimal at 200 s); above 450 s the bound stays at 575 and the values are noise.

WordMeaning

Part 12

Scope and further reading

Scope. The chapter measures the rolling horizon only on seed 0 of the small and medium instances, twice per block length, with the benchmark's limits; the large-instance numbers are the benchmark's, cited. It does not implement overlapping blocks, LNS or the Lagrangian bound: those are the capstones. The noise measurement is five runs at one time limit on one instance, enough to show its size, not to describe its distribution. All runs used the author's 14-thread laptop, possibly with other work running, which is part of what makes wall-clock limits noisy.

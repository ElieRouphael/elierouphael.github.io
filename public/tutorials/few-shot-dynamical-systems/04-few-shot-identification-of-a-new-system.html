Few-shot identification of a new system

Chapter 03 packaged twenty long recordings into a two-dimensional family: an average system, two directions of variation and a noise level. This chapter puts that backbone to work on a brand new system, seen through only a couple of seconds of data. The backbone is frozen; only two numbers are estimated, with the family's own spread as a prior and a Laplace approximation for the uncertainty. Below, the adaptation runs in your browser, against the from-scratch fit of chapter 02 and against doing nothing.

Part 0

The question

A new member of the family is drawn, and the learner gets two seconds of it: twenty samples under a random three-sine input. Chapter 02 showed what four free coefficients do with so little; this chapter asks what two numbers do when they are the right two. Everything else, the mean system \bar\eta, the directions U, the residual level \hat\sigma = 0.019, is frozen from chapter 03.

The notebook's new system. Coefficients (1.533, 0.445, 0.910, 1.509), hidden design variables c = (0.46, 0.79): a stiff, well-damped member with natural period 5.1 s, so that 2 s is 0.39 of a period. Its best representation in the family is z = (1.46, -1.99): typical along the first direction, two standard deviations out along the second.

Part 1

From four numbers to two

The likelihood. If the model were right and the residual white with variance \hat\sigma^2, the data y given a context z would have log-likelihood -\tfrac{1}{2\hat\sigma^2}\sum_t (y_t - \hat q_t(\bar\eta + Uz; u))^2 up to a constant. The prior. Pretraining scaled the context to unit variance across the family, so z \sim \mathcal N(0, I) says "a typical member". Bayes' rule multiplies the two; taking the negative logarithm and doubling gives the maximum a posteriori (MAP) objective [2]:

\hat z = \arg\min_z \ \frac{1}{\hat\sigma^2}\sum_{t=0}^{T} \big(y_t - \hat q_t(\bar\eta + Uz;\,u)\big)^2 + \lVert z \rVert^2 .

It is a nonlinear least-squares problem again, with two extra residuals z_1, z_2 appended to the scaled data residuals, which is exactly how the notebook writes it for least_squares. The page solves it by Levenberg–Marquardt with the Jacobian from the chain rule, S = \partial \hat q / \partial z = J_\eta U, where J_\eta is the sensitivity matrix of chapter 02. Two things improve at once: there are fewer unknowns, and the prior keeps them in the region where systems of this kind actually live.

The prior as a ridge. The term \lVert z \rVert^2 is a ridge penalty [4], and its weight is not a tuning knob: it is fixed by pretraining, because \hat\sigma sets the units of the data term and the unit variance of z sets the units of the prior. Replacing \lVert z \rVert^2 by \tau^2 \lVert z \rVert^2 claims the family varies \tau times less than it does; the widgets let you try it.

What is few-shot adaptation, in this course?

In plain words: keep everything the family taught you fixed and let the new system's few data points choose only where it sits inside the family.

More precisely: estimate the context z \in \mathbb R^2 of a frozen decoder \eta(z) = \bar\eta + Uz by MAP under the pretraining prior \mathcal N(0, I) and the pretraining noise level \hat\sigma, then report a Gaussian posterior from the curvature at the optimum. The machine-learning names are meta-learning with a fixed feature extractor, or in-context identification [5][6]; the control name is Bayesian identification with an empirical prior [3].

Part 2

How sure is it?

The Laplace approximation replaces the posterior by a Gaussian centred at \hat z whose precision is the curvature of the objective there [1]. With the Gauss–Newton approximation of that curvature,

\operatorname{Cov}(z \mid \text{data}) \approx \big(\hat\sigma^{-2} S^\top S + I\big)^{-1}, \qquad S = \frac{\partial \hat q}{\partial z}\Big|_{\hat z} = J_\eta U .

Read it as posterior precision = data precision + prior precision. The data term \hat\sigma^{-2} S^\top S is the Fisher information of the record about z; the identity is the prior's. Each eigenvalue of the data term counts how many "units of prior" the record is worth along that eigen-direction. For the notebook's 2 s experiment the posterior covariance is

\operatorname{Cov}(z \mid \text{data}) = \begin{pmatrix} 0.0471 & 0.0607 \\ 0.0607 & 0.7223 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{standard deviations } (0.217,\ 0.850),

whose precision has eigenvalues 24.0 and 1.37: the data are worth 23 units of prior along one direction and 0.37 along the other. Two seconds pin the first direction of the family, stiffness and hardening moving together, and say almost nothing about the second, damping against gain, which the prior fills in. The two-sigma ellipse drawn in the demos is the set (z - \hat z)^\top \operatorname{Cov}^{-1} (z - \hat z) \le 4; its axes are the eigenvectors of the covariance with half-lengths 2\sqrt{\lambda}.

Three caveats. The Gaussian is an approximation to a posterior that is not Gaussian, because \hat q is nonlinear in z; the residual is coloured, so the data precision is optimistic for the same reason as in chapter 02; and the decoder clips to the admissible box, which the Jacobian ignores. For contexts within the training cloud none of these bites hard, and chapter 07 will put the posterior to a harder test.

Part 3

The notebook's example

With only two seconds of data, a third of an oscillation period, fitting four coefficients from scratch overfits badly. It explains the short experiment, but the coefficients it finds, (1.045, 0.010, 0.645, 5.0) with the damping on its lower bound and the hardening on its upper one, describe a different, barely damped oscillator (damping ratio 0.005 against the truth's 0.18), and its predictions drift far from reality: RMSE 0.158 on the validation input, worse than the mean system's 0.056. Adapting two pretrained context values gives 0.014. For scale, the true coefficients themselves score 0.0038 (the quintic term and the bias are not in the model), and the best context the family can offer, the projection of the true coefficients, scores 0.0042: the family can represent this system almost perfectly, and 2 s got more than halfway from the mean system to that floor on a logarithmic scale.

What the prior does on this draw. The posterior ellipse contains the true system, at 1.89 posterior standard deviations, just inside the two-sigma contour. But this system lies two standard deviations out along the direction the data do not pin, and the prior pulls the estimate back towards the origin: with the prior switched off the context estimate predicts the validation input with RMSE 0.0050, better than the 0.0140 of the MAP estimate. The widget runs the notebook's exercise 1 in both directions.

A prior is a bet on the population, not on the individual. On one draw it can lose, as here; over many draws it wins, which chapters 05 shows and which is the only sense in which a prior can be right. What it must never do is tighten without evidence: a prior weight of 3 shrinks the ellipse's long axis by a factor of 2.6 and leaves the truth 6.4 posterior standard deviations outside it.

Part 4

Twenty new systems

One example proves little. The notebook's exercise 2 repeats the comparison for twenty new systems at the same 2 s budget; the page does the same with its own draws, and you can ask for twenty more.

The mean error from scratch is three times the context's in the notebook's run, and the median ratio between the two is 3.6, but the spread is wide: the context sometimes loses because its two numbers cannot express a system the family covers poorly, or because the prior pulls the wrong way, while the from-scratch fit sometimes wins because the random input happened to be informative. The next chapter turns this into a systematic study over data budgets from 1.5 to 25 s.

Part 5

Check your understanding

1. What is frozen and what is estimated when a new system arrives?

Frozen: the physics equation, \bar\eta, U, the whitening scale and \hat\sigma. Estimated: the two-number context z, by MAP, from the new system's data alone.

2. Where do the two terms of the MAP objective come from, and why is their relative weight not a free choice?

From the Gaussian likelihood with the pretraining noise level \hat\sigma and from the standard-normal pretraining prior on z. Both scales were fixed in chapter 03, so the objective \hat\sigma^{-2}\lVert r \rVert^2 + \lVert z \rVert^2 has no tuning parameter; changing the prior weight changes the claim about how much the family varies.

3. What is S, and how is it computed?

The sensitivity of the predicted output to the context, S = J_\eta U: the chapter 02 sensitivity matrix with respect to the four coefficients, multiplied by the two directions of the family. It is the Jacobian the solver uses and the matrix whose \hat\sigma^{-2} S^\top S is the data's precision about z.

4. Read the posterior covariance of the notebook's example.

Standard deviations 0.217 and 0.850 along z_1 and z_2; precision eigenvalues 24 and 1.37, of which 1 each is the prior's. Two seconds pin the first direction of the family and leave the second almost entirely to the prior.

5. Why does the from-scratch fit do worse than not fitting at all here?

Because with 20 samples it can drive the damping to its lower bound and the hardening to its upper bound and still match the record; the resulting oscillator rings for 30 s where the real one settles. Its validation RMSE of 0.158 is three times the mean system's 0.056.

6. What does a tighter prior do to the estimate and the ellipse?

It pulls the estimate towards the origin, the mean system, and shrinks the ellipse by the prior's factor, without any new data. With a weight of 3 the truth sits six posterior standard deviations outside the ellipse. Confidence has been bought, not earned.

7. On the notebook's example, does the family's own prior help?

Not on this draw: the true context lies two standard deviations out along the poorly determined direction, and the no-prior estimate scores 0.0050 against the MAP estimate's 0.0140. Over the twenty systems of Part 4 the prior-based context wins on average by a factor of three. Both facts are consistent: a prior is a bet on the population.

8. Does the context win on every draw?

No. In the notebook's own twenty-system run from scratch wins 5 of 20, twice by more than a factor of 2. The notebook's solution text says the context wins on essentially every draw; the numbers it computes say otherwise.

Part 6

Exercises

  1. A worse prior. Repeat the two-second adaptation with the prior term z replaced by 3z in the residual (a much tighter prior, as if the family were believed to vary less than it does). How do the estimate and its posterior ellipse change?
    Hint

    Remember to put 9I instead of I in the covariance formula.

    Solution

    The estimate moves from (1.33, -0.71) to (1.05, -0.19), towards the average system, and the posterior standard deviations shrink from (0.217, 0.850) to (0.171, 0.325), but for the wrong reason: the estimator is more confident without having seen more data. The truth is now 6.4 posterior standard deviations away instead of 1.9, and the validation RMSE rises from 0.0140 to 0.0240. With too little data to overrule a strong prior, the estimate is biased towards \bar\eta rather than towards the true system.

    def adapt_tight(family, y, u, tightness=3.0):
    residual = lambda z: np.r_[(predict(family.decode(z), u) - y) / family.sigma, tightness * z]
    sol = least_squares(residual, np.zeros(family.U.shape[1]))
    S = sol.jac[:-len(sol.x)] * family.sigma
    cov = np.linalg.inv(S.T @ S / family.sigma**2 + tightness**2 * np.eye(len(sol.x)))
    return sol.x, cov

z_tight, cov_tight = adapt_tight(family, y_short, u_short)
  2. Does from scratch ever catch up on this short an experiment? Repeat the comparison for 20 different new systems at the same 2 s budget and report the mean RMSE of "from scratch" and "pretrained + context". Does the ranking ever flip?
    Hint

    Keep the per-system pairs, not only the means.

    Solution

    Mean RMSE 0.0768 from scratch against 0.0252 with the context (the mean system scores 0.0607). The context wins 15 of the 20 pairs with a median ratio of 3.6, but the ranking does flip: from scratch wins 5 of 20, twice by more than a factor of 2 (0.0153 against 0.0314, and 0.0159 against 0.0392). The notebook's printed solution says the context wins "on essentially every draw" and that from scratch "never wins by much"; its own loop shows otherwise. The flips happen when the random 2 s input excites the system well and the prior pulls the context the wrong way.

    errs = {"scratch": [], "context": []}
for _ in range(20):
    s = sample_system(rng); u = multisine(20, rng); _, y = simulate(s, u, rng)
    u_val = multisine(300, rng, n=6); q_val, _ = simulate(s, u_val, rng, noise=False)
    errs["scratch"].append(rmse(predict(fit_from_scratch(y, u), u_val), q_val))
    errs["context"].append(rmse(predict(family.decode(adapt(family, y, u)[0]), u_val), q_val))

  3. Posterior precision for a linear model. Suppose \hat q were exactly linear in z, \hat q = \hat q_0 + Sz. Derive the exact posterior and show that the Laplace formula is then exact.
    Hint

    Complete the square in z in the exponent of likelihood times prior.

    Solution

    The log posterior is -\tfrac{1}{2\hat\sigma^2}\lVert y - \hat q_0 - Sz \rVert^2 - \tfrac12 \lVert z \rVert^2 plus a constant, a quadratic in z with Hessian -(\hat\sigma^{-2} S^\top S + I). A Gaussian with that precision and mean \hat z = (\hat\sigma^{-2} S^\top S + I)^{-1} \hat\sigma^{-2} S^\top (y - \hat q_0) is the exact posterior, and the Laplace approximation, which uses the same Hessian at the same mode, coincides with it. The nonlinearity of the decoder and the dynamics is the only source of approximation error.

  4. The wrong noise level. Rerun the notebook's adaptation with \hat\sigma = 0.01, the sensor noise alone, instead of the family's 0.019. What happens to the estimate and the ellipse?
    Hint

    The data term is multiplied by (0.019 / 0.01)^2 = 3.6; the prior term is unchanged.

    Solution

    Weighting the data 3.6 times more is equivalent, up to a common factor, to dividing the prior weight by 1.9, so the estimate moves towards the likelihood centre: from (1.33, -0.71) to (1.32, -1.37). On this draw that improves the prediction (validation RMSE 0.0081 instead of 0.0140), since the truth lies that way. But the ellipse shrinks, from standard deviations (0.217, 0.850) to (0.125, 0.654), because the data precision is now counted 3.6 times too large for the coloured residual the system actually has: a more accurate estimate reported with an unjustified confidence. The residual level belongs to the family, not to the sensor.

  5. The misfit as a warning light. The notebook's adapt returns a third number, the output misfit of the adapted model on its own record. What is it for the notebook's example, how does it compare with \hat\sigma, and what would a misfit of 0.05 suggest?
    Hint

    A model that belongs to the family should leave a residual of about \hat\sigma on its own data.

    Solution

    The misfit is 0.0110, below the family's 0.0190: with only 20 samples and two free numbers the record is matched slightly better than a typical long record, as expected. A misfit of 0.05, two and a half times \hat\sigma, would say that no context explains this record to the family's usual accuracy, which is the sign that the new system does not belong to the family. Chapter 07 builds the detector on exactly this number.

Part 7

The chapter on one page

  1. Freeze the backbone, estimate the context: MAP with the pretraining likelihood scale \hat\sigma and the standard-normal prior, a two-variable least-squares problem.
  2. Uncertainty from the Laplace approximation: posterior precision equals data precision \hat\sigma^{-2} S^\top S plus the prior's identity, with S = J_\eta U.
  3. The notebook's example: from 2 s, validation RMSE 0.014 with the context against 0.056 for the mean system and 0.158 from scratch; the true coefficients would give 0.004.
  4. What 2 s determine: one direction of the context (23 units of prior), not the other (0.37); the posterior ellipse has standard deviations 0.22 and 0.85 and contains the truth at 1.9 sigma.
  5. The prior can lose on a draw (no prior scores 0.005 here) and must win on the population; tightening it without data is confidence bought, not earned.
  6. Twenty systems: mean RMSE 0.025 against 0.077, 15 wins of 20, and 5 losses, twice by more than a factor of 2.

Part 8

Scope and further reading

Scope. This chapter covers the adaptation step: the MAP estimate of the context, its Laplace posterior and a first comparison with the from-scratch and no-adaptation baselines on one example and on twenty systems. It does not vary the data budget (chapter 05), choose the experiment (chapter 06), test whether the new system belongs to the family (chapter 07) or use the model for control (chapter 08). Full Bayesian treatments that sample the posterior rather than approximating it are out of scope [7].

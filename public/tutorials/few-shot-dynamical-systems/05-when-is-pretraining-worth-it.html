When is pretraining worth it?

Chapter 04 showed one example where a pretrained context beat from-scratch identification on two seconds of data, and one where the prior pulled the wrong way. This chapter replaces anecdotes with a study: for five experiment lengths, forty new systems each, one random experiment per system, four ways of predicting a fresh 30 s input. The answer has a shape, and the shape is the central message of few-shot learning: pretraining buys data efficiency, not a better final model. The study runs in your browser, next to the notebook's own numbers.

Part 0

The design of the study

For each budget T \in \{15, 30, 60, 120, 250\} samples, that is 1.5 to 25 s, the notebook draws 40 new systems from the family. Each gets one random three-sine experiment of length T, and each method must then predict the system's disturbance-free response to a fresh 30 s six-sine input:

  1. No adaptation: always predict with the average system \bar\eta. The cost of ignoring the new data.
  2. From scratch: fit all four coefficients to the experiment (chapter 02).
  3. Pretrained + context: adapt the two-number context to the same data (chapter 04).
  4. Reference: the true coefficients \eta plugged into the learner's model. Not zero error, because the model lacks the quintic term and the bias.

The page adds a fifth line, best in family: the context obtained by projecting the true coefficients onto the family, the best any amount of data on the new system could do with a two-number context and this backbone. It separates the part of the context's error that more data can remove from the part that only a better family could.

Why a fresh validation input. The score is never the fit to the experiment itself, which the from-scratch method wins by construction, but the disturbance-free response to an input none of the methods has seen. That is the yardstick of chapter 01, and it is what makes overfitting visible.

Part 1

Reading the curves

Medians and quartiles. Forty errors per budget and method are summarised by their median and the band from the first to the third quartile. Medians are used because the error distributions have long right tails: a few from-scratch fits at 1.5 s predict with errors of 0.3 or more, and a mean would be dominated by them. The mean from-scratch error at 1.5 s is 0.179 against a median of 0.138; for the context it is 0.033 against 0.024. The ratios the notebook prints are ratios of medians,

\rho(T) = \frac{\operatorname{median}_n \text{RMSE}_{\text{scratch}}(T, n)}{\operatorname{median}_n \text{RMSE}_{\text{context}}(T, n)}, \qquad n = 1, \dots, 40,

which are 5.9, 2.1, 1.6, 1.8 and 1.1 at the five budgets: a steady narrowing, not monotone, because forty systems and forty random inputs per budget leave sampling noise of a few tens of percent in each ratio. The hero demo's rerun shows how much they move with a different draw.

Logarithmic axes. Both axes are logarithmic: the budgets span a factor of 17 and the errors a factor of 60, and on a log scale a constant ratio between two methods is a constant vertical gap. Equal vertical spacing of the "no adaptation" line means its error does not depend on the budget at all, as it should: it never looks at the data.

Paired comparisons. Every method sees the same system, the same experiment and the same validation input, so the comparison is paired. Win counts (how often the context beats from scratch on the same system) are a sharper statistic than the ratio of medians, and they are in the table below.

Part 2

What the numbers say

BudgetNo adaptationFrom scratchContextBest in familyReferenceScratch ÷ contextContext winsScratch worse than nothing

Medians over the notebook's 40 systems per budget; the last two columns are paired counts. Three lessons carry through the rest of the course.

  1. Scarce data: from scratch is worse than doing nothing. At 1.5 s the from-scratch median is 0.138 against 0.062 for the average system, and from scratch loses to the average system on 35 of 40 systems. Four free coefficients overfit fifteen samples almost every time. The pretrained context is already useful: 0.024, better than the average system on 36 of 40.
  2. More data: the gap narrows. From scratch improves steadily, from 0.138 to 0.011; the context improves from 0.024 to 0.010. At 25 s the medians differ by 10%, and the context still wins 28 of 40 pairs. Pretraining buys data efficiency, not a better final model.
  3. All learned models stay above the reference. The reference sits at 0.002 to 0.003 at every budget; no learned model gets within a factor of three of it, on any system.

Two floors, not one. The notebook attributes the remaining error to the imperfect pretraining and the rank-2 restriction, and says that neither can be fixed by collecting more data on the new system. The best-in-family line makes that precise. The context's median at 25 s is 0.0097; the best context the family can offer for the same systems scores 0.0058; the reference 0.0025. The gap from 0.0097 down to 0.0058 is estimation error in z, and more or better data on the new system can still close it. The gap from 0.0058 down to 0.0025 is the family itself, the imperfect \alpha and the two-dimensional restriction, and no amount of data on the new system touches it. Only a better model class does.

With little data, the question is not which method fits the experiment better but which one can be trusted outside it. Pretraining wins there by a factor of six at 1.5 s, by 10% at 25 s, and by nothing at all once the data alone determine four coefficients. What it cannot do is beat its own family.

Part 4

One number or two?

The notebook's exercise 2 asks how much worse a one-number context does. A rank-one family keeps only the first direction of chapter 03, stiffness and hardening moving together, and cannot represent the damping–gain direction at all. The intuition is that systems whose true context lies along z_2 are systematically mispredicted. The data say when that matters.

In the notebook's own run at 1.5 s the one-number context scores a median of 0.0280 against 0.0242 for two numbers on the same forty systems, and two numbers win 25 of 40: a 16% difference, not the "noticeably worse" of its printed solution. The reason is chapter 04's posterior: at 1.5 s the data are worth a fraction of a unit of prior along the second direction, so the two-number context sets z_2 mostly from the prior anyway, and the one-number context loses little by not having it. At 6 s the medians are 0.0196 against 0.0130, at 25 s 0.0159 against 0.0086: the second number pays off once the data can determine it. The rank of the context is a bet on the budget as much as on the family.

Part 5

Check your understanding

1. Why is the score a fresh validation input and not the fit to the experiment?

Because the from-scratch fit wins the latter by construction, with four free coefficients. Only prediction on data the methods have not seen reveals overfitting, and only the disturbance-free response removes the luck of the noise.

2. Why medians rather than means?

The error distributions have long right tails, especially from scratch at short budgets, where a few fits predict with errors above 0.3. The mean at 1.5 s is 0.179 against a median of 0.138. Medians describe the typical system; means describe the worst ones.

3. Why does "no adaptation" draw a flat line?

It never uses the experiment, so its error cannot depend on the budget. Its median wanders between 0.044 and 0.063 only because each budget has its own forty systems; the band shows the spread of the family around its mean member.

4. The ratio of medians goes 5.9, 2.1, 1.6, 1.8, 1.1. Why is it not monotone?

Sampling noise. Forty systems and forty random inputs per budget leave each median uncertain by tens of percent, and the two medians are estimated from the same forty systems but differently affected by them. The page's rerun with other draws gives a different sequence with the same shape.

5. What does "from scratch is worse than doing nothing" mean, in numbers?

At 1.5 s the from-scratch error exceeds the average system's on 35 of 40 systems, with medians 0.138 against 0.062. By 6 s it is 7 of 40, by 25 s one.

6. Which part of the context's remaining error can more data remove?

The part above the best-in-family floor: at 25 s, from 0.0097 down to 0.0058. The rest, from 0.0058 down to the reference's 0.0025, is the family's imperfect \alpha and its two-dimensional restriction, and needs a better model class, not more data on the new system.

7. Does from scratch ever overtake the context within 25 s?

Not on the median: the ratio ends at 1.1, and the context wins 28 of 40 pairs at 25 s. The curves converge; the crossing, if any, lies beyond the budgets tried.

8. When does the second context number start to matter?

Once the experiment can determine it. At 1.5 s a one-number context is only 16% worse in median; at 6 s the medians are 0.0196 against 0.0130, at 25 s 0.0159 against 0.0086.

Part 6

Exercises

  1. Where exactly does from scratch catch up? Using the budget_err arrays, find the smallest budget at which the median from-scratch error drops below the median pretrained-context error, if it does within the budgets tried.
    Hint

    Compare the two medians budget by budget.

    Solution

    It does not, within 25 s: the medians are 0.138/0.024, 0.047/0.022, 0.020/0.013, 0.016/0.009 and 0.011/0.010. From scratch improves faster, by a factor of 12 against 2.4 over the range, so the curves converge, but the crossing, if any, lies beyond 25 s.

    med_scratch = np.median(budget_err["scratch"], axis=1)
med_context = np.median(budget_err["context"], axis=1)
crossing = [T for T, s, c in zip(budgets, med_scratch, med_context) if s < c]
crossing[:1] if crossing else "no crossing within the tried budgets"
  2. Restricting the family to one dimension. Refit the family with rank=1 and repeat the comparison at the shortest budget. How much worse does a one-number context do compared with the two-number one?
    Hint

    Adapt both families to the same forty experiments so that the comparison is paired, and try a longer budget too.

    Solution

    At 1.5 s the one-number context scores a median of 0.0280 against 0.0242 for two numbers on the same forty systems, two numbers better on 25 of 40: about 16% worse, not much. The notebook's solution calls it "noticeably worse"; the numbers do not support that at this budget, because 1.5 s do not determine the second direction and the two-number context fills it from the prior anyway. The gap grows with the budget: 0.0196 against 0.0130 at 6 s, 0.0159 against 0.0086 at 25 s, when the data start to determine z_2 and only the two-number context can use it.

    rank1_family, *_ = dynutils.build_family(np.random.default_rng(dynutils.SEED), rank=1)
T = budgets[0]
errs = []
for n in range(40):
    s = sample_system(rng); u = multisine(T, rng); _, y = simulate(s, u, rng)
    u_val = multisine(300, rng, n=6); q_val, _ = simulate(s, u_val, rng, noise=False)
    z1, _, _ = adapt(rank1_family, y, u)
    errs.append(rmse(predict(rank1_family.decode(z1), u_val), q_val))
np.median(errs), np.median(budget_err["context"][0])
  3. Means against medians. Recompute the study's summary with means instead of medians. Which conclusions change, and why?
    Hint

    Look at the largest from-scratch errors at 1.5 s.

    Solution

    The means are 0.179 against 0.033 at 1.5 s (ratio 5.4) and 0.026 against 0.016 at 25 s (ratio 1.6), so by the mean the gap at 25 s looks larger than by the median (1.1). The reason is the right tail: at every budget a few from-scratch fits land far off, and the mean weights them fully. Neither statistic is wrong; they answer different questions, the typical system against the average cost over all systems. Report both, and say which.

  4. Two floors. Add the best-in-family oracle, family.decode(family.project(s["eta"])), to the study. How does the context's gap to the reference split into a part that data can close and a part that it cannot?
    Hint

    Neither the oracle nor the reference uses the experiment, so their medians should not trend with the budget.

    Solution

    The oracle's median is 0.0043 to 0.0063 across budgets, the reference's 0.0020 to 0.0029. At 25 s the context's 0.0097 is 1.7 times the oracle, a gap that longer or better-designed experiments can still close; the oracle is 2.3 times the reference, a gap fixed by the family (its \alpha estimates and the rank-two restriction) that no data on the new system can touch. Chapter 06 attacks the first gap by choosing the experiment; chapter 07 asks what happens when the second gap is large because the system is not in the family.

  5. Win rates. For each budget, count the systems on which the context beats from scratch and on which from scratch beats doing nothing. What do these paired counts add to the medians?
    Hint

    (budget_err["context"] < budget_err["scratch"]).sum(axis=1).

    Solution

    Context beats from scratch on 39, 32, 34, 32 and 28 of 40 systems; from scratch beats doing nothing on 5, 23, 33, 36 and 39 of 40. The counts show that the 1.1 ratio at 25 s is still a 70% win rate, and that from scratch at 1.5 s is not merely worse on average but worse on almost every individual system. Paired counts use the pairing the study was designed with; medians throw it away.

The chapter on one page

  1. The study: five budgets from 1.5 to 25 s, forty new systems each, four methods scored on a fresh 30 s input.
  2. Scarce data: from scratch 0.138 against 0.062 for the average system and 0.024 for the context at 1.5 s; overfitting on 35 of 40 systems.
  3. The gap narrows: scratch ÷ context goes 5.9, 2.1, 1.6, 1.8, 1.1; the context still wins 28 of 40 pairs at 25 s.
  4. Floors: the reference at 0.002 to 0.003, the best-in-family oracle at 0.004 to 0.006; the context's gap to the oracle is estimation error, the oracle's gap to the reference is the family.
  5. Rank: a one-number context is 16% worse at 1.5 s and twice as bad at 25 s; the second number pays once the data determine it.
  6. Reading studies: medians for the typical system, means for the tail, paired win counts for the design; forty systems leave tens of percent of noise in a ratio.

Part 8

Scope and further reading

Scope. This chapter is an empirical study with the methods of chapters 02 to 04 and random experiments. It does not choose the experiment (chapter 06), does not test whether a new system belongs to the family (chapter 07), and does not use the models for control (chapter 08). Its statistics are deliberately elementary: medians, quartiles and paired counts over forty systems; resampling methods would give confidence intervals for the ratios [4].

The data-efficiency reading of few-shot learning is the standard one [1][3]; the overfitting of four free coefficients at fifteen samples is the bias–variance trade-off in its plainest form [2][5], and the two floors are the classical split between model-class error and estimation error [6][7]. So far every experiment has been random. Next, chapter 06 asks whether the experiment itself can be chosen.

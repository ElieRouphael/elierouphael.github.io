Learning to Identify New Dynamical Systems · Chapter 07 of 09

When the shared knowledge does not apply

Every chapter so far drew new systems from the same family the pretrained model was built on. Real deployments are not always this polite: a worn bearing, a manufacturing defect, or simply a different product line can push a new system outside what pretraining ever saw. This chapter asks what happens then, and whether we can notice it. The answer has two halves: the pretrained adapter degrades gracefully but surely, and the one warning light it carries, the misfit of its own fit, comes on late. Both halves run in your browser.

Part 0

The question

The pretrained model is only as good as the family it has seen. Chapter 05 showed that a two-number context beats four free coefficients when data are scarce, because the family tells the adapter where systems of this kind live. The same knowledge becomes a liability when a new system lives somewhere else. This chapter moves new systems away from the family in a controlled way, measures how the adapter degrades against the from-scratch fit, and asks whether the adapter can tell, from its own data, that something is wrong.

Part 1

Moving outside the learned family

What the context can and cannot express. The decoder reaches the coefficients \bar\eta + Uz: a two-dimensional plane in the four-dimensional coefficient space. Any change of coefficients splits into a component in that plane, which some z can follow, and a component in its orthogonal complement, which no z can. The notebook takes a unit vector of the complement and pushes new systems along it:

\eta_{\text{new}} = \operatorname{clip}\big(\eta + \delta\, v\big), \qquad U^\top v = 0, \quad \lVert v \rVert = 1, \qquad v \approx (0.33,\ 0.88,\ -0.24,\ -0.24),

mostly an increase of damping with a little more stiffness and a little less gain and hardening: a worn bearing, say. The shift \delta runs from 0 to 0.6, and the identification budget is 12 s, long enough that from scratch is already competitive inside the family.

Two caveats about the direction. The orthogonal complement is two-dimensional, so "a direction orthogonal to U that moves damping most" is not unique: the notebook takes the vector of an SVD basis with the largest damping component, while the basis-free choice, the damping axis projected onto the complement, is (0.12, 0.97, 0.12, -0.18), 25° away. And orthogonality is measured in physical units, whereas the family was learned in whitened units: divided by the estimation standard deviations of chapter 03, the notebook's direction is (31, 182, -31, -0.8), almost pure damping. Neither caveat changes the conclusions, since both directions leave the family; the explorer lets you compare them.

The unrepresentable component, in numbers. Projecting a shifted system's true coefficients onto the plane and measuring what is left in whitened units gives the distance from the family: a median of 1.8 units for unshifted systems (their own individual deviations and the plane's imperfection), 14 at \delta = 0.3, 30 at \delta = 0.6. The best context the family can offer, the projection itself, predicts the shifted systems with a median error that grows from 0.0036 to 0.0217 across the shifts; switch it on in the hero demo. That floor, not the adapter, is what fails first.

Part 2

The warning light

The statistic. The adaptation of chapter 04 already returns a third number, the output misfit: the residual RMS of the adapted model on the record it was fitted to,

m = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T+1}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\big(y_t - \hat q_t(\bar\eta + U\hat z;\,u)\big)^2}, \qquad \text{flag} \iff m > \tau_{95}.

If the best context still cannot explain the data, the residual is larger than it is for normal members of the family. The threshold \tau_{95} is calibrated as the 95th percentile of the misfit over 60 in-family systems identified the same way: by construction the test then raises a false alarm on about 5% of systems that do belong to the family. The notebook's 60 calibration misfits run from 0.010 to 0.023 with a median of 0.013, and \tau_{95} = 0.0175.

Why the misfit is a weak signal. Decompose the residual of a correct-in-family model: sensor noise (0.01), the coloured disturbance's effect on the position (0.0165 in chapter 01), minus what two free numbers absorb over 12 s. The misfit of an in-family system is therefore about 0.013 whatever the system, and a shift adds to it only the part of the mismatch that a re-optimised z cannot hide. At \delta = 0.3 the median misfit is 0.0134 against 0.0133 unshifted, a difference far smaller than the spread of either cloud. The statistic is sound and cheap, and it is dominated by something that has nothing to do with the family.

  1. The adapter degrades steadily. From 0.0096 inside the family to 0.0133 at \delta = 0.6; its floor, the best-in-family projection, degrades faster, from 0.0036 to 0.0217, and from \delta = 0.2 on the adapter actually beats its own oracle, because a z fitted to data can compensate a little for the missing direction while the geometric projection cannot.
  2. From scratch is unaffected, and improves. It assumes nothing about the family. Its error falls from 0.0132 to 0.0048 because the shifted systems are more heavily damped (mean damping 0.34 to 0.85) and move less (validation RMS 0.144 to 0.087), so there is less to mispredict. With 12 s of data from scratch is already competitive inside the family, and the pretrained model loses its advantage after a small shift: the context wins 23 of 30 pairs at \delta = 0, 14 of 30 at 0.2, 5 of 30 at 0.3.
  3. Detection lags degradation. The test flags 10% of the unshifted systems (3 of 30, consistent with the 5% target at this sample size) and rises to about 40% at the largest shifts; the notebook's text says "roughly three quarters", which its own printout does not support. At \delta = 0.3 the adapter already loses to from scratch on 25 systems of 30 while 17% are flagged.

Use the small adapter while it explains the data, and fall back to, or enlarge towards, full identification when it does not. The rule is simple; making it both sensitive and trustworthy is not, because the statistic that drives it is dominated by disturbance, not by the family. Guarantees for such rules on learned models are an open question.

Part 5

Check your understanding

1. What does "outside the family" mean geometrically?

A coefficient vector with a component orthogonal to the plane \bar\eta + Uz. The in-plane component is representable by some context; the orthogonal component is not, whatever z is chosen, and it is measured here in whitened units as the distance from the plane.

2. Why is the shift direction not unique, and does it matter?

The complement of a plane in four dimensions is itself two-dimensional, so "the orthogonal direction that moves damping most" depends on the basis chosen for it; the notebook's SVD vector and the projected damping axis differ by 25°. It does not matter for the conclusions: any direction with a sizeable component outside the plane produces the same degradation pattern.

3. Why does the from-scratch error decrease as the shift grows?

The shift mostly increases damping, from 0.34 on average to 0.85 at \delta = 0.6; heavily damped systems move less (validation RMS 0.087 against 0.144), so the same coefficient errors produce smaller prediction errors. From scratch assumes nothing about the family, so nothing else changes for it.

4. How is the misfit threshold set, and what does it guarantee?

As the 95th percentile of the misfit over 60 in-family systems identified from 12 s. It guarantees a false-alarm rate of about 5% on systems like the calibration ones, nothing about detection; the 10% observed at \delta = 0 is 3 systems of 30, within sampling noise.

5. Why does detection lag degradation?

Because the misfit is dominated by the sensor noise and the coloured disturbance, about 0.013 for any in-family system, and a shift adds only the part of the mismatch that a re-fitted z cannot absorb. At \delta = 0.3 the median misfit has risen by 0.0001 while the adapter's error has risen by 15% and it loses to from scratch on 25 of 30 systems.

6. What does raising the percentile to 99 do?

It cuts the false-alarm rate to about 1% by construction and detects fewer shifted systems at every \delta: 3% at 0.3 and 17% at 0.6 in the notebook's study, against 17% and 37% at the 95th. The same trade-off governs every anomaly threshold.

7. Why does the best-in-family oracle do worse than the adapter at large shifts?

The oracle is a geometric projection of the true coefficients, chosen without looking at data. The adapter chooses z to fit the record, and a context that is wrong in coefficient space can still predict the output better by compensating the missing direction's effect on the response. Prediction and coefficient accuracy are different targets.

8. What is the decision rule, and why is it unsatisfying?

Trust the adapter while its misfit is below the threshold; switch to full identification when it is not. It is unsatisfying because the switch comes after the adapter has already lost its advantage, and because the threshold trades false alarms for detection without any guarantee about either on systems the calibration set did not resemble.

Part 6

Exercises

  1. A direction that stays inside the family. Repeat the shift study along a direction inside U, the normalised first column, at \delta = 0.4. Does the pretrained model degrade, and does the misfit test flag anything?
    Hint

    Compare with the in-family baseline (0.0096, 10% flagged) and with the orthogonal shift at the same distance (0.0129, 13%).

    Solution

    Median error 0.0104 and 2 of 30 flagged (7%), against 0.0096 and 10% in the family and 0.0129 and 13% for the orthogonal shift. Moving along a direction the context can represent leaves both the error and the flag rate near the baseline, because the adapter simply finds a z that reaches that part of coefficient space (the shifted systems' whitened distance from the plane stays at the in-family value). It is specifically the unrepresentable component of a shift that hurts.

    inside = family.U[:, 0] / np.linalg.norm(family.U[:, 0])
errs_inside, flags_inside = [], []
for n in range(30):
    s = sample_system(rng)
    s["eta"] = np.clip(s["eta"] + 0.4 * inside, dynutils.LOW + 0.02, dynutils.HIGH)
    u = multisine(T, rng); _, y = simulate(s, u, rng)
    u_val = multisine(300, rng, n=6); q_val, _ = simulate(s, u_val, rng, noise=False)
    z_, _, misfit = adapt(family, y, u)
    errs_inside.append(rmse(predict(family.decode(z_), u_val), q_val)); flags_inside.append(misfit > threshold)
np.median(errs_inside), np.mean(flags_inside)
  2. A stricter threshold. Recompute the threshold at the 99th percentile. How do the false-alarm rate on in-family systems and the detection rate at \delta = 0.3 change?
    Hint

    The widget in Part 2 does this for any percentile on the notebook's own misfits.

    Solution

    \tau_{99} = 0.0202. On the 60 calibration systems the false-alarm rate falls from 5% to 1.7% (one system; by construction about 1%). On 30 fresh systems at \delta = 0.3 detection falls from 10% to 7%; on the main study's systems at the same shift from 17% to 3%, and at \delta = 0.6 from 37% to 17%. Fewer false alarms, longer detection lag: the trade-off of every anomaly threshold.

    threshold_99 = np.quantile(in_family, 0.99)
delta = 0.3
misfits = []
for n in range(30):
    s = sample_system(rng)
    s["eta"] = np.clip(s["eta"] + delta * direction, dynutils.LOW + 0.02, dynutils.HIGH)
    u = multisine(T, rng); _, y = simulate(s, u, rng)
    misfits.append(adapt(family, y, u)[2])
np.mean(in_family > threshold_99), np.mean(np.array(misfits) > threshold_99)
  3. The distance from the plane. For each shifted system compute the whitened residual of its true coefficients after projection onto the family, and plot the adapter's error and the misfit against it. Which of the two tracks the distance?
    Hint

    (s["eta"] - family.decode(family.project(s["eta"]))) / family.scale.

    Solution

    The median distance is 1.8, 4.6, 9.4, 14, 20, 25 and 30 whitened units at the seven shifts: it grows linearly with \delta, as it must for a shift orthogonal to the plane. The adapter's error tracks it monotonically; the misfit barely moves until the distance exceeds about 20 units. The distance is the quantity one would like to detect, and it is exactly what a learner without the true coefficients cannot compute; the misfit is its blurred shadow in the data.

  4. A better statistic? The misfit ignores the structure of the residual. Propose a statistic that would separate an unrepresentable shift from the disturbance, and say what it would need.
    Hint

    The disturbance is a stationary coloured noise; a model mismatch produces a residual correlated with the input and the predicted output.

    Solution

    Regress the residual on the output sensitivities with respect to the coefficients the context cannot move, J_\eta v for v in the complement, and test whether the coefficient is zero against the residual's own variance; or compare the residual's correlation with the input to what the disturbance alone would give. Either needs the sensitivity machinery of chapter 02 and a model of the disturbance's colour, and both would flag at smaller shifts, because they look for the shape of the mismatch rather than its size. A full treatment is model-validation theory [1].

  5. Fallback in practice. Implement the decision rule: use the adapter when the misfit is below \tau_{95}, otherwise the from-scratch fit. Score it across the seven shifts and compare with always using one or the other.
    Hint

    Everything needed is already in shift_err and flagged.

    Solution

    With the notebook's arrays the rule takes the from-scratch error on the flagged systems and the context error otherwise. Median errors of rule, adapter and from scratch: 0.0092, 0.0096, 0.0132 at \delta = 0 (the 3 false alarms cost little); 0.0102, 0.0111, 0.0072 at 0.3 (5 flagged, of which only 2 were better served by from scratch); 0.0127, 0.0133, 0.0048 at 0.6 (11 flagged, 10 of them rightly). The rule recovers a few percent of the gap, never more, because the detector fires on a third of the systems at most. With 12 s of data on this family, always fitting from scratch would be the safer default; the adapter earns its place at short budgets, where chapter 05 showed its advantage and where the misfit is even less informative.

Part 8

Scope and further reading

Scope. This chapter studies one kind of shift, a coefficient change along a fixed direction outside the family, at one budget, with one detector, the output misfit against a calibrated percentile. It does not treat shifts of the disturbance or the sensor, structural changes of the equation, sequential or online detection, or detectors that use the residual's structure, which the exercises point to. The classical theory of residual tests and change detection is the background [1][2].

What many systems have in common

Chapter 02 fitted one system at a time and found that a short experiment leaves the coefficients poorly determined. Suppose instead we have long historical recordings of twenty other systems from the same family, say from end-of-line tests. Can we learn from this population what systems of this kind have in common, and package it into something a new system can borrow from? This chapter builds that package: a mean system, two directions of variation found by principal component analysis, and a two-number context. It is the pretrained backbone every later chapter reuses, and it is rebuilt live below from the notebook's own twenty fits.

Part 0

The question

Twenty systems are drawn from the family of chapter 01, each recorded for 80 s (800 samples) under a random six-sine input, and each identified on its own by the output-error fit of chapter 02. That gives twenty estimates \hat\eta_1, \dots, \hat\eta_{20}. The question is what they have in common, and the answer has to be found from the estimates alone: the learner is never told that the simulator generates \eta from two hidden design variables.

Two steps. First identify each training system, keeping not only the estimate but its uncertainty. Then look for a low-dimensional description of how the coefficients vary from system to system. The first step is chapter 02 twenty times over; the second is principal component analysis with one essential modification.

Part 1

Step 1: identify each system

With 80 s of data each, the from-scratch fit returns \hat\eta_i and the Gauss–Newton covariance \hat\sigma_i^2 (J_i^\top J_i)^{-1} of each fit. Both are kept: the covariance is what will rescue the second step.

The residual level is also recorded: \hat\sigma, the root-mean-square output misfit of the fits, is the noise a model of this family should expect. It comes out at 0.0190, and that number is no accident: the sensor noise (0.01) and the coloured disturbance's typical effect on the position (0.0165 in chapter 01) add in quadrature to . Chapter 04 will use \hat\sigma to weigh data against prior.

Part 2

Step 2: the shared geometry

The model of the family. We look for the best rank-r affine approximation of the estimates,

\hat\eta_i \approx \bar\eta + U z_i, \qquad z_i \in \mathbb R^r, \quad z_i \sim \mathcal N(0, I),

where \bar\eta is the average system, the columns of U are the main directions of variation, and the context z_i is scaled to unit variance across the family. \bar\eta and U are the pretrained backbone; z is the small part that changes from system to system. With r = 2, two numbers describe a member.

PCA by the singular value decomposition. Stack the centred estimates into the n \times 4 matrix X with rows \hat\eta_i - \bar\eta, and factor X = W S V^\top. The columns of V are the principal directions, and

\lambda_k = \frac{s_k^2}{n - 1}, \qquad \text{explained}_k = \frac{\lambda_k}{\sum_j \lambda_j}, \qquad U = V_r\,\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_1}, \dots, \sqrt{\lambda_r}), \qquad z_i = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1^{-1/2}, \dots)\, V_r^\top (\hat\eta_i - \bar\eta).

Here \lambda_k is the variance of the estimates along direction k, and keeping the first r directions is the best rank-r approximation of X in the least-squares sense [2][1]. Folding \sqrt{\lambda_k} into U is what makes z have unit variance: a context of z = (1, 0) is a system one standard deviation along the first direction. The page computes the decomposition as the eigen-decomposition of the 4 \times 4 covariance X^\top X / (n - 1), which has the same V and \lambda.

Encoding and decoding. A context decodes to coefficients by \eta(z) = \bar\eta + Uz, clipped to the admissible box of chapter 02; known coefficients project to a context by least squares, z = \arg\min_z \lVert \bar\eta + Uz - \eta \rVert, which the notebook uses only for evaluation, since a new system's coefficients are exactly what is not known.

What is a pretrained backbone, and what is a context?

In plain words: the backbone is everything that is the same for every member of the family, learned once from many of them; the context is the handful of numbers that say which member you are looking at.

More precisely: here the backbone is the physics equation together with \bar\eta, U, the whitening scale and \hat\sigma; the context is z \in \mathbb R^2 with a standard-normal prior. A large foundation model for dynamical systems replaces the fixed equation by a neural state-space model and PCA by a learned encoder, but keeps the same division of labour: a shared backbone plus a small system-specific context [5][6].

Mean system \bar\eta
The average of the training estimates; the model you would use knowing nothing about a new member.
Directions U
How the coefficients move together across the family, one column per retained direction.
Context z
Unit-variance coordinates of a member along those directions.
Rank r
How many directions are kept; the family's effective dimension.
Part 3

The trap, and its fix

Plain PCA looks for the directions of largest spread in the estimates. But \hat\eta_i spreads for two reasons: genuine differences between systems, and estimation error. Chapter 02 showed that \alpha is poorly identified, so its estimates scatter widely, and plain PCA spends a principal direction on this noise. How badly depends on the signal-to-noise ratio of each coefficient, which the page computes from the simulator's hidden mixing matrix (genuine spread) and the fits' covariances (estimation error):

CoefficientGenuine spread across the family (sd)Estimation sd from 80 sSignal-to-noise

Noise-whitened PCA. The remedy is to measure every coefficient in units of its own estimation uncertainty before running PCA. With s_j the average estimation standard deviation of coefficient j over the training fits,

s_j = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n}\textstyle\sum_i \big[\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\eta_i)\big]_{jj}}, \qquad X_{ij} = \frac{\hat\eta_{ij} - \bar\eta_j}{s_j}, \qquad U = \mathrm{diag}(s)\, V_r\, \mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_k}),

so PCA runs on the whitened matrix and U is mapped back to physical units. Directions are then ranked by signal-to-noise ratio rather than raw spread. It is a simple form of factor analysis: probabilistic PCA with a known, coefficient-dependent noise variance [3]. For the notebook's family s = (0.0107, 0.0048, 0.0080, 0.298): one whitened unit of \alpha is thirty physical units of k.

The printout, in numbers. Plain PCA explains 92.2% and 7.3% of the raw variance with its first two directions; whitened PCA explains 83.2% and 14.6% of the whitened variance, 97.8% together, and the third and fourth directions carry 1.8% and 0.4%: the learner has discovered, from data alone, that the family is essentially two-dimensional. The test that matters is whether the learned z encodes the hidden design variables. The share of the variance of c_1 and c_2 explained by an affine function of z,

R^2_j = 1 - \frac{\sum_i (c_{ij} - \hat c_{ij})^2}{\sum_i (c_{ij} - \bar c_j)^2}, \qquad \hat c_i = A z_i + a \ \text{ fitted by least squares},

is 0.98 and 0.22 for plain PCA and 0.99 and 0.96 for whitened PCA. Plain PCA recovers the first design variable but largely misses the second, because its leading direction is the scatter of \alpha. In the whitened metric the learned plane makes principal angles of 1.8° and 6.7° with the true plane spanned by the simulator's mixing matrix; plain PCA's makes 1.7° and 51.5°.

What a model can learn from a population is limited by what can be identified in each of its members. Carry the uncertainty of each fit into the pretraining step, or the pretraining will learn the noise.

Part 4

What the learned family looks like

The hero demo shows the learned contexts: a colour gradient along one axis of the cloud for c_1 and along another for c_2. The learned coordinates are a rotated and rescaled copy of the hidden variables: fitting c \approx Az + a gives

A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.529 & 0.072 \\ -0.176 & -0.397 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad a = (-0.192,\ 0.114),

with residuals of 0.044 and 0.088 on variables that range over [-1, 1]. PCA cannot know the names of the hidden variables, and it does not need to: what matters is that two numbers locate a member. The explorer decodes any context into a member of the family.

What pretraining inherits. The mean system \bar\eta = (1.117, 0.322, 0.869, 1.019) is the average of twenty estimates; the true coefficients of the same twenty systems average (1.106, 0.320, 0.858, 0.986), and the simulator's centre \eta_0 = (1.2, 0.32, 0.9, 1.0) differs from both because twenty random design variables do not average to zero. The directions are right to within a few degrees, and the estimates of \alpha are off by up to 154%. Later chapters will see all of this as a floor under what the context can achieve.

From here on, every chapter needs this family. dynutils.build_family(rng) rebuilds it with exactly this code, so calling it with a freshly seeded generator reproduces the same twenty systems, the same fits and the same U at the top of every later notebook; the page does the same from the stored fits.

Part 5

Check your understanding

1. What are the two steps of pretraining here, and what does each produce?

Identify each training system from its long record (twenty estimates with their Gauss–Newton covariances and a residual level), then find the low-dimensional affine model \hat\eta \approx \bar\eta + Uz of how the estimates vary by PCA. The backbone is \bar\eta, U, the whitening scale and \hat\sigma; the context z is per system.

2. Why is \sqrt{\lambda_k} folded into U?

So that the context has unit variance across the family. Then a standard-normal prior on z in chapter 04 says exactly "a typical member of this family", and z = (2, 0) means two standard deviations out along the first direction.

3. Why does plain PCA fail on this family?

Because it ranks directions by raw spread, and the largest raw spread is \alpha's estimation scatter (standard deviation 0.30, about as large as its genuine variation), not a genuine difference between systems. Its first direction loads 0.99 on \alpha; the second design variable, which lives mostly in d and b, is explained with R^2 = 0.22.

4. What does whitening do, in one sentence?

It divides each coefficient by its average estimation standard deviation before PCA, so that directions are ranked by signal-to-noise ratio: k's genuine spread is 30 estimation deviations wide, \alpha's about one.

5. Does the learned z equal the hidden c?

No, and it cannot: PCA is defined up to rotation, scaling and sign. It equals an affine image of c, with R^2 of 0.99 and 0.96, which is all that later chapters need.

6. What does \hat\sigma = 0.019 measure, and why is it larger than the sensor noise?

The root-mean-square misfit between each fitted model and its training record. It combines the sensor noise (0.01) with the coloured disturbance's effect on the position (0.0165) in quadrature, and is the noise level a model of this family should expect on any record.

7. Is twenty training systems enough?

For the subspace, five would do: projecting all twenty true coefficient vectors through a family built from five systems already gives R^2 of 0.999 and 0.994. More systems stabilise the mean system, the split of variance between the two directions (60/40 with five, 83/15 with twenty) and the context scale. The notebook's exercise 1 solution says the opposite; see exercise 1 below.

8. In what sense is this a miniature foundation model?

It learns from many systems a shared backbone and a small per-system context, and it will adapt to a new system by estimating the context only. A large model swaps the physics equation for a neural state-space model and PCA for a learned encoder; the division of labour is the same.

Part 6

Exercises

  1. How many training systems does the family need? Refit the family with only 5 and 10 training systems and compare the variance explained by the first two directions and the R^2 against the hidden design variables.
    Hint

    An affine map from z \in \mathbb R^2 to one design variable has three parameters. Ask what an in-sample R^2 on five points can mean, then find a fairer test.

    Solution

    With 5 systems the first two directions explain 99.9% of the whitened variance and the in-sample R^2 is 0.998 and 0.990; with 10, 98.0% and 0.984, 0.978; with 20, 97.8% and 0.993, 0.958. The notebook's solution says the R^2 is "markedly lower" with five systems; it is higher, because a three-parameter fit to five points is bound to score well, so the in-sample figures say little. A fairer test projects the true coefficients of all twenty systems through each family's subspace: R^2 of 0.999 and 0.994 with 5 systems, 0.999 and 0.995 with 10 or 20. The subspace is found almost at once; more systems buy a stable mean, a stable variance split (60/40 with five against 83/15 with twenty) and a reliable context scale. The hero demo's slider runs this live.

    for n in (5, 10, 20):
    fam_n = Family().fit(train_fits[:n], train_data[:n])
    r2_n = r_squared(fam_n.z_train, hidden[:n])
    z_all = np.array([fam_n.project(s["eta"]) for s in train_systems])
    print(n, fam_n.explained[:2].sum().round(3), r2_n.round(3), r_squared(z_all, hidden).round(3))
  2. How much does whitening change the backbone itself? Compare family.mean and naive.mean against family.U and naive.U. Which points closer to the true mixing matrix, up to rotation and scale?
    Hint

    Both means are the plain average of the same estimates. Compare subspaces, by the R^2 against the hidden variables or by principal angles between \mathrm{span}(U) and \mathrm{span}(M).

    Solution

    The means are identical, (1.117, 0.322, 0.869, 1.019). The directions differ: whitened PCA's R^2 is 0.99 and 0.96 against plain PCA's 0.98 and 0.22, as the notebook prints (its solution text quotes 0.98/0.96 against 0.96/0.35, which are not the printed values). In whitened units the principal angles between the learned plane and the true one are 1.8° and 6.7° for whitened PCA, 1.7° and 51.5° for plain PCA: plain PCA's second direction is dominated by \alpha's estimation noise rather than genuine variation.

    def principal_angles(X, Y):
    Qx, _ = np.linalg.qr(X); Qy, _ = np.linalg.qr(Y)
    return np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip(np.linalg.svd(Qx.T @ Qy)[1], -1, 1)))
W = family.scale[:, None]
print(principal_angles(family.U / W, dynutils._M / W), principal_angles(naive.U / W, dynutils._M / W))
  3. A rank-one family. Fit the family with rank=1. Which design variable survives, and what does that say about the second direction's 14.6%?
    Hint

    Compute R^2 of the one-dimensional z against both hidden variables.

    Solution

    R^2 = 0.975 for c_1 and 0.157 for c_2: the first direction is essentially the stiffness axis, and the second design variable, which chapter 01 showed to govern the damping ratio, lives almost entirely in the 14.6% that rank one discards. "Variance explained" is measured in the whitened metric and is not the same as importance for prediction; rank three adds nothing (0.993, 0.961).

  4. An oracle family. Run the same whitened PCA on the true coefficients of the twenty systems instead of the estimates. How close does the real family come to this ceiling?
    Hint

    Pass (s["eta"], np.diag(family.scale**2)) as the fits so that the same whitening is used.

    Solution

    The oracle explains 88.8% and 11.1% of the whitened variance with two directions and reaches R^2 of 0.999 and 0.995; the real family, built from estimates, reaches 0.993 and 0.958. The gap is the price of identifying each member from one noisy record, almost all of it paid in \alpha, and it is exactly the kind of imperfection that chapter 07 will have to detect when a new system does not fit.

  5. Recover the hidden variables from the context. Fit c \approx Az + a by least squares and report the residuals. What would a nonlinear encoder gain here?
    Hint

    np.linalg.lstsq(np.c_[family.z_train, np.ones(20)], hidden).

    Solution

    A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.529 & 0.072 \\ -0.176 & -0.397 \end{pmatrix}, a = (-0.192, 0.114), root-mean-square residuals 0.044 for c_1 and 0.088 for c_2 on variables that span [-1, 1]. The map from design variables to coefficients is affine in the simulator, so a nonlinear encoder would gain nothing on the structure; the residual comes from estimation error in the twenty fits, which more data per system, not a richer encoder, would reduce.

Part 7

The chapter on one page

  1. Step 1: twenty systems, 80 s each, identified from scratch; estimates, covariances and a residual level \hat\sigma = 0.019 kept.
  2. Step 2: PCA finds \hat\eta \approx \bar\eta + Uz with unit-variance context z; two directions explain 97.8% of the whitened variance.
  3. The trap: plain PCA ranks by raw spread and spends its first direction on \alpha's estimation scatter; it explains c_2 with R^2 = 0.22.
  4. The fix: whiten each coefficient by its estimation standard deviation, 0.0107, 0.0048, 0.0080 and 0.298 for k, d, b, \alpha; R^2 becomes 0.99 and 0.96, principal angles 1.8° and 6.7°.
  5. What was learned: the family is two-dimensional, z is an affine image of the hidden design variables, and five training systems already fix the subspace; twenty stabilise the rest.
  6. The backbone: \bar\eta, U, s, \hat\sigma, rebuilt deterministically at the top of every later chapter.

Part 8

Scope and further reading

Scope. This chapter builds the pretrained family from long records of twenty systems: from-scratch identification of each, then noise-whitened PCA of the estimates, with the diagnostics that show the learned structure is the hidden one. Nothing is yet done with a new system; chapter 04 freezes the backbone and estimates only the context from a few seconds of data [7].

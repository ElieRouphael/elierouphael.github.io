From a better model to better control

The last five chapters compared models purely by prediction error. But models are rarely the end goal; we want to act. This chapter asks the question a control engineer actually cares about: does a better-identified model translate into a better-controlled system? The test is deliberately unforgiving: a feedforward input computed from the model and applied open loop, so that nothing corrects the model's mistakes while the system runs. Below, the input is computed in your browser for four models of a new system and applied to the true one.

Part 0

The question

A model is a means. In this course the end is a controlled cart: make it follow a reference trajectory r_t, here a rest position of 0.2 followed by two smooth steps, down at 3 s and partly back up at 8 s. The question is whether the gains in prediction error of chapters 04 to 06 survive the trip from model to action. The candidate models are the ones the course has built: the average system, the pretrained context adapted from a designed 6 s experiment, a from-scratch fit on the same experiment (added by the page), and the true coefficients as the ceiling.

Part 1

Feedforward control from a model

The problem. A feedforward input is an input sequence chosen in advance so that the model's predicted position follows the reference, with a penalty on rough inputs [1]:

u^\star = \arg\min_{|u_t| \le 2}\ \sum_{t=0}^{T} \big(\hat q_t(\hat\eta; u) - r_t\big)^2 + \lambda \sum_{t=0}^{T-2} (u_{t+1} - u_t)^2, \qquad T = 120,\ \lambda = 0.05.

It is nonlinear least squares in 120 unknowns, the inputs, with the model's coefficients fixed: the mirror image of chapter 02, where the inputs were fixed and the coefficients unknown. The smoothness term is a ridge on the differences of u; without it the problem is ill-posed, because the actuator delay makes the last input irrelevant and the saturation flattens the cost for large commands.

The Jacobian. The derivative of the predicted position with respect to each input follows from linearising the model along its own trajectory. Input u_s first acts at step s + 1 through the actuator slope, and its effect then propagates through the linearised dynamics:

\delta v_{s+2} = \Delta t\, b\, \varphi'(u_s), \quad \delta q_{s+2} = \Delta t\, \delta v_{s+2}, \qquad \delta v_{t+1} = \delta v_t + \Delta t\big(-(k + 3\alpha \hat q_t^2)\,\delta q_t - d\,\delta v_t\big), \quad \delta q_{t+1} = \delta q_t + \Delta t\, \delta v_{t+1}, \qquad \frac{\partial \hat q_t}{\partial u_s} = \delta q_t .

Each column of the 121 \times 120 Jacobian is the model's impulse response to a kick at one sample, scaled by the actuator slope there; the page builds it exactly, the notebook by forward differences with step 10^{-5}. Levenberg–Marquardt with the smoothness term folded into the normal equations converges in a dozen iterations; SciPy's bounded trust-region solver and the page's agree on the inputs to a few parts in ten thousand, and the bound of ±2 is never active for this reference.

What is feedforward, and what is it not?

In plain words: work out in advance, from the model, the whole input that should produce the desired motion, then play it back without looking at what the system actually does.

More precisely: an open-loop input u^\star that inverts the model along the reference, here by regularised least squares. It is not feedback: no measurement of the running system changes the input, so model error and disturbance pass straight into the tracking error. That is what makes it a clean test of the model and a poor controller on its own; in practice feedforward from a model is combined with feedback that corrects the rest [2][3].

Part 2

Why feedforward is the honest test

Every model believes its own input: the promised tracking error is 0.005 to 0.007 for all four models, because the optimiser can always bend 120 inputs to make its model follow the reference. What differs is what happens on the true system. For the notebook's 15 systems without disturbance, the achieved error is 0.0565 for the average-system model, 0.0183 for the from-scratch fit, 0.0108 for the adapted context and 0.0087 for the true coefficients. The ordering is the prediction ordering of chapter 05 at 6 s, and the gaps between promise and achievement are the model errors, laid bare because nothing corrects them.

The disturbance floor. With the disturbance switched on the errors rise to 0.0577, 0.0191 and 0.0140 for average, adapted and true coefficients: the disturbance adds about 0.011 in quadrature to every model's error, and it adds the same amount whatever the model, because feedforward cannot see it. Only feedback can reject it. A feedback controller would reduce all the errors, but the ordering, and the reason for it, would remain.

Two numbers the notebook's text gets wrong. The adapted model does not "match what the true coefficients achieve": its mean tracking error is 1.36 times theirs, and it is better on 3 of 15 systems with the disturbance and on none without it. The sentence "it can even be marginally better, because it partly absorbs the unmodelled terms" describes the luck of three disturbance realisations, not a property of the adapter. The true statement is strong enough: six seconds of designed data, through the pretrained family, remove two thirds of the average model's tracking error and come within 40% of the ceiling.

Part 3

Fifteen new systems

The from-scratch fit on the same 6 s designed experiment, which the notebook does not run, lands between the average system and the adapted context at 0.0183 without disturbance: with 60 samples four free coefficients are still worse than two constrained ones, as chapter 05 found for prediction, and the designed experiment of chapter 06 does not change that ordering. Two numbers, chosen inside the family, beat four numbers chosen freely, for control as for prediction.

Part 4

Random against designed

The notebook's exercise 1 adapts from a random 6 s experiment instead of the designed one and tracks 15 further systems. Chapter 06 found a 55% reduction of prediction error from design at this budget. Here the random experiments' mean tracking error is 0.0171, below the designed experiments' 0.0191; the notebook's solution says the opposite. Nothing is wrong with design; the experiment is simply unable to show it. The two groups are different systems, there are 15 of each, and the disturbance adds a floor of 0.011 in quadrature that compresses a 55% difference in model quality into a few percent of tracking error. The page can run the comparison the way it should be run: paired, on the same systems, without disturbance.

Part 5

The smoothness penalty

The penalty \lambda trades tracking against gentleness, and the trade is a property of the optimisation, not of the model. The notebook's exercise 2 raises it tenfold on the true-coefficient model; the page sweeps it.

Part 6

Check your understanding

1. In what sense is feedforward design the mirror image of identification?

Identification fixes the inputs and fits the coefficients to the measured output; feedforward fixes the coefficients and fits the inputs to the desired output. Both are nonlinear least squares on the same model, and the page solves both with the same Levenberg–Marquardt machinery, with the Jacobian taken with respect to the coefficients in one case and the inputs in the other.

2. Why does every model promise the same small tracking error?

Because 120 free inputs are enough to make any of these models follow a smooth reference closely; the optimiser does not know the model is wrong. The promised errors are 0.005 to 0.007 for all four models; the achieved errors differ by a factor of six.

3. What does the gap between promised and achieved error measure?

The model error, propagated through the input the model chose. Feedforward applies the input open loop, so no part of that gap is corrected during the run. With the disturbance on, the gap also contains the disturbance's effect, which is the same for every model.

4. Does the adapted model match the true coefficients?

No. Its mean tracking error is 0.0191 against 0.0140 with the disturbance, 0.0108 against 0.0087 without; it is better on 3 of 15 systems with the disturbance's luck and on none without. It removes two thirds of the average-system error and comes within 40% of the ceiling, which is the claim the data support.

5. Why can the disturbance's contribution be described as a floor added in quadrature?

The disturbance is independent of the model error and roughly independent of the input, so the two add in mean square: for the true coefficients 0.0087 without disturbance becomes 0.0140 with it, and \sqrt{0.0140^2 - 0.0087^2} = 0.011. Feedforward cannot touch that part; feedback can.

6. Why does the random-experiment comparison fail to show the design advantage?

Fifteen systems per group, different systems in the two groups, and a disturbance floor of 0.011 that compresses a 55% difference in model quality into a few percent of tracking error. The random group's mean even comes out lower. A paired comparison on the same systems without disturbance is the experiment that could show it.

7. What does the smoothness penalty do, and why is it needed at all?

It charges for changes of the input from sample to sample. Without it the problem is ill-posed: the last input has no effect because of the actuator delay, and the saturation makes large commands nearly equivalent. Raising \lambda tenfold roughly halves the input's total variation and doubles the tracking error of the true-coefficient model, from 0.0067 to 0.0128 without disturbance.

8. What would feedback change, and what would it not?

Feedback would reject much of the disturbance and part of the model error, lowering every model's tracking error. It would not change the ordering: a controller designed on a worse model demands more of the feedback loop, and the margin it has to spend is finite. The reason for the ordering, model error, stays.

Part 7

Exercises

  1. A random experiment instead of a designed one. Repeat the control comparison, but adapt the context from a random 6 s experiment instead of the designed one. Does the "context" tracking error move noticeably closer to "average" or stay close to "true η"?
    Hint

    Compare the two means with the disturbance floor in mind, and ask what a paired comparison would need.

    Solution

    On 15 further systems with random experiments the mean tracking error is 0.0171, against 0.0191 for the designed experiments on the 15 systems above, 0.0577 for the average model and 0.0140 for the true coefficients. The notebook's solution says the random experiment's error "sits above the designed experiment's, closer to the midpoint"; it is in fact slightly lower. The design advantage that chapter 06 measured on prediction, about 55% at 6 s, is invisible here because the two groups are different systems, there are only 15 per group, and the disturbance adds a 0.011 floor in quadrature to every tracking error. The page's paired rerun, both experiments on the same systems and no disturbance, is the comparison that can show it.

    ctrl_err_random = []
for n in range(15):
    s = sample_system(rng)
    u_random = multisine(60, rng)
    z_, _, _ = adapt(family, simulate(s, u_random, rng)[1], u_random)
    q_ctrl, _ = simulate(s, feedforward(family.decode(z_)), rng)
    ctrl_err_random.append(rmse(q_ctrl, ref))
np.mean(ctrl_err_random), np.mean(ctrl_err["context"])
  2. A heavier smoothness penalty. Recompute the feedforward input for the true-coefficient model with \lambda = 0.5 instead of 0.05. How do the input and the tracking error change?
    Hint

    Measure the input's total variation, \sum_t |u_{t+1} - u_t|, and track without disturbance to see the effect cleanly.

    Solution

    On the notebook's exercise system the total variation of the input falls from 3.16 to 2.01 and the largest step between samples from 0.068 to 0.049; the tracking error without disturbance rises from 0.0067 to 0.0128 (with the notebook's disturbance realisation it reads 0.0312). Across \lambda = 0.005, 0.05, 0.5, 5 the error goes 0.0049, 0.0067, 0.0128, 0.0257 while the total variation goes 4.18, 3.16, 2.01, 0.96. A heavier penalty produces a gentler input that follows the sharp parts of the reference less closely, exactly as \lambda dictates and independently of which model supplied the coefficients.

    s = sample_system(rng)
u_smooth = feedforward(s["eta"], lam=0.5)
q_smooth, _ = simulate(s, u_smooth, rng)
rmse(q_smooth, ref)
  3. Promised against achieved. For each model and system, compute the RMSE the model predicts for its own input and the RMSE achieved on the true system without disturbance. Plot one against the other.
    Hint

    rmse(predict(eta, u_ff), ref) for the promise, simulate(s, u_ff, rng, noise=False) for the achievement.

    Solution

    The promises are 0.0052, 0.0069 and 0.0072 on average for the average-system, adapted and true-coefficient models; the achievements 0.0565, 0.0108 and 0.0087. The average-system model promises the most and delivers the least, because a wrong model can be bent to the reference as easily as a right one. The ratio of achieved to promised error is the cleanest single measure of how much a model can be trusted for open-loop control: 11 for the average system, 1.6 for the adapted context, 1.2 for the truth.

  4. From scratch, for control. Add a fourth model, the from-scratch fit on the same designed 6 s experiment, and track the 15 systems without disturbance. Where does it land?
    Hint

    fit_from_scratch(simulate(s, u_design, rng)[1], u_design), then the same feedforward.

    Solution

    At 0.0183 on average, between the average system's 0.0565 and the adapted context's 0.0108, with a median of 0.0184. Four free coefficients from 60 samples are worse for control than two constrained ones, by the same margin as for prediction in chapter 05, and the designed experiment does not change that: it is designed for the context's information, not for identifying four coefficients.

  5. Close the loop. Add a proportional feedback term, u_t = u^\star_t + K (r_t - y_t), to the feedforward input of each model with a modest gain, and track the 15 systems with the disturbance on. What happens to the errors, and to their ordering?
    Hint

    Simulate step by step, since the input now depends on the measurement; keep K small enough that the loop stays well damped.

    Solution

    Every error falls, the average-system model's the most in absolute terms, because feedback corrects both the disturbance and part of the model error. The ordering stays: the controller built on the better model needs less correction, and a proportional loop on a lightly damped oscillator cannot be made arbitrarily tight without exciting the resonance of chapter 01. Model-based feedforward plus feedback for the remainder is the standard architecture, and the model quality decides how much is left for the loop to do [2].

Part 8

The chapter on one page

  1. Feedforward: a regularised least-squares input that makes the model follow the reference; applied open loop, so model error passes straight through.
  2. The solver: Levenberg–Marquardt on 120 inputs with the exact input Jacobian from the linearised dynamics; the bound of ±2 never binds.
  3. Without disturbance: tracking RMSE 0.0565 (average system), 0.0183 (from scratch, same 6 s), 0.0108 (adapted context), 0.0087 (true coefficients); every model promised about 0.006.
  4. With the disturbance: 0.0577, 0.0191, 0.0140; a floor of 0.011 in quadrature that only feedback can remove.
  5. The honest claim: the adapted model removes two thirds of the average-system error and comes within 40% of the ceiling; it does not match it, and is never better once the disturbance's luck is removed.
  6. Random against designed: 0.0171 against 0.0191 on 15 systems each; the design advantage is real on prediction and invisible here. Pair the experiments and drop the disturbance to see it.
  7. Smoothness: tenfold \lambda halves the input's variation and doubles the tracking error; a property of the optimisation, not of the model.

Part 9

Scope and further reading

Scope. This chapter uses one controller, regularised feedforward by model inversion, as a test of model quality on one reference, with the models of chapters 04 to 06. It does not design feedback, does not treat robustness to model error formally, and does not optimise the identification experiment for the control goal, which is the subject of identification for control [4][5].

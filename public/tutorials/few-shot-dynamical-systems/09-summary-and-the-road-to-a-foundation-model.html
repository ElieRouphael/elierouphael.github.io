Learning to Identify New Dynamical Systems · Chapter 09 of 09

Summary and the road to a foundation model

Eight chapters ago we had a family of nonlinear oscillators and a learner that could only choose an input and read a noisy position. Along the way, a two-number context learned from twenty other systems turned out to identify new systems from a few seconds of data, tell us which experiment to run, warn us, late, when it no longer applied, and control a system it had never seen. This closing chapter runs the whole pipeline on one new system in your browser, collects every number the course verified, and maps each piece onto its counterpart in a real, large-scale foundation model for dynamical systems.

Part 2

Four lessons

  1. Knowledge of similar systems turns a four-parameter problem into a two-parameter one, and that matters most exactly when data are scarce: a factor of six in error at 1.5 s, 10% at 25 s (chapter 05). The caveat: the two numbers can only reach what the family contains, a floor that more data on the new system never lowers.
  2. The value of data depends on the experiment. An information criterion computed from the pretrained model alone, before any measurement, halves the prediction error of a 3 to 12 s experiment by making the system swing through its informative regime (chapter 06). The caveat: it minimises the posterior's volume, not the prediction error, and the posterior it shrinks can be confidently wrong.
  3. Shared knowledge is a double-edged sword. Outside the family it biases the answer, and the same probabilistic machinery can detect this, but late: the misfit test flags about 40% of the systems at the largest shifts tried, after the adapter has already lost its advantage over from-scratch identification (chapter 07). Detection lags degradation because the statistic is dominated by the disturbance, not by the family.
  4. Better models translate into better control, in the same order and by nearly the same ratios as for prediction (chapter 08). The caveat: feedforward passes every model error straight through, the disturbance sets a floor no open-loop input can remove, and the adapted model comes within 40% of the true coefficients rather than matching them.

A shared backbone plus a small system-specific context is the division of labour of every foundation model for dynamical systems. In this course the backbone is a physics equation with a mean and two directions, and the context is two numbers; the gains, the limits and the failure modes are the same ones a large model meets, small enough to be inspected line by line.

Part 4

From the toy to the real thing

The backbone. Here it is a known equation with a mean coefficient vector and two directions. At scale it is a neural state-space or sequence model trained jointly on many systems, and its "directions" are whatever its context pathway can express: a low-rank adapter, a hypernetwork, or an encoder that reads a short record and emits the context directly [1][2]. Chapter 03's lesson survives the change: what the backbone learns is bounded by what can be identified in each training system, and the noise of the per-system estimates must be weighed, not averaged away.

The context and its posterior. Two numbers with a Gaussian prior and a Laplace posterior become a vector with a learned prior and amortised inference. Chapter 04's accounting, data precision plus prior precision, is the same, and so is chapter 06's overconfidence: a posterior computed under the model's own noise assumptions does not know about coloured disturbances or structural error. Calibration is an open problem at every scale [3].

Design, detection and control. Scoring candidate inputs by expected information becomes constrained, sequential design on a plant that must not be driven anywhere unsafe; the misfit threshold becomes out-of-distribution detection on learned features; feedforward tracking becomes model predictive control or policy learning with the adapted model in the loop [4][5]. The order of the questions does not change: can the context be recovered, how sure are we, which data to collect, does the family still apply, and does it all help the task.

Part 5

Open questions this toy problem raises

  1. Identifiability. When can the context of a learned backbone be recovered from partial, noisy measurements, and how much data does it need? Chapter 02's flat valley along \alpha is the four-parameter version of a question a thousand-parameter adapter faces in every direction.
  2. Calibration. The Laplace posterior assumes white noise; real disturbances are coloured. Chapter 06 put the truth 3.5 posterior standard deviations from a designed estimate. How do we obtain uncertainty we can trust for decisions?
  3. Safe excitation. Informative experiments push systems hard: the best inputs of chapter 06 swing the cart to its largest deflections. How do we trade information against state and input constraints, ideally with guarantees?
  4. Adapter capacity. How do we decide online when a small context suffices and when the model must grow? Chapter 07's misfit test is the simplest such rule, and it fires late.
  5. Closing the loop. Identification, uncertainty and control were designed one after the other here. How should they be designed jointly, so that the experiment serves the controller and the controller's margin reflects the identification's uncertainty?

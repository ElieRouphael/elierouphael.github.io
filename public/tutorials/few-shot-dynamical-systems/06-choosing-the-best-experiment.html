Choosing the best experiment

Every experiment so far has been a random multisine. This chapter asks whether the pretrained family can tell us, before touching the new system, which experiment will teach us the most. The tool is the Fisher information of the context, the same matrix that gave chapter 04 its posterior, now read the other way round: as a property of the input. Hundreds of candidate experiments are scored in your browser, the physics behind the winners is visible, and the notebook's sixty-trial study of whether design actually improves predictions is shown with its bootstrap intervals and rerun live.

Part 0

The question

Chapter 05 found that the first few seconds of data decide most of what a context estimate can be. If the experiment is short, it had better be a good one. An experiment is informative if systems with different contexts respond differently to it: if a small change in z barely changes the output, the data cannot tell the contexts apart. The pretrained model can evaluate that for any candidate input, for any plausible context, without a single measurement of the new system. This chapter does exactly that, then checks whether the promise delivers.

Part 1

Fisher information and D-optimal design

The information in an experiment. For input u and context z, the output sensitivity S(u; z) of chapter 04 has entries S_{tj} = \partial \hat q_t / \partial z_j, and the Fisher information of the context is

\mathcal I(u; z) = \hat\sigma^{-2}\, S(u; z)^\top S(u; z) + I, \qquad S_{tj} = \frac{\partial \hat q_t}{\partial z_j},

whose inverse is the Laplace posterior covariance. The identity is the prior's contribution; the first term grows with the length of the experiment and with how strongly the output responds to each context direction. It is a property of the input and the model only: no measurement y appears in it [1].

Averaging over the unknown. The new system's z is not known before the experiment, so the criterion averages over plausible systems, taken to be the 20 training contexts, and maximises the expected log-determinant. This is Bayesian D-optimal design [2][3]:

u^\star = \arg\max_{u \in \mathcal U} \ \frac{1}{|Z|}\sum_{z \in Z} \log\det \mathcal I(u; z).

Why the log-determinant. The determinant of the posterior covariance is proportional to the squared area of the posterior ellipse, so maximising \log\det \mathcal I = -\log\det \operatorname{Cov} minimises the expected (log) volume of the uncertainty region. The logarithm makes the criterion invariant to rescaling the context and additive over independent experiments; for a 2 \times 2 matrix with eigenvalues \lambda_1, \lambda_2 it is simply \log \lambda_1 + \log \lambda_2, so an experiment that leaves one direction at the prior's value of 1 is penalised however much it learns about the other. Alternatives weight the directions differently: A-optimality minimises the trace of the covariance, E-optimality its largest eigenvalue [3].

In practice. The admissible set \mathcal U is "random three-sine inputs of peak 1", and the maximisation is a search: score many candidates, keep the best. With exact sensitivities the score of a 4 s candidate costs 20 simulations with their sensitivity equations, and the browser scores 300 candidates in a fraction of a second.

What is optimal experiment design, here?

In plain words: use the model you already have to predict which input would make different members of the family answer most differently, and run that one.

More precisely: choose the input that maximises a scalar summary of the expected Fisher information of the unknown parameters, averaged over a prior on those parameters. The parameters are the two context numbers, the prior is the training cloud, the summary is the log-determinant, and the search is over random candidates. Nothing of the new system is used: the design is done before the experiment, with the pretrained model only.

Part 2

What makes an experiment informative

The hero demo shows the physics. Scores correlate strongly with how much the mean system moves (0.89 over the notebook's 300 candidates): the best experiments push slowly enough to swing the cart through large, two-sided motions near the resonance band of chapter 01, the worst shake it at high frequency, which the mass filters out, so the sensor sees mostly noise. The notebook's best candidate has its energy at 0.19 Hz, the worst at 0.72 Hz.

But the score is not just "more motion". How the system moves decides which coefficient combinations become distinguishable, and the candidate that moves the mean system most is not the best one: it ranks 53rd of 300 by score. The cleanest demonstration is a single sine.

Two things are visible. A single frequency, even at resonance, scores far below the best three-sine input: the information about z_1 (stiffness and hardening) and z_2 (damping against gain) comes from how amplitude and phase change across frequencies, and one sine gives only one amplitude and one phase. And the sine sweep's own maximum sits just above the band, not inside it, because above resonance the response is governed by the mass line, where stiffness changes the phase quickly. Design criteria capture such things automatically; the rule of thumb "excite the resonance" captures only part of them.

Same system, three experiments

The notebook draws a new system and identifies it three times from 4 s: with the worst candidate, with a random one, and with the designed one. The page adapts the context from the notebook's recorded measurements and draws the three posteriors.

Overconfidence, quantified. The designed experiment's ellipse is the smallest by a factor of 300 in determinant and the furthest from the truth in its own units: 3.5 standard deviations. The random experiment's is at 2.2, the worst experiment's at 1.0. The precision is real, the accuracy is not: the coloured disturbance and the missing quintic term bias the estimate by an amount that does not shrink with the ellipse. Part 4

Does it improve predictions?

The criterion targets the posterior volume of the context, not the prediction error. Whether the promise delivers is an empirical question, and the notebook answers it with 60 new systems per budget. In every trial a fresh pool of 200 candidates is scored, the designed and the worst experiment are picked, and a random experiment is drawn without looking at its score; all three are adapted and validated on a fresh 30 s input. The comparison is fair by construction: the designed input is chosen before the new system is touched, and the random one is never scored.

BudgetRandom, meanDesigned, meanWorst, meanReductionDesigned wins95% bootstrap CI of the mean reductionDesigned worse than worst

The notebook's table, with one column added from its exercise 1. The interval is a percentile bootstrap of the mean paired difference, random minus designed, over 5000 resamples of the 60 trials [5]:

d_n = \text{RMSE}_{\text{random}, n} - \text{RMSE}_{\text{designed}, n}, \qquad \bar d^{(b)} = \frac{1}{60}\sum_{n \in \text{resample}_b} d_n, \qquad \text{CI} = \big[\bar d^{(\cdot)}_{2.5\%},\ \bar d^{(\cdot)}_{97.5\%}\big].

Read the table as a function of the budget.

  1. Very short (1.5 s). No experiment can reveal much in a quarter of an oscillation period. The difference between designed and random is not significant (the interval contains zero, the win rate is 52%, and the designed experiment is worse than the worst one in 43% of trials); the prior does most of the work.
  2. 3 s and more. Design pays off: the designed experiment lowers the mean error by 53%, 55% and 45%, wins about three paired trials in four, and its interval excludes zero. The worst experiments waste most of the data, and get relatively worse as the budget grows, because a long bad experiment is still a bad experiment.
  3. Not every trial. Even at 3 s the designed experiment loses to the worst one in one trial in six. The criterion is an average-case guarantee over the training contexts, not a per-trial one, and a particular system's noise realisation can work against it.

The design is computed from the pretrained model alone, before any data, and it halves the prediction error of a 3 to 12 s experiment. The criterion targets parameter uncertainty, not prediction error, and the posterior it shrinks can be confidently wrong: design tells you where to look, not what you will find.

Part 5

Check your understanding

1. What does the Fisher information of the context depend on, and what does it not depend on?

On the input, the model and the context at which it is evaluated, through the sensitivity S; and on \hat\sigma and the prior. Not on any measurement of the new system. That is why it can be computed before the experiment.

2. Why average over the training contexts?

Because the new system's context is unknown before the experiment, and an input good at one context may be poor at another. Averaging the log-determinant over the 20 training contexts is a Monte Carlo estimate of the expected criterion under the family's prior.

3. Why the log-determinant rather than, say, the trace?

The determinant is the squared volume of the posterior ellipse, so maximising its log minimises the expected uncertainty volume; the log makes the criterion scale-invariant and additive over experiments, and it punishes an experiment that leaves one direction unlearned however good it is on the other. The trace (A-optimality) would let a very well-learned direction compensate for a badly learned one.

4. The best candidate moves the mean system most? True or false.

False. Scores correlate with motion at 0.89, but the candidate with the largest motion ranks 53rd of 300 by score. How the system moves matters: the best inputs excite several frequencies around the resonance band, which separates stiffness from damping and gain.

5. Why does a single sine at resonance score so poorly?

It yields one amplitude and one phase, which cannot pin two context numbers whose effects are told apart by how the response changes across frequencies. Every pure sine in the sweep scores below 7; the best three-sine candidate scores 10.

6. The designed experiment's posterior is the smallest and does not contain the truth. Contradiction?

No. The posterior measures precision under the model's assumptions (white noise, correct model); the coloured disturbance and the quintic term bias the estimate by an amount the ellipse knows nothing about. Higher precision with the same bias means the truth falls further outside, in the ellipse's own units: 3.5 standard deviations for the designed experiment against 1.0 for the worst.

7. Why is the 1.5 s result "not significant"?

The bootstrap interval of the mean paired reduction, [-0.004, +0.008], contains zero, and the designed experiment wins only 52% of the paired trials. In a quarter of a period no input is informative enough for the choice to matter.

8. Is the comparison fair to the random experiment?

Yes: the random input is drawn and never scored, so it is not the pool's median or any other function of the design criterion; the designed input is chosen before the system is drawn; all three experiments see the same system, the same noise process and the same validation input.

Part 6

Exercises

  1. Does the designed experiment stay good for every system? Using the 3 s results, check in what fraction of the 60 trials the designed experiment's error is worse than the worst experiment's. Is "design never hurts" true in practice?
    Hint

    (design_err["designed"][1] > design_err["worst"][1]).mean().

    Solution

    In 17% of the trials, about one in six (the random experiment is worse than the worst one in 47%). The notebook's solution says "a small but nonzero fraction"; one in six is worth stating. The design criterion is an average-case guarantee, scored by averaging over the training contexts, not a per-trial one, and a particular new system's noise realisation can still work against it. The fraction falls to 10% at 6 s and 3% at 12 s.

    i = 1
frac_worse = (design_err["designed"][i] > design_err["worst"][i]).mean()
frac_worse
  2. Scoring candidates without the family. Score the same 300 candidates using only motion, the standard deviation of the average system's predicted response, and pick its best and worst. How similar is the motion-based "best" input to the Fisher-information-based one?
    Hint

    Two random multisines are different signals whatever their quality; compare ranks, not waveforms.

    Solution

    They are different inputs: the motion-best candidate ranks 53rd of 300 by design score, and the design-best ranks 10th by motion. The notebook's solution compares the two waveforms by their sample correlation and calls them "correlated but not identical"; that correlation is -0.07, and the sample correlation between two random multisines says nothing about their quality in any case. It also quotes a score–motion correlation of 0.85 where the notebook prints 0.89. Motion is a decent proxy for the ranking, because large slow swings are what both reward, and an imperfect one, because the design score also cares about which parts of state space the motion visits.

    motion_best, motion_worst = candidates[np.argmax(motion)], candidates[np.argmin(motion)]
rank_by_score = (scores > scores[np.argmax(motion)]).sum() + 1
rank_by_motion = (motion > motion[np.argmax(scores)]).sum() + 1
rank_by_score, rank_by_motion
  3. Write out the criterion for a linear model. If \hat q were linear in z, \hat q = \hat q_0 + S z, show that the Fisher information does not depend on z and that the posterior covariance is exactly \mathcal I^{-1}, so that the design criterion needs no averaging.
    Hint

    Chapter 04's exercise 3 did most of the work.

    Solution

    With S constant, the log posterior is a quadratic in z with Hessian -(\hat\sigma^{-2} S^\top S + I), so the posterior is exactly Gaussian with precision \mathcal I for every z and every y. The expected log-determinant is then just \log\det \mathcal I(u), and D-optimal design is a deterministic function of the input. The averaging over training contexts in the notebook is the price of the nonlinearity of the decoder and the dynamics.

  4. Additivity. Two experiments u_a and u_b are run one after the other on the same system, each from rest. Show that the Fisher information of the combined data is \mathcal I(u_a) + \mathcal I(u_b) - I, and explain why the log-determinant of the sum is not the sum of the log-determinants.
    Hint

    Stack the two sensitivity matrices; the prior enters once.

    Solution

    The combined residual vector stacks the two, so S^\top S adds: \hat\sigma^{-2}(S_a^\top S_a + S_b^\top S_b) + I = \mathcal I(u_a) + \mathcal I(u_b) - I, the prior counted once. Determinants do not add, so two experiments good at the same direction are worth less together than two complementary ones; this is why the best single multisine mixes frequencies, and why sequential design re-scores candidates after each experiment with the information already gathered.

  5. Goal-oriented design. The criterion minimises the posterior volume of z, but the course scores prediction error on a fresh input. Propose a criterion that targets the prediction error of the validation input directly, and say what it would need that D-optimal design does not.
    Hint

    Propagate the posterior covariance of z through the sensitivity of the validation response.

    Solution

    With S_v the sensitivity of the validation response to z, the predicted variance of the validation output is \operatorname{tr}(S_v \mathcal I(u)^{-1} S_v^\top), and one would minimise its expectation over the training contexts. It needs the validation input, or a distribution over the inputs the model will be used for, which D-optimal design does not; it is a form of what the notebook calls goal-oriented design, a research topic of its own, and the same idea underlies model-based control in chapter 08, where the goal is tracking a reference.

Part 8

Scope and further reading

Scope. This chapter covers Bayesian D-optimal input design for the context of a pretrained family, by scoring random candidates, and its empirical validation on prediction error. It does not optimise the input directly (gradient-based design over the signal, or over the frequencies and phases of a multisine), does not design sequentially, and does not target prediction error or a control goal, all of which the exercises point to. Classical input design for linear systems in the frequency domain is the background [4].

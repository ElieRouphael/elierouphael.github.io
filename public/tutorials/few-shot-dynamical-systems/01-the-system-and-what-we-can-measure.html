Learning to Identify New Dynamical Systems · Chapter 01 of 09

The system, and what we can measure

Engineers rarely meet a dynamical system that is completely new: a new motor resembles motors characterised before, a new joint behaves like the other joints of its series. Foundation models for dynamical systems try to exploit exactly that, learning from many systems what systems of a kind share, so that a new one can be understood from very little data. This course builds the smallest version of the idea in which every step can be inspected. This first chapter sets up the plant: a family of nonlinear oscillators, the two hidden design variables behind their coefficients, and the thin slice of all this that the learner is allowed to see. Part 1

The oscillator

The equation. Our systems are carts attached to a nonlinear spring and a damper, pushed by a force-limited actuator. With position q and velocity v,

\dot q = v, \qquad \dot v = -k\,q \;-\; d\,v \;+\; b\,\varphi(u) \;-\; \alpha\,q^3 .

It is a Duffing oscillator with a hardening spring [5], and the four physical coefficients are collected in \eta = [k,\ d,\ b,\ \alpha]^\top.

SymbolMeaning
klinear stiffness; sets the natural frequency \omega_n = \sqrt{k}
ddamping; sets how sharp the resonance is, through \zeta = d / (2\sqrt{k})
binput gain, how strongly the actuator pushes
\alphahardening of the spring at large deflection
\varphi(u) = 0.3 \tanh(u / 0.6)a saturating actuator: commands beyond \pm 1 gain little extra force

The actuator. Near zero, \varphi(u) \approx 0.5\,u; far from zero it flattens at \pm 0.3. Every input in the course is scaled to a peak command of 1, which is already deep into the bend.

Discretisation. The simulator samples at \Delta t = 0.1 s and advances with the semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler scheme [4]: the velocity is updated first, and the position then uses the new velocity. The actuator acts with a one-sample delay, so the force at step t comes from the command u_{t-1}:

v_{t+1} = v_t + \Delta t\,\big(-k q_t - d v_t + b\,\varphi(u_{t-1}) - \alpha q_t^3\big), \qquad q_{t+1} = q_t + \Delta t\,v_{t+1}.

Every experiment starts at rest, deflected by 0.2: x_0 = (q_0, v_0) = (0.2, 0). For the nominal member \eta_0 = (1.2, 0.32, 0.9, 1.0) with the command held at zero, the first step is

v_1 = 0.1\,(-1.2 \cdot 0.2 - 1.0 \cdot 0.2^3) = 0.1\,(-0.24 - 0.008) = -0.0248, \qquad q_1 = 0.2 + 0.1 \cdot (-0.0248) = 0.19752,

and the recursion continues as in the table, computed live. The cubic term contributes \alpha q^2 / k = 0.033 of the linear restoring force at the initial deflection, a few percent: the spring is nearly linear in this operating range, which is why chapter 02 will find \alpha so hard to identify.

step tacceleration at t-1v_tq_t

Released from 0.2 with no input, the nominal member crosses zero at 1.5, 4.3, 7.2 and 10.1 s: a period of about 5.7 s, against 2\pi / \sqrt{k} = 5.74 s for the linearised spring, and the amplitude shrinks by a factor of about 0.40 per period, which is \exp(-2\pi\zeta / \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}) for \zeta = 0.146. The hardening spring would raise the frequency by a factor \sqrt{1 + 3\alpha A^2 / (4k)} = 1.012 at amplitude A = 0.2, about one percent: invisible in a 20 s experiment with noise.

Part 2

What makes it a family

Every system shares the equation above but has its own \eta. In the simulator, the coefficients of a system are generated from two hidden design variables c \in [-1, 1]^2 (think: spring material and cart mass), plus small individual deviations:

\eta = \eta_0 + M c + \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal N\big(0, \operatorname{diag}(0.015, 0.004, 0.008, 0.04)^2\big),
\eta_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1.20 \\ 0.32 \\ 0.90 \\ 1.00 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad M = \begin{pmatrix} 0.55 & 0.12 \\ 0.07 & 0.11 \\ 0.16 & -0.09 \\ 0.30 & 0.45 \end{pmatrix}.

The learner is never told \eta_0, M, c, or even that the family is two-dimensional. It must discover this structure from data; that is chapter 03's job.

What is a family of systems?

In plain words: many systems that obey the same kind of equation but differ in their coefficients, where the differences are not arbitrary: a few hidden design choices explain most of them.

More precisely: a distribution over coefficient vectors \eta concentrated near a low-dimensional manifold, here the affine image \eta_0 + M c of the design square, blurred by the individual deviation \epsilon. The learner's task across the course is to learn this manifold from many systems and then to locate a new system on it from little data.

Frequency and damping. Linearised about rest, with the actuator's small-signal gain \varphi'(0) = 0.5, each member is a second-order system with small-signal gain, natural frequency and damping ratio

\left|\frac{q}{u}\right|(\omega) = \frac{0.5\,b}{\left| k - \omega^2 + i\,d\,\omega \right|}, \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{k}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{d}{2\sqrt{k}}.

Across the design square, k runs from 0.53 to 1.87, so \omega_n runs from 0.73 to 1.37 rad/s and the natural frequency from 0.116 to 0.218 Hz, periods of 4.6 to 8.6 s. Keep this resonance band in mind: it returns in chapter 06, where the best experiments turn out to concentrate their energy there, as frequency-domain identification would advise [3]. The widget maps the whole square into the frequency–damping plane; it is the notebook's exercise 1, with its answer corrected.

The reason is in M: the first row says k moves by 0.55 with c_1 and only 0.12 with c_2, so c_1 sets the frequency. The second row says d moves by 0.07 with c_1 and 0.11 with c_2. Relative to their nominal values, d changes by 0.11 / 0.32 = 34\% per unit of c_2 while \sqrt k changes by only 5\%, so c_2 sets the damping ratio; along c_1, the 22\% of d is almost exactly cancelled by the roughly 23\% of \sqrt k, and \zeta barely moves. Frequency and damping are set by different design variables, and the hidden geometry chapter 03 will recover from data is this two-dimensional square.

What makes it realistic. The true systems also contain effects the learner's model will not have: a weak quintic spring term -\gamma q^5 with \gamma \in [0.035, 0.047], a small constant force bias r = 0.006\,c_1 + 0.002\,\xi, and a coloured random disturbance w_t (vibrations, say) acting on the cart. The one-sample actuator delay, in contrast, is in the learner's model.

Part 3

What the learner sees, and what it does not

In the laboratory, the learner can choose the input u_t and read a position sensor y_t = q_t + \nu_t with \nu_t \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_y^2), \sigma_y = 0.01. It does not see the velocity, the disturbance, or the coefficients.

QuantitySimulator knowsLearner receives
coefficients \eta, hidden design variables cyesno
input u_tyesyes, it chooses it
position q_tyesonly noisy y_t
velocity v_t, disturbance w_tyesno
quintic term, force biasyesno, not even in its model

The learner's model is the discretised equation without the unknown extra effects:

\hat v_{t+1} = \hat v_t + \Delta t\,\big(-k\hat q_t - d\hat v_t + b\,\varphi(u_{t-1}) - \alpha \hat q_t^3\big), \qquad \hat q_{t+1} = \hat q_t + \Delta t\,\hat v_{t+1}.

This mismatch is deliberate. Real models are always wrong in some way [2], and a method is only interesting if it degrades gracefully when that happens. The notebook records one experiment on the central member; the widget shows what the learner gets from it, and what it does not.

RMSE(true position, disturbance-free)– RMSE(measurement, true position)– RMSE(measurement, disturbance-free)–

The disturbance. It is a first-order autoregressive process,

w_{t+1} = \rho\,w_t + \sigma_w\,\xi_t, \qquad \xi_t \sim \mathcal N(0, 1), \qquad \rho = 0.8, \quad \sigma_w = 0.01,

added to the acceleration. Its stationary variance follows from \operatorname{Var}(w) = \rho^2 \operatorname{Var}(w) + \sigma_w^2:

\sigma_{w,\infty} = \frac{\sigma_w}{\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}} = \frac{0.01}{0.6} = 0.0167, \qquad \tau = -\frac{\Delta t}{\ln \rho} = 0.45 \text{ s}.

A disturbance that keeps its sign for half a second is integrated twice by the oscillator before it averages out, so a small force noise becomes a visible position error. The notebook's exercise 2 asks how much; the widget runs it live, with its own random numbers, and lets you change the colour.

Part 4

How models will be judged

The gap between the true position and the dashed line in the experiment above is caused by the coloured disturbance alone. No model can predict it in advance: it is a fresh random draw on every run. A model that happened to track one realisation would merely be lucky. So, throughout the course, models are judged by how well they predict the system's disturbance-free response to a new input:

Score a model on the response the true system would have had, without its disturbance, to an input it has not seen. That measures the quality of the model, not our luck with the noise.

In numbers: on the recorded experiment the disturbance moves the position by RMSE 0.0165, more than the sensor noise's 0.0101. A model scored against the raw measurement could never do better than about 0.018 on that run, and most of that floor would be noise. Scored against the disturbance-free response, a perfect model scores zero, the average-system model will score about 0.056 in chapter 04, and the question of the whole course, how close to zero a few seconds of data can take you, becomes well posed.

A simulation privilege. The disturbance-free response is available because we own the simulator. On a real plant one would estimate the same quantity by averaging repeated experiments, or accept a noise floor in every score. The course keeps the clean version so that differences between methods are not buried under it.

Part 5

Check your understanding

1. Why does the frequency response carry a factor 0.5 in front of b?

Because the actuator enters as b\,\varphi(u) and \varphi'(0) = 0.3 / 0.6 = 0.5: linearised about rest, one unit of command gives half a unit of force before the gain b. The curve is therefore a small-signal statement; a command of peak 1 already loses 7% to saturation.

2. Why is a command of 2 almost wasted compared with a command of 1?

\varphi(1) = 0.279 is 93% of the force limit 0.3 and \varphi(2) = 0.299 is 99.7%. Doubling the command adds 7% of force. Amplitude is not where an experiment's information comes from in this family; frequency content is (chapter 06).

3. What is semi-implicit about the Euler step, and why use it?

The velocity is updated explicitly, but the position update uses the already-updated velocity. For an undamped oscillator this symplectic variant preserves a slightly modified energy instead of pumping it up as the fully explicit scheme does, so long simulations stay bounded at \Delta t = 0.1 s [4]. The learner's model uses the same scheme, so discretisation is not part of the mismatch.

4. Which design variable sets the natural frequency, and which the damping ratio?

c_1 sets the frequency (k moves by 0.55 per unit of c_1) and c_2 sets the damping ratio (d moves by 34% of its nominal value per unit of c_2, while \sqrt k moves by 5%). Along c_1 the changes in d and \sqrt k nearly cancel in \zeta. The notebook's exercise 1 solution gets this wrong; see the widget in Part 2.

5. What exactly does the learner receive from an experiment?

The command sequence it chose, u_0, \dots, u_{T-1}, and T + 1 noisy position readings y_0, \dots, y_T. No velocity, no disturbance, no coefficients. Everything in later chapters is built from these two sequences.

6. In which ways is the learner's model wrong, and in which is it right?

Wrong: it has no quintic term, no force bias and no disturbance. Right: the same equation structure, the same actuator curve, the same one-sample delay and the same integration scheme. The mismatch is small but structural, which is the realistic case.

7. How large is the disturbance in steady state, and how long does it remember itself?

Standard deviation \sigma_w / \sqrt{1 - \rho^2} = 0.0167, larger than the per-step kick 0.01, and correlation time -\Delta t / \ln 0.8 = 0.45 s, about four and a half samples. The oscillator integrates such a slowly varying force efficiently.

8. Why not judge models on how well they predict the measured y?

Because y contains a noise realisation (RMSE 0.0101 on the recorded run) and a disturbance realisation (0.0165) that no model can know. Scoring against them sets a floor of about 0.018 that hides differences between methods. The disturbance-free response of the true system to a new input has no such floor.

9. Where is the resonance band of the family, in hertz and seconds?

Natural frequencies from 0.116 Hz (corner (-1, -1)) to 0.218 Hz (corner (+1, +1)), periods from 8.6 s down to 4.6 s. A 2 s experiment, chapter 04's budget, covers less than half a period of any member.

Part 6

Exercises

  1. A stiffer, less damped corner of the family. Draw 200 random design variables c uniformly in [-1, 1]^2, compute the corresponding \eta with sample_system(rng, c), and scatter the natural frequency \sqrt k against the damping ratio d / (2\sqrt k). Which corner of c-space gives the highest-frequency systems, and which the most lightly damped?
    Hint

    Colour the points by c_1, then by c_2, and fit \omega_n and \zeta as affine functions of c.

    Solution

    With the notebook's random stream, the fits are \omega_n \approx 0.253\,c_1 + 0.055\,c_2 + 1.085 and \zeta \approx -0.003\,c_1 + 0.043\,c_2 + 0.147; the correlation of \zeta with c_2 is 0.99 and with c_1 only −0.09. So the stiffest, highest-frequency systems sit at c_1 \to +1, and the most lightly damped at c_2 \to -1: two different edges. They are not the same corner. The highest-frequency corner (+1, +1) is in fact the most heavily damped (\zeta = 0.183), and the lightest damping, \zeta = 0.096, belongs to the slowest corner (-1, -1). The notebook's printed solution claims that c_1 \to +1 gives both; it does not, because along c_1 the relative growth of d (22% per unit) is cancelled by that of \sqrt k (23%).

    cs = rng.uniform(-1, 1, (200, 2))
etas = np.array([sample_system(rng, c)["eta"] for c in cs])
k, d = etas[:, 0], etas[:, 1]
wn, zeta = np.sqrt(k), d / (2 * np.sqrt(k))
A = np.c_[cs, np.ones(200)]
print(np.linalg.lstsq(A, wn, rcond=None)[0], np.linalg.lstsq(A, zeta, rcond=None)[0])
print(np.corrcoef(zeta, cs[:, 0])[0, 1], np.corrcoef(zeta, cs[:, 1])[0, 1])
plt.scatter(wn, zeta, c=cs[:, 1], cmap="viridis", s=12); plt.colorbar(label="c2")
  2. How much does the disturbance matter? For one system, simulate the same input 50 times with noise=True and once with noise=False. Plot the disturbance-free trajectory against the envelope (min and max across the 50 runs). On what fraction of the 20 s experiment does the spread exceed the sensor noise alone (\sigma_y = 0.01)?
    Hint

    Take the standard deviation across runs at each sample; the band starts at zero because every run starts from the same state.

    Solution

    With the notebook's stream, the spread exceeds 0.01 on 92% of the samples, from t = 1.7 s onwards, with mean spread 0.022 and peak 0.031. Over 20 other seeds the fraction ranges from 49% to 92%, median 90%. The disturbance is coloured (\rho = 0.8) and the oscillator integrates it, so it builds up over a run rather than averaging out sample by sample: the gap in the experiment figure is dominated by the disturbance, not by sensor noise. The widget in Part 3 repeats the experiment live and lets you set \rho = 0 to see the band shrink.

    s = sample_system(rng); u = multisine(200, rng)
clean, _ = simulate(s, u, rng, noise=False)
noisy = np.array([simulate(s, u, rng)[0] for _ in range(50)])
spread = noisy.std(axis=0)
print(f"fraction of samples with spread > sensor noise: {(spread > 0.01).mean():.0%}")
  3. Static deflection. Hold the command at u = 1 on the nominal member \eta_0. Where does the cart settle, and how much does the hardening term move the answer?
    Hint

    At rest the acceleration is zero: solve k q + \alpha q^3 = b\,\varphi(1).

    Solution

    The force is b\,\varphi(1) = 0.9 \times 0.2793 = 0.2514. The linear spring alone would give q = 0.2514 / 1.2 = 0.2095; with the cubic term the root of 1.2 q + q^3 = 0.2514 is q = 0.2026, 3% less. The hardening term is a few-percent correction throughout the operating range, which is why chapter 02 will struggle to pin \alpha down.

    from scipy.optimize import brentq
k, d, b, a = dynutils._ETA0
f = b * dynutils.actuator(1.0)
print(f / k, brentq(lambda q: k * q + a * q**3 - f, 0, 1))   # 0.2095  0.2026
  4. Does the one-sample delay matter? Predict the central member's response to the notebook's 30 s demo input with the learner's model, once with the delay and once with the command applied immediately. Compare the two.
    Hint

    Shifting the input one sample earlier removes the delay; measure the RMSE between the two predictions against the RMS of the motion.

    Solution

    The two predictions differ by RMSE 0.0166 (largest difference 0.038) on a motion of RMS 0.149: about 11%, the same size as the disturbance's effect. A learner that ignored the delay would carry a model error as large as the irreducible one, and would try to absorb it into d and b. This is why the delay is kept in the learner's model.

    with_delay = predict(eta, u_demo)
no_delay = predict(eta, np.r_[u_demo[1:], u_demo[-1]])
print(rmse(with_delay, no_delay), np.abs(with_delay - no_delay).max(), np.sqrt(np.mean(with_delay**2)))
  5. Read the resonance from a step. In the hero demo, switch to the unit step and estimate each member's period and decay per period from the swings. Check them against 2\pi / \sqrt k and \exp(-2\pi\zeta / \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}).
    Hint

    Period from successive peaks; decay from the ratio of successive overshoots about the final value.

    Solution

    For the central member, the linear period is 5.74 s and the decay per period \exp(-2\pi \cdot 0.146 / 0.989) = 0.40; for the corner (-1, -1), 8.6 s and 0.54; for (+1, +1), 4.6 s and 0.31. The free decay of the nominal member crosses zero at 1.5, 4.3, 7.2 and 10.1 s, a period of 5.7 s. Reading the period from a step is easy; reading \alpha is not, because it changes the frequency by about one percent at these amplitudes.

Part 7

The chapter on one page

  1. The plant: a Duffing oscillator \dot v = -kq - dv + b\varphi(u) - \alpha q^3 with a saturating actuator \varphi(u) = 0.3\tanh(u / 0.6), sampled at 0.1 s by semi-implicit Euler with a one-sample input delay, started at (0.2, 0).
  2. The family: \eta = \eta_0 + Mc + \epsilon with two hidden design variables; c_1 sets the natural frequency (0.116 to 0.218 Hz), c_2 the damping ratio (0.096 to 0.205).
  3. The extras: a quintic spring term, a force bias and a coloured disturbance (\rho = 0.8, stationary sd 0.0167, correlation time 0.45 s) that the learner's model omits; the model is deliberately misspecified.
  4. The learner: chooses u_t, reads y_t = q_t + \nu_t with \sigma_y = 0.01, and sees nothing else.
  5. The numbers: on the recorded experiment the disturbance alone moves the position by RMSE 0.0165, the sensor noise by 0.0101; across 50 repeats the spread exceeds the sensor noise on 92% of samples.
  6. The yardstick: every chapter scores a model on the disturbance-free response of the true system to a new input, which isolates model quality from measurement luck.

Scope and further reading

Scope. This chapter only sets the stage: the plant, the hidden structure of the family, the learner's observation model and the scoring rule. No estimation happens yet.

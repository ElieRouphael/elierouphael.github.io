Identifying one system from scratch

Chapter 01 built the simulator and set the rules: the learner chooses an input, reads a noisy position sensor, and fits a model that is missing two of the true system's effects. This chapter asks the most direct question first. Treating every system on its own, with no help from any other system, how well can we identify it? The answer is the baseline every later chapter has to beat, and it comes with a lesson that will not go away: three of the four coefficients are easy, and the fourth is not identified at all. Part 0

The question

One system, one experiment, four unknown coefficients \eta = (k, d, b, \alpha). The learner has the input it chose, u_0, \dots, u_{T-1}, and T + 1 noisy positions y_0, \dots, y_T. The "from-scratch" estimate uses nothing else: no other system, no prior, and a starting guess that is deliberately generic. Whatever a pretrained family buys in chapters 03 to 08 has to be measured against this.

The test system. The notebook draws one member, \eta = (0.852, 0.311, 0.777, 0.922) at design variables c = (-0.64, 0.28), a softish, moderately damped oscillator with natural period 2\pi / \sqrt{k} = 6.8 s, and repeats the same experiment 30 times at two lengths: 4 s (40 samples) and 40 s (400 samples). The hero demo lets you fit any length between 1 and 40 s.

Part 1

Output-error identification

The estimator. Given one experiment, output-error identification chooses the coefficients whose freely simulated position best matches the measurements [1]:

\hat\eta = \arg\min_{\eta}\ \sum_{t=0}^{T} \big(y_t - \hat q_t(\eta; u)\big)^2 ,

where \hat q_t(\eta; u) is the learner's model of chapter 01 run from the known initial state (0.2, 0). The model is simulated over the whole record; the data enter only through the residuals.

Why not regress on the equation? The alternative, equation error, rearranges the model into a regression on the measured signals: \ddot q = -kq - d\dot q + b\varphi(u) - \alpha q^3 is linear in \eta, so one could estimate it by ordinary least squares if \dot q and \ddot q were available. They are not; they would have to be obtained by differencing a noisy position. A first difference (y_{t+1} - y_t) / \Delta t has noise variance 2\sigma_y^2 / \Delta t^2, a second difference 6\sigma_y^2 / \Delta t^4:

\text{sd of velocity noise} = \frac{\sqrt 2\,\sigma_y}{\Delta t} = 0.141, \qquad \text{sd of acceleration noise} = \frac{\sqrt 6\,\sigma_y}{\Delta t^2} = 2.45,

against accelerations of order k q \approx 0.3 in these experiments. With \sigma_y = 0.01 and \Delta t = 0.1 s, the regression would be fitting noise eight times larger than its signal (the page computes these as and ). Output error never differentiates the data; the price is a nonlinear least-squares problem instead of a linear one.

What it estimates, statistically. Under white measurement noise and a correct model, output error is the maximum-likelihood estimator. Neither condition holds here: the true system has a quintic term, a force bias and a coloured disturbance that the model lacks. Under process noise the likelihood would call for a state estimator such as an extended Kalman filter; the course keeps the simpler estimator for transparency, and its bias is part of what the experiments measure [7].

Bounds and the starting guess. The optimisation runs inside physically sensible bounds, k \in [0.05, 5], d \in [0.01, 2], b \in [0.05, 3], \alpha \in [0, 5], and starts from the generic guess (1, 0.5, 1, 0.5), deliberately not taken from the family structure, so that from-scratch identification uses no information about it. The bounds will matter: when a coefficient is not identified, the solver parks it on a bound.

What is output-error identification?

In plain words: guess the coefficients, simulate the model from the start with the recorded input, compare the simulated output with the recorded one, and adjust the coefficients until the two agree as well as they can.

More precisely: the nonlinear least-squares problem above, in which the residual vector r(\eta) = \hat q(\eta; u) - y is a smooth function of \eta through the recursion of the model. Its minimiser is the maximum-likelihood estimate under white Gaussian output noise; its local geometry, through the Jacobian J = \partial r / \partial \eta, tells how well each coefficient is determined.

Part 2

Solving the least-squares problem

Sensitivities. The Jacobian needs \partial \hat q_t / \partial \eta. Differentiating the model recursion gives a second recursion, run alongside the first, for the sensitivities s_t = \partial \hat q_t / \partial \eta and \rho_t = \partial \hat v_t / \partial \eta (row vectors of length 4, starting at zero because the initial state does not depend on \eta):

\rho_{t+1} = \rho_t + \Delta t\,\big[(-k - 3\alpha \hat q_t^2)\, s_t - d\,\rho_t + (-\hat q_t,\ -\hat v_t,\ \varphi(u_{t-1}),\ -\hat q_t^3)\big], \qquad s_{t+1} = s_t + \Delta t\,\rho_{t+1}.

The bracket's last term is the direct dependence of the acceleration on each coefficient; the rest propagates earlier changes through the dynamics. The page uses this exact Jacobian; it agrees with central finite differences to 7 \times 10^{-11}. SciPy's least_squares, which the notebook calls, builds its Jacobian by finite differences instead.

Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt. Near a minimum the cost f(\eta) = \tfrac12 \lVert r(\eta) \rVert^2 has gradient J^\top r and Hessian J^\top J + \sum_t r_t \nabla^2 r_t; Gauss–Newton drops the second-order term, which is small when residuals are small or nearly linear, and steps by \delta = -(J^\top J)^{-1} J^\top r. Levenberg–Marquardt damps the step [3][4]:

\big(J^\top J + \lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(J^\top J)\big)\,\delta = -J^\top r, \qquad \lambda \downarrow \text{ after a successful step},\ \lambda \uparrow \text{ after a failed one},

interpolating between Gauss–Newton (\lambda \to 0) and scaled gradient descent (\lambda large). Marquardt's diagonal scaling makes the method invariant to rescaling the coefficients, which is what SciPy's x_scale="jac" option provides for its trust-region-reflective solver [5]. The page's solver projects each step onto the box and freezes a coordinate that sits on a bound with the gradient pushing outwards; on the notebook's recorded experiments it reaches the same minimum as SciPy to five decimals in 6 to 14 iterations.

The covariance of the estimate. Linearising at the minimum and assuming white noise of variance \sigma^2, the Gauss–Newton covariance is

\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\eta) \approx \hat\sigma^2 \,(J^\top J)^{-1}, \qquad \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{T + 1}\sum_t r_t^2,

which the notebook's fit_from_scratch(..., return_cov=True) returns and chapter 03 will use to whiten the coefficients. Its square-rooted diagonal gives the standard errors drawn as whiskers in the hero demo. Part 5 shows why they should be read with suspicion here.

Part 3

Short experiments versus long ones

The notebook repeats the whole procedure 30 times at each length, with a fresh random three-sine input and fresh noise every time, and histograms the estimates. The 60 estimates below are SciPy's; the median relative errors are recomputed from them on the page.

Two lessons carry through the rest of the course.

  1. Short experiments leave the coefficients poorly determined. Four seconds is shorter than one oscillation period of this member (6.8 s), so many coefficient combinations explain the data equally well and the estimates scatter: median errors of 11% in k, 22% in d, 9% in b.
  2. Some coefficients are hard to identify even with plenty of data. With 40 s, k, d and b are within 6 to 9%, but \alpha's median error is 100% in the notebook's run, and between 44% and 99% over five other seeds. The hardening term only matters at large deflections, and it competes with the unmodelled quintic term and the disturbance. Identifiability depends on the experiment as much as on its length.

What this means for prediction, the yardstick of chapter 01: on a fresh 30 s validation input, the 4 s fits predict the disturbance-free response with a median RMSE of 0.027 (best 0.008, worst 0.25), the 40 s fits with 0.012, the true coefficients with 0.004, and the generic guess, which is just an average oscillator, with 0.044. A new system gives us little data. The obvious question, and the subject of the rest of the course: can other systems help?

Part 4

Why the hardening term will not identify

A coefficient is identified when changing it changes the predicted output by more than the noise can hide [6]. The cleanest way to see how much the data constrain \alpha is the profile of the cost: fix \alpha, re-optimise k, d, b, and plot the best achievable residual sum of squares against \alpha. Computed live for the notebook's recorded experiments:

In the 40 s record the deflection reaches 0.56, where the cubic term is \alpha q^2 / k \approx 0.34 of the linear restoring force; averaged over the record it is a few percent, and the quintic term and the bias, which the model cannot represent, are of the same order. The disturbance adds a residual of RMS 0.019, twice the sensor noise, and it is coloured, so it does not average out: it tilts the flat valley one way or the other, and the estimate slides to a bound. Equation-level flatness plus a structured residual is the recipe for an unidentified coefficient. Two of the notebook's exercises probe this, and both of their printed solutions need correcting.

Exercise 1: does the starting guess matter? If the valley were so flat that the solver stopped wherever it started, different guesses would give different estimates. The page refits the notebook's exercise experiment from the generic guess, from near the truth and from the far corner of the box:

Part 5

Error bars that lie

The Gauss–Newton covariance assumes white residuals. Here the residual is dominated by a coloured disturbance, and the formula answers the wrong question: it reports how precisely the coefficients would be known if the residual were fresh noise at every sample, which it is not. The notebook's 40 s fits make the point in numbers:

CoefficientMedian Gauss–Newton SEActual sd over 30 fitsRatio
k0.0110.0777.0
d0.00560.0529.4
b0.00950.0778.1
\alpha0.161.298.0

The claimed precision is about eight times too optimistic for every coefficient. Two things are going on. The residual's effective number of independent samples is far below T + 1 because the disturbance has a correlation time of 0.45 s and the oscillator stretches it further; and the disturbance biases each fit differently, so the estimates scatter for a reason the within-fit covariance cannot see. For 4 s fits the standard errors are enormous (a median of 6.3 on \alpha, seven times its value), which at least says the right thing: nothing is known about \alpha.

With plenty of data, three coefficients are determined to a few percent and the fourth not at all; with little data, none is. The within-fit error bars understate the scatter by an order of magnitude whenever the model is wrong in a correlated way. Both facts motivate chapter 03: learn from many systems which combinations of coefficients actually occur, and use that instead of the data alone.

Part 6

Check your understanding

1. What does "output error" mean, as opposed to "equation error"?

Output error compares the measured output with the output of the model simulated freely from the initial state; equation error compares the two sides of the model equation evaluated on the measured signals. The first needs a nonlinear solver but never differentiates the data; the second is linear in the coefficients but needs velocity and acceleration, whose noise is 14 and 245 times the position noise at this sampling rate.

2. Why is the estimator run inside bounds and from a generic guess?

Bounds keep the coefficients physical (stiffness and damping positive, no negative hardening) and stop an unidentified coefficient from running off; the generic guess (1, 0.5, 1, 0.5) makes sure the baseline uses no knowledge of the family. When a coefficient is not identified, it lands on a bound, which is exactly what \alpha does in a third of the fits.

3. What are the sensitivity equations and why run them instead of finite differences?

They are the model recursion differentiated with respect to each coefficient, run alongside the state. They give the Jacobian exactly, in one pass, at the cost of four extra state pairs; finite differences need five simulations and carry truncation and round-off error. SciPy uses finite differences here because the notebook passes no Jacobian, and the two solvers still meet at the same minimum.

4. What does the Levenberg–Marquardt damping \lambda do?

It blends the Gauss–Newton step, fast near a minimum, with a scaled gradient step, safe far from it. After a step that lowers the cost, \lambda shrinks; after one that fails, it grows and the step shortens. Marquardt's diagonal scaling keeps the method indifferent to the units of each coefficient.

5. Why does \alpha stay unidentified with 40 s of data?

Because its effect on the output is a few percent of the restoring force at these deflections, the same size as the unmodelled quintic term, the bias and the disturbance. The profile of the cost along \alpha changes by about a quarter over the whole range [0, 5], and a coloured residual tilts it towards one bound or the other. Longer records of the same kind do not help; a different experiment, with larger deflections and less disturbance, would.

6. Do different starting guesses give different estimates on a 4 s experiment?

No. The generic guess, a guess near the truth and the far corner of the box all converge to the same estimate with the same residual, on the notebook's exercise experiment and on all 30 of its 4 s experiments. The scatter across experiments comes from the data, as the notebook says, but through the minimum moving with the noise, not through the solver stopping in different places.

7. Does a ten-times quieter sensor make \alpha identifiable in 4 s?

Not by itself: with the disturbance kept, the median error of \alpha stays around 100%. Switching the disturbance off alone does not help either. Both together, which is what the notebook's helper silently does, cut every coefficient's error several-fold and bring \alpha to roughly 40 to 55%; over 40 s without either, it is known to a few percent.

8. Why are the Gauss–Newton standard errors eight times too small at 40 s?

They assume white residuals. The disturbance is coloured, so the residual has far fewer independent samples than T + 1, and each realisation biases the fit in its own direction. The formula measures precision given the model is right; the scatter measures what happens when it is not.

Part 7

Exercises

  1. How much does the guess matter? Refit the 4 s experiments from three different starting guesses: the generic one, one close to the true system, and one at the opposite corner of the bounds. Does the scatter of the resulting estimates change?
    Hint

    Compare not only the estimates but the residual sums of squares. If they agree to all digits, the solver found the same minimum.

    Solution

    It does not change at all. On the notebook's exercise experiment all three guesses return (0.9307, 0.2660, 0.7186, 0) with the same residual, and across the 30 four-second experiments the median relative errors are identical for the three guesses (11.1%, 22.1%, 8.6%, 103.4%). The cost surface is flat along \alpha, but each data set has one minimum and the solver finds it. The notebook's printed solution says different starting points "can converge to different, equally-fitting solutions"; on this problem they do not.

    def fit_from(y, u, guess):
    residual = lambda eta: predict(eta, u) - y
    return least_squares(residual, guess, bounds=(LOW, HIGH), x_scale="jac").x

u4 = multisine(40, rng); _, y4 = simulate(test, u4, rng)
guesses = {"generic": GENERIC_GUESS, "near truth": test["eta"] * 1.05, "far corner": HIGH * 0.9}
{name: fit_from(y4, u4, g) for name, g in guesses.items()}
  2. A cleaner sensor. Repeat the 4 s study with the sensor noise cut to a tenth (\sigma_y = 0.001). Does better identifiability of \alpha follow?
    Hint

    Look at what simulate(..., noise=False) switches off before you add the small noise back.

    Solution

    With the notebook's helper, the median errors drop from (11.1, 22.1, 8.6, 103) percent to (3.5, 5.3, 1.9, 38): every coefficient improves several-fold, \alpha included, which is more than the "a little" in the notebook's solution. But the helper simulates with noise=False, which removes the coloured disturbance as well as the sensor noise, so this is a quiet sensor and a quiet plant. The page's widget shows that either change alone leaves \alpha at about 100% error in 4 s. It is the whole residual, disturbance included, that hides a term worth a few percent of the force. Over 40 s with neither noise source, \alpha is within 5%.

    def simulate_low_noise(system, u, rng):
    q, _ = simulate(system, u, rng, noise=False)
    return q, q + 0.001 * rng.normal(size=q.size)

low_noise = np.array([fit_from_scratch(simulate_low_noise(test, u, rng)[1], u)
                      for u in (multisine(40, rng) for _ in range(30))])
np.median(np.abs(low_noise - test["eta"]) / test["eta"], axis=0)
  3. Derive the sensitivity equations. Differentiate the model recursion of chapter 01 with respect to k and check your result against a finite difference on one experiment.
    Hint

    Both \hat q_t and \hat v_t depend on k; apply the chain rule to -k\hat q_t and to -\alpha \hat q_t^3.

    Solution

    With s_t = \partial \hat q_t / \partial k and \rho_t = \partial \hat v_t / \partial k: \rho_{t+1} = \rho_t + \Delta t\,(-\hat q_t - k s_t - d \rho_t - 3\alpha \hat q_t^2 s_t), s_{t+1} = s_t + \Delta t\,\rho_{t+1}, from s_0 = \rho_0 = 0. The -\hat q_t is the direct effect, the rest the propagated one. For d, b, \alpha the direct terms are -\hat v_t, \varphi(u_{t-1}), -\hat q_t^3. On the notebook's 40 s experiment the recursion agrees with a central difference of step 10^{-6} to 7 \times 10^{-11}.

    def predict_jac(eta, u):
    k, d, b, a = eta; q, v = X0; s = np.zeros(4); r = np.zeros(4)
    out, J = [q], [s.copy()]
    for t in range(len(u)):
        f = actuator(u[t - 1]) if t else 0.0
        r = r + DT * ((-k - 3 * a * q**2) * s - d * r + np.array([-q, -v, f, -q**3]))
        v = v + DT * (-k * q - d * v + b * f - a * q**3); q = q + DT * v
        s = s + DT * r; out.append(q); J.append(s.copy())
    return np.array(out), np.array(J)
  4. Error bars against scatter. For the 40 s fits, compare the median Gauss–Newton standard error of each coefficient with the standard deviation of the 30 estimates. What ratio do you find, and why?
    Hint

    fit_from_scratch(y, u, return_cov=True) returns the covariance; take the square root of its diagonal.

    Solution

    Median standard errors (0.011, 0.0056, 0.0095, 0.16) against actual standard deviations (0.077, 0.052, 0.077, 1.29): ratios of 7 to 9 for every coefficient. The covariance formula assumes independent residuals of variance \hat\sigma^2; the actual residual is a coloured disturbance with a correlation time of several samples, amplified by the oscillator, and it biases each fit differently. Treat these error bars as a lower bound on the uncertainty whenever the model is misspecified.

  5. Prediction, not coefficients. Score each of the 60 fits on the disturbance-free response of the test system to a fresh 30 s input, as chapter 01 prescribes. How do 4 s and 40 s compare, and where do the true coefficients and the generic guess sit?
    Hint

    Simulate the true system with noise=False on a new multisine(300, ...) and use rmse(predict(eta_hat, u_val), q_val).

    Solution

    Median validation RMSE 0.027 for the 4 s fits (range 0.008 to 0.25) and 0.012 for the 40 s fits (0.004 to 0.083); the true coefficients give 0.004 and the generic guess 0.044. Even the 40 s fits are three times worse than the truth on average, and the worst 4 s fit is worse than not fitting at all. Chapter 04 will reach 0.014 from 2 s of data by using other systems.

Part 8

The chapter on one page

  1. Output error: fit the freely simulated output to the measurements; never difference noisy data, whose velocity and acceleration noise would be 0.14 and 2.45 against accelerations of 0.3.
  2. The solver: Levenberg–Marquardt on an exact Jacobian from the sensitivity equations, inside physical bounds, from a generic guess; it meets SciPy's answer to five decimals.
  3. 4 s of data: median errors 11%, 22%, 9% in k, d, b; \alpha anywhere in [0, 5].
  4. 40 s of data: 6%, 9%, 6%; \alpha still at 100% median error, with a third of the estimates on a bound.
  5. Why: the cubic term is worth a few percent of the force, the size of the unmodelled effects; the cost profile along \alpha is flat and a coloured residual tilts it.
  6. Corrections: starting guesses do not change the estimates; a quieter sensor helps \alpha only together with a quieter plant.
  7. Error bars: Gauss–Newton standard errors are eight times too small under the coloured disturbance.
  8. Prediction: 4 s fits reach a validation RMSE of 0.027, 40 s fits 0.012, the truth 0.004; the question for the rest of the course is how far other systems can close that gap from little data.

Part 9

Scope and further reading

Scope. This chapter covers output-error identification of one system from one experiment: the estimator, its solution by damped Gauss–Newton with bounds, the Gauss–Newton covariance, and an honest look at identifiability through repeated experiments and cost profiles. It does not use any other system; that begins in chapter 03, which identifies twenty systems this way and asks what their coefficients have in common. State estimators that would handle process noise properly are out of scope throughout the course.

Part 1

The model: a line plus noise

We have n observations. Observation i has an input x_i and an output y_i. Here x_i is the weight of car i in kg, y_i is its CO2 emission rate in g/km, and n = 36. The model says that the output is a straight-line function of the input, plus a random error:

y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n.
  • \beta_0 is the intercept: the height of the line at x = 0. It has the units of y (g/km).
  • \beta_1 is the slope: the change in y when x grows by one unit (g/km per kg).
  • \varepsilon_i is the error: everything that pushes y_i off the line and is not captured by x_i (engine tuning, fuel type, measurement error). It averages zero.

The true \beta_0 and \beta_1 are unknown; a hat, as in \hat\beta_0 and \hat\beta_1, marks an estimate computed from data. Any candidate line gives each point a prediction \hat y_i = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_i and a residual e_i = y_i - \hat y_i: the vertical gap between the point and the line, positive above the line and negative below.

A good line makes the residuals small. Simply adding them fails, because positive and negative gaps cancel: in the demo above, press flat line at the mean, and the residuals sum to zero although that line ignores the weight entirely. Least squares squares each residual first, then adds [1], [2]:

S(\beta_0, \beta_1) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\big)^2.

S turns every candidate pair (\beta_0, \beta_1) into one number, the total squared miss, and the best line is the pair that makes it smallest. Squaring punishes a gap of 2 four times as hard as a gap of 1. It also makes S a smooth bowl in the two coefficients, so calculus can find its lowest point exactly. (The name regression is older than the method's use in machine learning: it comes from Galton's studies of heredity [3].)

What is linear regression?

In plain words: drawing the straight line, or the flat plane, through a cloud of points that misses them least, where the miss is the total of the squared vertical gaps.

More precisely: estimating the coefficients \beta of a model y = X\beta + \varepsilon that is linear in \beta, by minimising the sum of squared residuals \lVert y - X\beta \rVert^2; the minimiser solves the normal equations X^\top X \hat\beta = X^\top y [4], [5].

Residual
The vertical gap between a point and the fitted line, e_i = y_i - \hat y_i.
Least squares
Choosing the line that makes the sum of the squared residuals smallest.
Intercept, slope
The line's height at x = 0, and its rise per unit of x.
R^2
The fraction of the variation in y that the fitted line explains.
Part 2

Deriving the best line

At the bottom of a smooth bowl the slope is zero in every direction (chapter 02 develops this idea). So we set both partial derivatives of S to zero. The chain rule gives the derivative of a square (\dots)^2 as 2(\dots) times the derivative of what is inside.

The intercept. The inside, y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i, has derivative -1 with respect to \beta_0:

\frac{\partial S}{\partial \beta_0} = -2\sum_{i} \big(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\big) = 0.

Divide by -2n, and write \bar x = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i and \bar y = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i y_i for the two means:

\bar y - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 \bar x = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1 \bar x.

In words: the residuals sum to zero, and the fitted line passes through the centre of the cloud, the point (\bar x, \bar y).

The slope. The inside has derivative -x_i with respect to \beta_1:

\frac{\partial S}{\partial \beta_1} = -2\sum_{i} x_i\big(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\big) = 0.

Substitute \beta_0 = \bar y - \beta_1 \bar x. The bracket becomes (y_i - \bar y) - \beta_1 (x_i - \bar x), so

\sum_{i} x_i (y_i - \bar y) = \hat\beta_1 \sum_{i} x_i (x_i - \bar x).

One last step. Deviations from a mean always sum to zero, \sum_i (y_i - \bar y) = 0, so subtracting \bar x times that sum changes nothing: \sum_i x_i(y_i - \bar y) = \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y). The same trick works on the right-hand side. Therefore

\hat\beta_1 = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2} = \frac{\mathrm{cov}(x, y)}{\mathrm{var}(x)}.

The last form divides the top and the bottom by n, which turns the sums into the covariance of x and y and the variance of x. Read it as: the slope is how strongly x and y move together, measured in units of how much x varies on its own. If y tends to sit above its mean whenever x sits above its mean, the covariance is positive and the line rises.

Part 4

Several inputs: matrices, projection and R²

With p inputs (below, p = 2) the model is y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \dots + \beta_p x_{ip} + \varepsilon_i, where x_{ij} is input j of observation i. In matrix form:

  • y (n \times 1) holds the outputs, \varepsilon (n \times 1) the errors, \beta ((p+1) \times 1) the coefficients.
  • X (n \times (p+1)) is the design matrix. Row i is (1, x_{i1}, \dots, x_{ip}); the column of ones carries the intercept.
y = X\beta + \varepsilon

The sum of squared residuals is S(\beta) = (y - X\beta)^\top (y - X\beta). Setting its gradient -2X^\top(y - X\beta) to zero repeats Part 2 for all coefficients at once, and gives the normal equations:

X^\top X\,\hat\beta = X^\top y \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y.

The inverse exists as long as no input is an exact linear combination of the others. Row j of X^\top(y - X\hat\beta) = 0 reads \sum_i x_{ij} e_i = 0: the residuals are orthogonal to every column of X. With p = 1 these are exactly the two zeros of Part 3's second table.

Geometric reading. Think of y as one arrow in n-dimensional space, one coordinate per observation. As \beta varies, X\beta sweeps out a flat subspace: every prediction the model can produce. Least squares picks the point \hat y = X\hat\beta of that subspace closest to y: the foot of the perpendicular, where the residual e = y - \hat y meets the subspace at a right angle. So \hat y is the orthogonal projection of y onto the columns of X.

R^2. SS_{\mathrm{tot}} = \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2 is the total spread of y around its mean, the squared miss of the flat line \hat y = \bar y. SS_{\mathrm{res}} = \sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2 is the spread left after the fit.

R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{\mathrm{res}}}{SS_{\mathrm{tot}}}

Because the residual is perpendicular to the fitted part, Pythagoras gives SS_{\mathrm{tot}} = \sum_i (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2 + SS_{\mathrm{res}} (when the model has an intercept). So R^2 lies between 0 and 1, and is the fraction of the variance of y that the model explains. In Part 3's example, SS_{\mathrm{tot}} = 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 2.25 = 5 and SS_{\mathrm{res}} = 1.8, so R^2 = 1 - \frac{1.8}{5} = 0.64. With a single input, R^2 equals the squared correlation of x and y: here r = \frac{4}{\sqrt{5 \times 5}} = 0.8 and r^2 = 0.64.

Adding an input enlarges the subspace, so SS_{\mathrm{res}} can only shrink: R^2 never falls when an input is added, even a useless one. Try it on the cars: add inputs that are pure random numbers, unrelated to anything.

R^2 adjusted R^2

The spread of CO2, SS_{\mathrm{tot}}, split by Pythagoras

The fix is the adjusted R^2, which charges each input for the data it uses up: 1 - (1 - R^2)\frac{n-1}{n-p-1}. It does not climb with noise. In the demo it wanders up and down around its starting value, and on average useless inputs leave it where it was. It rises only when the new input's F statistic, the test of Part 7, is above 1. Part 7 uses both on the real second input.

Reading the coefficients. In the multiple model, \beta_j is the change in the prediction when x_j grows by one unit, with all the other inputs held fixed.

Assumptions. Least squares can always be computed. Reading its output as true effects with trustworthy error bars relies on:

  1. Linearity: the average of y is linear in the inputs.
  2. Independent errors across observations.
  3. Constant error variance at every input value.
  4. Zero-mean errors at every input value (no omitted cause correlated with the inputs).
  5. No perfect collinearity, so X^\top X is invertible.
  6. For exact small-sample intervals, normally distributed errors.

In the code below. X1 is the 36 \times 1 matrix of weights and y the CO2 vector. LinearRegression adds the column of ones itself and solves least squares: b0 is \hat\beta_0, b1 is \hat\beta_1, and simple.score(X1, y) returns R^2. X2 has two columns, Weight and Volume, so multi solves the normal equations with p = 2, giving b0_m, b_w and b_v.

Part 5

The data

Thirty-six cars, each with an engine displacement (Volume, cm³), a Weight (kg) and a CO2 emission rate (g/km). It is a small teaching dataset, the one used in the W3Schools Python tutorial [7]: two candidate causes, one outcome, and a table small enough to look at directly. Part 6

Simple linear regression

A simple linear regression models one predictor:

\widehat{\text{CO2}} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{Weight}

scikit-learn's LinearRegression fits \beta_0 and \beta_1 by ordinary least squares, with exactly the formulas derived in Part 2.

X1 = df[["Weight"]].values
y = df["CO2"].values

simple = LinearRegression().fit(X1, y)
b1 = simple.coef_[0]
b0 = simple.intercept_
r2_simple = simple.score(X1, y)
print(f"CO2 = {b0:.2f} + {b1:.4f} * Weight")
print(f"R2 = {r2_simple:.3f}")
CO2 = 80.06 + 0.0170 * Weight
R2 = 0.305

This is the line that the demo's least squares button draws. Read the slope as a rate: among these cars, each extra kilogram goes with about \hat\beta_1 \approx 0.017 g/km more CO2 on average, or 17 g/km per tonne. That is an association, not proof that weight itself causes the emissions: heavier cars also tend to have bigger engines, as Part 7 shows. The intercept, \hat\beta_0 \approx 80, is what the line predicts at zero weight: not physically meaningful here, just where the line crosses the axis. R^2 says how much of the variance in CO2 the line explains: here about 30%. The rest is unexplained noise or missing predictors.

Part 7

Multiple linear regression

Add Volume as a second predictor:

\widehat{\text{CO2}} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{Weight} + \beta_2 \cdot \text{Volume}

Geometrically this fits a plane through the 3D cloud of (Weight, Volume, CO2) points, instead of a line through a 2D cloud.

X2 = df[["Weight", "Volume"]].values
multi = LinearRegression().fit(X2, y)
b_w, b_v = multi.coef_
b0_m = multi.intercept_
r2_multi = multi.score(X2, y)
print(f"CO2 = {b0_m:.2f} + {b_w:.4f} * Weight + {b_v:.4f} * Volume")
print(f"R2 = {r2_multi:.3f}  (simple regression was {r2_simple:.3f})")
CO2 = 79.69 + 0.0076 * Weight + 0.0078 * Volume
R2 = 0.377  (simple regression was 0.305)

The weight coefficient has fallen from 0.0170 to 0.0076. A plane is hard to draw, but a slice of it is easy: fix the engine volume, and the plane becomes a line in weight, with slope 0.0076 whatever the volume. Below, the cars are coloured by engine size, and each colour gets the plane's slice at that size.

up to 1,300 cm³ 1,400 to 1,600 cm³ 2,000 cm³ and up simple regression the plane at your volume

R^2 rose from 0.305 to 0.377 once Volume was added. Part 4 showed that R^2 can never fall when an input is added, so a rise alone proves little. Two checks put it in proportion. The adjusted R^2 charges each input for the data it uses up; an F test asks how often a gain this large would appear if Volume carried no information at all.

from scipy import stats

n = len(y)
adjusted = lambda r2, p: 1 - (1 - r2) * (n - 1) / (n - p - 1)
print(f"adjusted R2: simple {adjusted(r2_simple, 1):.3f}, multiple {adjusted(r2_multi, 2):.3f}")
F = (r2_multi - r2_simple) / ((1 - r2_multi) / (n - 3))
print(f"F test for adding Volume: F = {F:.2f}, p = {stats.f.sf(F, 1, n - 3):.3f}")
print(f"correlation of Weight and Volume: {np.corrcoef(df['Weight'], df['Volume'])[0, 1]:.2f}")
g = np.polyfit(df["Weight"], df["Volume"], 1)[0]        # slope of Volume on Weight, cm3 per kg
print(f"b_w + b_v * {g:.3f} = {b_w + b_v * g:.4f}   (the simple slope was {b1:.4f})")
adjusted R2: simple 0.284, multiple 0.339
F test for adding Volume: F = 3.79, p = 0.060
correlation of Weight and Volume: 0.75
b_w + b_v * 1.211 = 0.0170   (the simple slope was 0.0170)

The adjusted R^2 still rises, from 0.284 to 0.339, but the F test gives p = 0.060: suggestive, not conclusive, with 36 cars. Engine size seems to carry some information about emissions that weight alone does not, but this table cannot pin down how much.

Each coefficient is now a partial effect: the change in predicted CO2 per unit of one predictor, holding the other fixed. The weight coefficient fell from 0.0170 to 0.0076 because weight and volume move together (correlation 0.75): heavier cars tend to have bigger engines, so the simple slope credited weight with part of what volume does. The last line makes this exact. Volume rises by 1.211 cm³ per kg of weight on average, and the simple slope splits into the partial effect of weight plus the effect of the volume that comes with it, the omitted-variable identity:

\underbrace{0.0170}_{\text{simple}} = \underbrace{0.0076}_{\text{weight, volume fixed}} + \underbrace{0.0078 \times 1.211}_{\text{the volume that comes with the weight}}.

Finally, a prediction for a car that is not in the table:

# A hypothetical mid-size car: 1300 kg, 1300 cm3 engine
example = pd.DataFrame({"Weight": [1300], "Volume": [1300]})
pred = multi.predict(example.to_numpy())
print(f"predicted CO2 for a 1300 kg / 1300 cm3 car: {pred[0]:.1f} g/km")
predicted CO2 for a 1300 kg / 1300 cm3 car: 99.7 g/km

The slice demo above makes the same prediction for any weight and volume you choose, and warns when the combination lies outside the range of the 36 cars, where a fitted plane is an extrapolation.

Part 8

The chapter on one page

  1. The model: a line plus noise; the residual is the vertical gap between a point and the line.
  2. Least squares: choose the line with the smallest sum of squared residuals, the bottom of a smooth bowl.
  3. The formulas: the slope is \frac{\mathrm{cov}(x, y)}{\mathrm{var}(x)}, the line passes through the means, and the residuals sum to zero and are orthogonal to the input.
  4. Several inputs: the normal equations; the fit is the projection of y, and R^2 never falls when an input is added.
  5. Partial effects: with correlated inputs, coefficients share the credit; weight's slope falls from 0.0170 to 0.0076 once volume is in.
  6. Check before believing: adjusted R^2, an F test and a residual plot say what a rise in R^2 is worth.
  7. Explained, not correct: R^2 measures explained variance; a low R^2 can still be an honest, useful model of a genuinely noisy process.

Part 9

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

  1. Standardised coefficients. Weight and Volume are on different scales, so their raw coefficients are not directly comparable as "which matters more". Standardise both predictors (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation) before fitting, and compare the resulting coefficients.
    Hint

    StandardScaler().fit_transform(df[["Weight", "Volume"]]) does both steps. A standardised coefficient is the raw one times its input's standard deviation.

    Solution
    from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
Xs = StandardScaler().fit_transform(df[["Weight", "Volume"]])
std_model = LinearRegression().fit(Xs, y)
print(std_model.coef_)  # now directly comparable: larger |coef| = stronger standardised effect

    The standardised coefficients are 1.80 for weight and 2.99 for volume: one standard deviation of volume (384 cm³) goes with 3.0 g/km more CO2, one standard deviation of weight (239 kg) with 1.8 g/km, the other held fixed. Volume has the larger standardised effect, but with the two inputs so correlated the split between them is poorly determined (the F test of Part 7 gave p = 0.060): treat the ranking as a hint, not a finding.

  2. Residuals. Plot the residuals (y - \hat y) of the multiple regression against the fitted values. Is there a pattern, or do they look like unstructured noise?
    Hint

    With 36 points the eye is easily fooled. Average the residuals over each quarter of the fitted values, and try adding a squared term in weight.

    Solution
    fitted = multi.predict(X2)
resid = y - fitted
plt.scatter(fitted, resid)
plt.axhline(0, color="k", lw=1)
plt.xlabel("fitted CO2"); plt.ylabel("residual")

order = np.argsort(fitted)                  # mean residual in each quarter of the fitted values
print(np.round([resid[q].mean() for q in np.array_split(order, 4)], 2))
X3 = np.column_stack([X2, df["Weight"]**2])  # does a squared term help?
r2_sq = LinearRegression().fit(X3, y).score(X3, y)
F = (r2_sq - r2_multi) / ((1 - r2_sq) / (n - 4))
print(f"with Weight^2: R2 = {r2_sq:.3f}, F = {F:.2f}, p = {stats.f.sf(F, 1, n - 4):.3f}")

    There is a pattern: a gentle U. Averaged over each quarter of the fitted values, the residuals are +1.28, −2.29, −0.94 and +1.94 g/km, so the cars at both ends of the fitted range sit above the plane and those in the middle below it. A squared term in weight confirms it: R^2 rises from 0.377 to 0.516, and the F test gives p = 0.005. The plane misses some curvature, a mild one. With 36 cars, a picture alone would be easy to dismiss, which is why a check like this one (in general form, Ramsey's RESET test [6]) is worth running.

  3. A line by hand. Fit the least-squares line through (1, 2), (2, 2), (3, 4) and (4, 4) with pencil and paper, following Part 3: the means, the two sums, \hat\beta_1, \hat\beta_0, the residuals, S and R^2. Then check with LinearRegression.
    Hint

    Part 3's demo checks you: drag its points to heights 2, 2, 4 and 4, and its tables show every intermediate number.

    Solution

    \bar x = 2.5 and \bar y = 3. The deviations are -1.5, -0.5, 0.5, 1.5 for x and -1, -1, 1, 1 for y, so \sum (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y) = 1.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 1.5 = 4 and \sum (x_i - \bar x)^2 = 5. Hence \hat\beta_1 = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8 and \hat\beta_0 = 3 - 0.8 \times 2.5 = 1: the line is \hat y = 1 + 0.8\,x. The predictions are 1.8, 2.6, 3.4 and 4.2, the residuals 0.2, -0.6, 0.6, -0.2 (they sum to zero), S = 0.04 + 0.36 + 0.36 + 0.04 = 0.8, SS_{\mathrm{tot}} = 4 and R^2 = 1 - \frac{0.8}{4} = 0.8.

    m = LinearRegression().fit([[1], [2], [3], [4]], [2, 2, 4, 4])
print(round(m.intercept_, 6), m.coef_)   # 1.0 [0.8]
  4. Which single input? Fit CO2 on Volume alone. Which input explains more on its own? And why is the R^2 of the two together (0.377) so much less than the sum of the two single-input values?
    Hint

    The demo of Part 4 has a volume button. For the second question, look at the third view of Part 5's scatter plot.

    Solution
    X_v = df[["Volume"]].values
vol = LinearRegression().fit(X_v, y)
print(vol.intercept_, vol.coef_[0], vol.score(X_v, y))

    Alone, Volume gives CO2 = 83.75 + 0.0113 × Volume, with R^2 = 0.351, more than weight's 0.305. Together they reach only 0.377, far below 0.305 + 0.351 = 0.656, because the two inputs carry largely the same information (correlation 0.75): the second adds only what the first did not already explain. Single-input R^2 values add up only when the inputs are uncorrelated.

K-means and k-means++

Two hundred mall customers, their incomes and their spending scores, and no labels at all. K-means looks for a few groups whose points lie close together, by alternating two steps: assign every point to its nearest centre, then move each centre to the mean of its points. It always converges, but only to a local optimum, and which one depends on where the centres started. Step through it below, and watch it get stuck.

Part 1

The goal: a few centres, small distances

Picture the 200 customers as dots on a plane, one axis for income and one for spending score. Clustering asks for a few groups whose dots lie close together. K-means turns "close together" into one number and makes it as small as it can.

Notation. The data are n points x_1, \dots, x_n. Each x_i is a vector of d features, x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d. In the code the points are the rows of X, with n = 200 and d = 2 (income in thousands of dollars, spending score from 1 to 100). We choose a number of clusters k. Each cluster C_j (j = 1, \dots, k) is a set of points, and every point belongs to exactly one cluster. Each cluster has a centre (or centroid) \mu_j \in \mathbb{R}^d, a point in the same space as the data. The squared Euclidean distance between a point x and a centre \mu is

\lVert x - \mu \rVert^2 = \sum_{f=1}^{d} (x_f - \mu_f)^2 ,

the sum over the d features of the squared coordinate differences.

The objective. K-means looks for the clusters and centres that make the total squared distance from each point to its own centre as small as possible:

J = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \; \sum_{x \in C_j} \lVert x - \mu_j \rVert^2 .

Read it as: for each cluster, add up how far its points sit from its centre (squared), then add over the clusters. J is measured in squared feature units. A small J means tight, compact groups. J is called the inertia or within-cluster sum of squares; after fitting, scikit-learn stores it in inertia_.

Minimising J exactly is hard: the number of ways to split n points into k groups grows roughly like k^n. So k-means improves one split step by step instead.

What is k-means?

In plain words: a way to sort points into k groups with no labels to learn from, by finding k centres and sending each point to its nearest one, so that the groups are as tight as possible.

More precisely: the search for centres \mu_1, \dots, \mu_k and an assignment that minimise the inertia J, carried out by Lloyd's alternating steps from a chosen start [1], [2]. The problem is NP-hard in general; Lloyd's algorithm reaches a local minimum, and k-means++ seeding bounds how bad the start can be [3].

Part 2

Lloyd's algorithm, and why it only goes downhill

J depends on two things: the assignment (which cluster each point is in) and the centres. Fixing either one makes the other easy to choose. Lloyd's algorithm [1] alternates the two choices:

  1. Assignment step. Keep the centres fixed. Put each point in the cluster of its nearest centre.
  2. Update step. Keep the assignment fixed. Move each centre to the mean of its points, \mu_j = \frac{1}{\lvert C_j\rvert}\sum_{x\in C_j} x, where \lvert C_j\rvert is the number of points in C_j.

Repeat until no point changes cluster.

The assignment step cannot increase J. J has one term per point, its squared distance to its centre. Choosing the nearest centre makes each term as small as the current centres allow, so no term grows and neither does the sum.

The update step cannot increase J. With the assignment fixed, the clusters no longer interact, so look at one cluster C with m points and ask which centre \mu minimises

g(\mu) = \sum_{x\in C} \lVert x - \mu\rVert^2 = \sum_{x \in C}\sum_{f=1}^d (x_f - \mu_f)^2 .

Differentiate with respect to one coordinate \mu_f. Only the terms containing \mu_f matter, and the derivative of (x_f-\mu_f)^2 is -2(x_f-\mu_f):

\frac{\partial g}{\partial \mu_f} = -2\sum_{x\in C}(x_f - \mu_f) = -2\Big(\sum_{x\in C} x_f \;-\; m\,\mu_f\Big).

Setting this to zero gives \mu_f = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{x\in C} x_f: each coordinate of the best centre is the average of that coordinate over the cluster. So the best centre is the cluster mean, written \bar x. The second derivative is 2m > 0, so this is a minimum.

A second derivation shows how much a misplaced centre costs. Write x - \mu = (x - \bar x) + (\bar x - \mu) and expand the square. The cross terms add up to zero because \sum_{x\in C}(x-\bar x) = 0, which leaves

\sum_{x\in C}\lVert x-\mu\rVert^2 = \sum_{x\in C}\lVert x-\bar x\rVert^2 + m\,\lVert \bar x - \mu\rVert^2 .

The first term does not depend on \mu. The second is a penalty: placing the centre a distance \delta away from the mean adds exactly m\,\delta^2. The mean is the only choice with zero penalty.

Convergence. Each step lowers J or leaves it unchanged, J \ge 0, and there are only finitely many ways to assign n points to k clusters. So the algorithm stops after a finite number of steps. It stops at a local minimum: no single step can improve J, but a completely different split may have a lower J. Which local minimum it reaches depends on the starting centres.

Worked example. Six points on a line, x = 1, 2, 3, 8, 9, 12, with k = 2 and a poor start: \mu_1 = 1, \mu_2 = 4. The assignment step, with each point's distance to the two centres and its squared distance d^2 to the nearer one:

The next update gives \mu_1 = 2, \mu_2 = \frac{29}{3} \approx 9.67 and J \approx 10.67. After that no point changes cluster and the algorithm stops. Each step can only lower J; that is why k-means always stops, and why it stops at the first valley it finds.

Part 3

Better starting centres (k-means++), and choosing k

Random initialisation picks k data points uniformly at random as the starting centres. Two of them can fall inside the same natural group, and Lloyd's local moves often cannot pull one of them out to a group that has no centre.

k-means++ [3] chooses the starting centres one at a time and favours points far from the centres already chosen. Let D(x) be the distance from a point x to the nearest centre chosen so far.

  1. Pick the first centre uniformly at random from the data.
  2. Pick the next centre at random, choosing point x with probability
    P(x) = \frac{D(x)^2}{\sum_{x'} D(x')^2}.
  3. Repeat step 2 until there are k centres, then run Lloyd's algorithm from them.

A point next to a chosen centre has D(x) \approx 0 and is almost never picked; a point in a region with no centre is likely to be picked.

With the six points above, suppose the first centre is 1. Then D(x)^2 = 0, 1, 4, 49, 64, 121, which add to 239. The probability that the second centre lands in the far group \{8, 9, 12\} is \frac{49+64+121}{239} \approx 0.98. Uniform random choice among the five other points would give \frac{3}{5} = 0.6. On the customers:

The guarantee, proved by Arthur and Vassilvitskii (2007) [3], concerns the seeding alone, before any Lloyd step:

\mathbb{E}\big[J_{\text{k-means++}}\big] \le 8(\ln k + 2)\, J^{\ast},

where J^\ast is the smallest inertia any k centres can achieve and \mathbb{E} is the average over the random choices. The expected cost is within a factor O(\log k) of the best possible, and Lloyd's steps can only lower it further. Random seeding has no bound of this kind: its J can be arbitrarily worse than J^\ast. scikit-learn's seeding is the greedy k-means++ of the same paper: at each step it draws 2 + \lfloor \ln k \rfloor candidates from P(x) and keeps the one that lowers the current J most (the widget's third button).

Choosing k: the elbow [6]. Let J^\ast(k) be the best inertia with k clusters. It never increases with k: given the best k-cluster solution, put one extra centre exactly on a data point that is not yet a centre; that point's term drops to zero and no other term grows. At k = n every point is its own centre and J = 0. So "minimise J over k" always answers "as many clusters as points". Instead, plot J against k and look for the elbow. Before it, an extra centre separates groups that were forced together, and J drops a lot. After it, an extra centre only splits a genuine group in two, and J drops a little.

Part 4

Feature scale, and a 2-D view with PCA

Scale. The distance \lVert x-\mu\rVert^2 = \sum_f (x_f-\mu_f)^2 adds squared differences from every feature, in whatever units each feature has. A feature with a large numerical spread dominates the sum. Suppose income were recorded in dollars instead of thousands of dollars: a gap of 10 thousand dollars would contribute 10000^2 = 10^8, while the largest possible gap in spending score, from 1 to 99, contributes only 98^2 = 9604. K-means would then cluster on income alone. In this data income and spending score have similar spreads (standard deviations of about 26 each), so the chapter clusters them as they are. When age is added (standard deviation about 14 years), the three features are standardised first: each feature f is replaced by

z_f = \frac{x_f - m_f}{s_f},

where m_f and s_f are that feature's mean and standard deviation over the data. Every standardised feature has mean 0 and standard deviation 1, so each gets a comparable say in the distance. This is what StandardScaler does.

PCA. With three features the clusters can no longer be drawn directly. Principal component analysis (PCA) finds the directions along which the data vary most [5]. Take the standardised points z_i \in \mathbb{R}^d (mean zero) and a unit vector v (\lVert v\rVert = 1). Projecting each point onto v gives the number v^\top z_i, and the variance of these numbers is v^\top \Sigma v, where \Sigma = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i z_i z_i^\top is the d\times d covariance matrix. To find the direction of largest variance, maximise v^\top\Sigma v subject to v^\top v = 1. With a Lagrange multiplier \lambda, set the gradient of v^\top\Sigma v - \lambda(v^\top v - 1) to zero:

2\Sigma v - 2\lambda v = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Sigma v = \lambda v .

So the best direction is an eigenvector of \Sigma, and the variance along it is v^\top\Sigma v = \lambda v^\top v = \lambda, its eigenvalue. The first principal component is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue \lambda_1; the second is the eigenvector with the next largest, \lambda_2, at right angles to the first. A 2-D plot on these two directions keeps the share \frac{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3} of the total variance, which the code prints from explained_variance_ratio_. PCA is only used for viewing here: the clustering itself runs in the full 3-D space.

In the code. KMeans(n_clusters=k, init="k-means++", n_init=10) runs k-means++ seeding followed by Lloyd's algorithm 10 times and keeps the run with the lowest J. Left to its default, n_init="auto", scikit-learn runs k-means++ once and random initialisation 10 times. After fitting, km.cluster_centers_ holds the centres \mu_j, km.labels_ each point's cluster, and km.inertia_ is J. Part 6 plots J against k; Part 7 compares init="random" with init="k-means++" using n_init=1 (one Lloyd run each); Part 8 standardises three features and views the clusters through PCA.

Part 6

Choosing k: the elbow method

K-means needs k as an input. There is no single correct answer, but inertia, the objective J of Part 1, always decreases as k grows (Part 3), so we look for where it stops decreasing quickly: the "elbow".

ks = range(1, 11)
inertias = [KMeans(n_clusters=k, init="k-means++", n_init=10, random_state=42).fit(X).inertia_ for k in ks]

The drop from k = 1 to k = 5 is steep; after that it flattens. k = 5 is the classic answer for this dataset, and it lines up with the visual groups.

k = 5
km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, init="k-means++", n_init=10, random_state=42).fit(X)
mall["cluster"] = km.labels_
print(mall["cluster"].value_counts().sort_index().rename("customers"))

Five clean, interpretable segments fall out, with J = 44{,}448: a large middle group (81 customers around 55 k$ and a score of 50), high earners who spend (39), high earners who do not (35), and two small low-income groups, one spending freely (22) and one carefully (23). These are exactly the kind of customer segments a marketing team would want to target differently. Press run to convergence in the demo at the top from a k-means++ start, and you will usually land on them.

Part 7

Random against k-means++

init="random" just picks k data points at random as starting centroids: two starting points can easily land close together, wasting a cluster and biasing where k-means gets stuck. init="k-means++" instead picks starting centroids one at a time, favouring points far from centroids already chosen, spreading the starting guesses out before the iterating even begins.

We compare them fairly: one single run each (n_init=1, so neither method gets to retry and keep its best attempt), repeated over 30 random seeds.

random_inertias, pp_inertias = [], []
for seed in range(30):
    random_inertias.append(
        KMeans(n_clusters=5, init="random", n_init=1, random_state=seed).fit(X).inertia_)
    pp_inertias.append(
        KMeans(n_clusters=5, init="k-means++", n_init=1, random_state=seed).fit(X).inertia_)

random_inertias, pp_inertias = np.array(random_inertias), np.array(pp_inertias)
print(f"random init:   mean inertia {random_inertias.mean():,.0f}  "
      f"(std {random_inertias.std():,.0f}, worst {random_inertias.max():,.0f})")
print(f"k-means++ init: mean inertia {pp_inertias.mean():,.0f}  "
      f"(std {pp_inertias.std():,.0f}, worst {pp_inertias.max():,.0f})")
best = min(random_inertias.min(), pp_inertias.min())
print(f"runs ending at the best inertia found, {best:,.0f}: "
      f"random {np.sum(random_inertias < best + 1)} of 30, k-means++ {np.sum(pp_inertias < best + 1)} of 30")
random init:   mean inertia 55,718  (std 14,855, worst 91,762)
k-means++ init: mean inertia 48,140  (std 8,250, worst 66,733)
runs ending at the best inertia found, 44,448: random 10 of 30, k-means++ 18 of 30

k-means++ is better on average and more consistent (smaller spread) than random initialisation. The averages hide the shape of the thing: a single run ends either at the optimum, J = 44{,}448 (or a near-twin at 44{,}454), or at a bad local minimum between 66{,}000 and 92{,}000, where two centres share one natural group and another group has none. Random initialisation reached the optimum in 10 of the 30 runs, k-means++ in 18 (over 1,000 seeds: 39% against 52%). Neither is safe on its own, which is why restarts exist (exercise 1); k-means++ makes each restart more likely to count, and that is why scikit-learn defaults to it. Part 8

A 2-D view via PCA

The plots above used income and spending directly because there were only two features. With more features (say, income, spending and age), we need to project down to two dimensions to plot anything; principal component analysis does that by finding the directions of largest variance (Part 4).

X3 = StandardScaler().fit_transform(mall[["Annual Income (k$)", "Spending Score (1-100)", "Age"]])
pca = PCA(n_components=2).fit(X3)
X3_2d = pca.transform(X3)
print(f"variance explained by the first 2 components: {pca.explained_variance_ratio_.sum():.1%}")

km3 = KMeans(n_clusters=5, init="k-means++", n_init=10, random_state=42).fit(X3)
variance explained by the first 2 components: 77.6%

Adding Age changes which customers land together: the five 3-feature clusters are not the five 2-feature clusters above (an adjusted Rand index of 0.58 between the two labellings; the two high-income groups survive, the large middle group is cut by age). The plot lets us see the result of clustering in three dimensions at all; that is PCA's job here, not k-means's. The first two components keep 77.6% of the variance, so the picture is faithful but not complete.

Part 9

The chapter on one page

  1. The objective: the inertia J, the total squared distance from each point to its own centre.
  2. Lloyd's algorithm: assign to the nearest centre, move each centre to its mean; each half-step lowers J, so it stops, at a local minimum.
  3. The mean is the best centre: any other place costs exactly m\,\delta^2 more.
  4. k-means++: pick starts far from each other, with probability \propto D(x)^2; the expected cost is within 8(\ln k + 2) of the best.
  5. Choosing k: the elbow of J, or the peak of the silhouette; here both say 5.
  6. Starts matter: single runs end at the optimum or in a bad valley; k-means++ reaches the optimum more often, restarts cure most of the rest.

Part 10

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

  1. n_init in practice. Refit with init="random", n_init=10 (retry 10 times, keep the best). How does its inertia compare with a single k-means++ run? With 10 retries of k-means++?
    Hint

    Print inertia_ and n_iter_ for each. The race of Part 7 says how often a single random run misses; ten tries miss only if all ten do.

    Solution
    for init, n_init in (("random", 10), ("k-means++", 1), ("k-means++", 10)):
    km_ = KMeans(n_clusters=5, init=init, n_init=n_init, random_state=0).fit(X)
    print(init, n_init, round(km_.inertia_, 1), km_.n_iter_)

    All three reach the same inertia, 44,448.5, the optimum of Part 7. The whole risk of random initialisation is a single unlucky draw, and ten retries almost always cure it: with n_init=10, random initialisation reached the optimum for 29 of 30 seeds. What k-means++ still buys is speed: a single run from k-means++ seeds averaged 4.9 Lloyd iterations against 6.9 from random seeds over the 30 seeds of Part 7, and with n_init="auto" scikit-learn runs it once where it would run random initialisation ten times.

  2. Silhouette score. Compute sklearn.metrics.silhouette_score for k = 2, \dots, 8 instead of inertia. Does it pick the same k as the elbow method?
    Hint

    The widget of Part 6 has a silhouette button. Unlike inertia, the silhouette can go down when k goes up.

    Solution
    from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score
for k in range(2, 9):
    labels = KMeans(n_clusters=k, init="k-means++", n_init=10, random_state=42).fit_predict(X)
    print(k, silhouette_score(X, labels))

    The silhouette score is 0.297, 0.468, 0.493, 0.554, 0.540, 0.529, 0.455 for k = 2, \dots, 8: it peaks at k = 5, the elbow's choice. Unlike inertia it does not automatically favour larger k (it falls again after 5), so it is a useful second opinion [4]; it is also slower, since it compares every pair of points.

  3. A bad valley. Among the 30 single random-start runs of Part 7, take one that ended above 60,000 and cross-tabulate its labels against the five segments of Part 6. Which natural group has two centres, which has none, and why can no single assignment or update step repair it?
    Hint

    The demo at the top shows the same thing: choose a random start and press new start until a run converges above 60,000, then look at the crosses. A centre that owns half of a group is the mean of its half; moving it towards the empty group would first make J worse.

    Solution
    for seed in range(30):
    km_ = KMeans(n_clusters=5, init="random", n_init=1, random_state=seed).fit(X)
    if km_.inertia_ > 60000:
        print(seed, round(km_.inertia_)); print(pd.crosstab(km.labels_, km_.labels_)); break

    Twelve of the 30 runs end above 60,000. The first, seed 4, is the worst of them, at 89,897: the large middle group has swallowed the careful low-income group, both high-income groups are split between two centres, and the free-spending low-income group is scattered over two clusters. The commoner valleys near 66,500 are simpler: one high-income group split in two, and the middle group swallowing one small low-income group (in 400 random single runs, that was the shape of about nine bad valleys in ten). Every point is already with its nearest centre, and every centre is already the mean of its points, so both half-steps leave everything unchanged: that is the definition of a local minimum. Getting out would need a joint move, taking one of the two sharing centres across empty space to the orphaned group, which makes J larger before it makes it smaller. Lloyd's algorithm never takes such a move; k-means++ seeding and restarts exist to avoid starting in that valley at all.

  4. Scale matters. Cluster the same two features with income in dollars (multiply the column by 1,000) and k = 5. What happens to the clusters, and why does standardising fix it?
    Hint

    Part 4 did the arithmetic: a gap of 10 thousand dollars contributes 10^8 to the squared distance, the whole range of the spending score less than 10^4.

    Solution
    Xd = X * np.array([1000, 1])
labels_d = KMeans(n_clusters=5, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit_predict(Xd)
print(pd.crosstab(labels_d, km.labels_))

    With income in dollars the five clusters are five income bands, straight vertical stripes: the spending score, whose whole range is worth less than one thousandth of a 10 k$ income gap, no longer influences any assignment. Standardising both features (or, as here, leaving both in units with similar spreads of about 26) gives each a comparable say. K-means has no notion of what a feature means; it only sees squared differences, in whatever units it is given.

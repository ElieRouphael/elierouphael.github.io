Support vector machines

A support vector machine looks for the boundary between classes that leaves the widest possible margin to the nearest points of each class, the support vectors. With a kernel it can bend that boundary into a curve. Below, it learns to tell three species of iris apart from the size of their petals; it is retrained in your browser every time you move a slider.

Part 1

A straight boundary and the distance to it

Start with two classes. Training example i has a feature vector x_i of p numbers (below, p = 2: petal length and width, in cm) and a label y_i equal to +1 or -1. A linear classifier has a weight vector w (also p numbers) and a number b, the bias. It predicts

\hat y = \mathrm{sign}\big(w^\top x + b\big),

where w^\top x = w_1 x_1 + \dots + w_p x_p is the dot product. The decision function f(x) = w^\top x + b is positive on one side of the boundary and negative on the other. The boundary itself, where f(x) = 0, is a hyperplane: a line when p = 2, a plane when p = 3.

w is perpendicular to the boundary. Two points x_a and x_b on the boundary satisfy w^\top x_a + b = 0 and w^\top x_b + b = 0. Subtracting gives w^\top (x_a - x_b) = 0: w is at a right angle to every direction that lies along the boundary.

Distance to the boundary. Take any point x, and let x_0 be the closest point on the boundary. The shortest path from the boundary to x runs along the perpendicular, the unit vector \frac{w}{\lVert w \rVert}, where \lVert w \rVert = \sqrt{w^\top w} is the length of w. So x = x_0 + t\, \frac{w}{\lVert w \rVert} for some number t, the signed distance. Apply f to both sides:

f(x) = \underbrace{w^\top x_0 + b}_{=\,0} + \; t\,\frac{w^\top w}{\lVert w \rVert} = t\,\lVert w \rVert.

Solving for t and dropping the sign:

\text{distance}(x) = \frac{\lvert w^\top x + b \rvert}{\lVert w \rVert}.

In words: the decision function, divided by the length of w, measures how far a point is from the boundary; its sign says on which side.

Part 2

The widest margin

If a straight line can separate the two classes, many lines can. The SVM picks the one farthest from the closest points of both classes [1]. The margin is the distance from the boundary to the nearest training point.

Fixing the scale. Multiplying w and b by the same positive number leaves the boundary f = 0 where it is. So we may scale them until the closest points satisfy \lvert w^\top x_i + b \rvert = 1. By Part 1 these points sit at distance \frac{1}{\lVert w \rVert}, and the empty band between the lines w^\top x + b = \pm 1 is \frac{2}{\lVert w \rVert} wide. "Every point is on its correct side and at least that far away" becomes one inequality per point (multiplying by the label makes both classes read "positive"):

y_i\big(w^\top x_i + b\big) \ge 1 \quad \text{for all } i.

Making the margin \frac{1}{\lVert w \rVert} as large as possible is the same as making \lVert w \rVert as small as possible. This gives the hard-margin SVM:

\min_{w,\,b}\ \tfrac{1}{2} \lVert w \rVert^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i\big(w^\top x_i + b\big) \ge 1 \ \text{ for all } i.

Squaring and the \tfrac{1}{2} do not move the minimiser; they make the objective a smooth convex bowl with a single global minimum (chapter 02).

A hand example. Take the line x_1 + x_2 = 4, that is w = (1, 1) and b = -4, so \lVert w \rVert = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414.

Here f(x) = x_1 + x_2 - 4, and the distance is \frac{\lvert f(x) \rvert}{\sqrt{2}}. Every point satisfies y\,f(x) \ge 1. B and C have y\,f(x) = 1 exactly: they sit on the margin, and the band between the classes is \frac{2}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 1.414 wide. No line can do better, since B and C are only \sqrt{2} apart, so this is the SVM solution. Moving A or D a little changes nothing; B and C pin the boundary in place. They are the support vectors. Look for a wider street yourself:

Soft margin. Real classes overlap, so some points must be allowed inside the margin or on the wrong side. Each point gets a slack \xi_i \ge 0 measuring its violation, charged at a price C > 0 per unit [1]:

\min_{w,\,b,\,\xi}\ \tfrac{1}{2} \lVert w \rVert^2 + C \sum_i \xi_i \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i\big(w^\top x_i + b\big) \ge 1 - \xi_i, \ \ \xi_i \ge 0.

A point with \xi_i = 0 respects the margin; 0 < \xi_i \le 1 means inside the margin but on the correct side; \xi_i > 1 means misclassified. At the optimum each slack is as small as allowed, \xi_i = \max\big(0,\ 1 - y_i f(x_i)\big), which gives an equivalent problem without constraints:

\min_{w,\,b}\ \tfrac{1}{2} \lVert w \rVert^2 + C \sum_i \max\big(0,\ 1 - y_i(w^\top x_i + b)\big).

The second term is the hinge loss: zero for points beyond the margin on the correct side, growing linearly as a point enters the margin or crosses the boundary. (A point F = (2.5, 2) with label -1 would have y\,f = -0.5 above, so a hinge loss of 1.5.) The first term asks for a wide margin, the second for few violations, and C sets the exchange rate: a large C gives a narrow margin that follows the training points closely, a small C a wide margin that tolerates more violations.

What is a support vector machine?

In plain words: a classifier that draws the boundary between two classes as far as it can from the nearest examples of each, so that small changes in the data do not push points across it. Those nearest examples, the support vectors, are the only ones that matter.

More precisely: the classifier \mathrm{sign}\big(f(x)\big) with f(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i\, k(x_i, x) + b, whose weights solve the soft-margin problem: a wide margin, traded against the hinge loss at the price C. Only the support vectors have \alpha_i > 0 [1], [2].

Margin
The distance from the boundary to the nearest training point; with slack allowed, \frac{1}{\lVert w \rVert} by definition.
Support vector
A training point on the margin, inside it, or on the wrong side: the points that fix the boundary.
Hinge loss
Zero beyond the margin, growing linearly inside it and beyond the boundary.
Kernel
A similarity between two points that stands in for a dot product in a larger space.
Part 3

Kernels: curved boundaries from dot products

Two facts about the solution make curved boundaries possible.

w is built from the training points. Split w into a combination of the training points plus a part w_\perp perpendicular to all of them. w_\perp changes no value w^\top x_i, so it helps no constraint, but it adds \lVert w_\perp \rVert^2 to the objective; at the optimum it is zero. Writing the weights of the combination as \alpha_i y_i:

w = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i\, x_i, \qquad f(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i\, x_i^\top x + b.

The optimality conditions (Lagrange multipliers) add that 0 \le \alpha_i \le C, and that \alpha_i = 0 for every point strictly beyond the margin, y_i f(x_i) > 1: such a point pays no hinge loss, even if it moves a little, so it has no say. The points with \alpha_i > 0 (on the margin, inside it, or misclassified) are the support vectors, and f is a sum over them only. In the example, \alpha_{\text{B}} = \alpha_{\text{C}} = 1 and \alpha_{\text{A}} = \alpha_{\text{D}} = 0 give w = -\text{B} + \text{C} = (1, 1), as found.

Only dot products appear. Substituting w back turns training into a problem over the \alpha_i alone (the dual problem, which is what solvers work with [3], [4]):

\max_{\alpha}\ \sum_i \alpha_i - \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \alpha_j\, y_i y_j\, x_i^\top x_j \quad \text{subject to} \quad 0 \le \alpha_i \le C, \ \ \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0.

The data enter only through dot products x_i^\top x_j, both here and in f(x).

The kernel trick. Replace every dot product by a kernel k(x, z) equal to \varphi(x)^\top \varphi(z) for some map \varphi into a larger feature space [2], [6]. The SVM then draws a straight boundary in that space, curved in the original one, without ever computing \varphi. For example, with p = 2,

k(x, z) = (x^\top z)^2 = x_1^2 z_1^2 + 2 x_1 x_2 z_1 z_2 + x_2^2 z_2^2

equals \varphi(x)^\top \varphi(z) with \varphi(x) = (x_1^2,\ \sqrt{2}\, x_1 x_2,\ x_2^2); a flat boundary in those three coordinates is an ellipse or a hyperbola in the plane. The decision function, a sum over the support vectors only, becomes

f(x) = \sum_{i \,\in\, \text{support vectors}} \alpha_i y_i\, k(x_i, x) + b.

The RBF kernel (radial basis function, or Gaussian) is

k(x, z) = \exp\!\big(-\gamma\, \lVert x - z \rVert^2\big).

It equals 1 when x = z, and decays towards 0 as the points move apart. Each support vector adds a bump centred on itself, and the boundary is where the sum of bumps crosses zero. \gamma > 0 (in units of \frac{1}{\text{cm}^2} here) sets how far each bump reaches: at distance \frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}} the similarity has fallen to e^{-1} \approx 0.37. With \gamma = 1, points at distance 0.5, 1 and 2 get similarities e^{-0.25} \approx 0.78, e^{-1} \approx 0.37 and e^{-4} \approx 0.02. A large \gamma gives narrow bumps and a boundary that can wrap around single points (overfitting); a small \gamma gives an almost straight boundary. See the bumps add up, on a line of points where no single threshold separates the classes:

class +1 class −1 one bump per support vector their sum minus a constant, f(x)

More than two classes. scikit-learn's SVC trains one SVM per pair of classes (one-vs-one: 3 SVMs for three species) and predicts the class winning the most pairwise votes [3].

In the code below. X holds petal length and width (cm) for each flower, y the species. SVC(kernel="rbf", C=1.0, gamma="scale") solves the soft-margin problem with the RBF kernel and C = 1; gamma="scale" sets \gamma = \frac{1}{p \cdot \mathrm{Var}}, with p = 2 and \mathrm{Var} the variance of all entries of X_train. svm.n_support_ counts the support vectors of each class (the points with \alpha_i > 0), and svm.support_vectors_ holds their coordinates, circled in the demo at the top.

Part 4

The data

Fisher's iris dataset [5]: 150 flowers, 3 species, 4 measurements each. To draw a 2D decision boundary we use just petal length and petal width, the two measurements that separate the species best.

In the demo at the top, setosa is linearly separable from the other two by petal size alone; versicolor and virginica overlap more. That is the interesting part for a classifier to resolve. The petals are measured to the nearest 0.1 cm, so many flowers share a point: the 150 flowers make only 102 distinct points in this plane, and at (4.8, 1.8) cm there are flowers of both versicolor and virginica, which no boundary drawn on petal size alone can tell apart.

Part 5

Fitting the SVM

We hold out 30% of the flowers as a test set, fit an RBF-kernel SVM on the rest, and score it on flowers it never saw during training.

X = iris[["petal_length", "petal_width"]].values
y = iris["species"].values

X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=42, stratify=y)

svm = SVC(kernel="rbf", C=1.0, gamma="scale").fit(X_train, y_train)
y_pred = svm.predict(X_test)
acc = accuracy_score(y_test, y_pred)
print(f"test accuracy: {acc:.3f}  ({len(X_test)} held-out flowers)")
print(f"support vectors used: {svm.n_support_.sum()} of {len(X_train)} training points")
print(f"gamma = 1 / (2 * Var) = {1 / (2 * X_train.var()):.3f} per cm^2")
test accuracy: 0.933  (45 held-out flowers)
support vectors used: 34 of 105 training points
gamma = 1 / (2 * Var) = 0.141 per cm^2

With gamma="scale", \gamma = 0.141 per cm², so each kernel bump reaches about \frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}} \approx 2.7 cm: further than the overlap between versicolor and virginica, so the boundary is smooth, close to straight.

Part 6

The decision boundary

Because we only used two features, the boundary the SVM learned can be drawn directly: colour every point of the plane by what the model would predict there. That is the demo at the top, whose defaults are this model. Only the support vectors (circled) determine where the boundary sits: every other training point could move around freely without changing the fit at all, as long as it does not cross into the margin. Here 34 of the 105 training points are support vectors: prediction needs only those, not the whole training set. How many there are depends on C (exercise 2).

One of them is a virginica at (4.8, 1.8) cm, the point shared by both species: no boundary can get every flower there right. The test accuracy is 42 of 45, 0.933.

The boundary rests on a few points near it; the flowers far from it have no say.

Part 7

The chapter on one page

  1. A linear classifier: \mathrm{sign}(w^\top x + b); a point's distance to the boundary is \frac{\lvert f(x) \rvert}{\lVert w \rVert}.
  2. The widest margin: minimise \lVert w \rVert subject to y_i f(x_i) \ge 1; the points on the margin are the support vectors.
  3. Soft margin: slack for overlapping classes, priced by C; it is the hinge loss plus a penalty on \lVert w \rVert.
  4. Kernels: only dot products appear, so a kernel can stand in for them and curve the boundary; the RBF kernel adds one bump per support vector.
  5. The knobs: C trades margin width against violations, \gamma sets how far each support vector reaches.
  6. On the irises: 42 of 45 test flowers right; the three errors sit where versicolor and virginica overlap.

Part 8

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

  1. Linear against RBF kernel. Refit with kernel="linear". Does the accuracy change much? Plot its decision boundary next to the RBF one: where does a straight boundary struggle that a curved one does not?
    Hint

    The demo at the top has a linear kernel button. Compare the two boundaries in the overlap region.

    Solution
    svm_linear = SVC(kernel="linear", C=1.0).fit(X_train, y_train)
print(accuracy_score(y_test, svm_linear.predict(X_test)))

    The linear kernel scores exactly the same, 42 of 45 (0.933), with 20 support vectors instead of 34, although not on the same flowers: the two models disagree on 2 of the 45. With gamma="scale" the RBF bumps reach about 2.7 cm, so its boundary is nearly straight anyway; the curvature only matters in the small overlap region between versicolor and virginica, and there both kernels must guess.

  2. The cost of a small margin. Try C=1000 (very little tolerance for margin violations) and C=0.01 (very tolerant). Compare the number of support vectors and the test accuracy in each case.
    Hint

    Slide C in the demo at the top and watch the count of support vectors. Which way does it move, and why?

    Solution
    for C in (0.01, 1000):
    m = SVC(kernel="rbf", C=C, gamma="scale").fit(X_train, y_train)
    print(C, m.n_support_.sum(), accuracy_score(y_test, m.predict(X_test)))

    With C=1000 only 12 training points are support vectors, and the test accuracy is 0.933; with C=0.01 all 105 are, and the test accuracy is 0.911. A small C makes margin violations cheap, so the margin widens until almost every point sits inside it, and every point inside the margin is a support vector; a large C narrows the margin until only the few points near the boundary, or across it, remain. The test accuracy barely moves over this 100,000-fold range of C: 0.911 and 0.933 differ by one flower out of 45.

    The demo at the top counts 11 support vectors at C = 1000, not 12, with the same boundary. Two training flowers are identical, two versicolor at (4.9, 1.5): scikit-learn's solver happens to spread their weight over both, so both count, while the demo's puts it all on one. When points repeat, the number of support vectors is not unique; the boundary is.

  3. The kernel width. Refit the RBF model with gamma=100 and with gamma=0.1, keeping C=1. Compare the training accuracy, the test accuracy and the number of support vectors.
    Hint

    A bump reaches about \frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}. Slide \gamma to the right in the demo and watch islands appear.

    Solution
    for g in (100, 0.1):
    m = SVC(kernel="rbf", C=1.0, gamma=g).fit(X_train, y_train)
    print(g, m.n_support_.sum(), accuracy_score(y_train, m.predict(X_train)),
          accuracy_score(y_test, m.predict(X_test)))

    With gamma=100 each bump reaches only about 0.1 cm: the boundary wraps small islands around single flowers, 82 of the 105 training points become support vectors, the training accuracy rises to 0.990 and the test accuracy falls to 0.844, overfitting as in chapter 00. With gamma=0.1 the test accuracy is 0.956 with 37 support vectors, the best of the settings tried so far; but choosing it because of its test score would be the leakage of chapter 00. Exercise 4 chooses honestly.

  4. Choosing C and gamma honestly. Choose C from 0.1, 1, 10, 100 and gamma from 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10 by 5-fold cross-validation on the training set alone, then report the test accuracy of the chosen model.
    Hint

    GridSearchCV does the folds and the refit; look at best_params_, best_score_, and at how many settings share the best score.

    Solution
    from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV
grid = GridSearchCV(SVC(kernel="rbf"), {"C": [0.1, 1, 10, 100], "gamma": [0.01, 0.1, 1, 10]}, cv=5)
grid.fit(X_train, y_train)
print(grid.best_params_, grid.best_score_, grid.score(X_test, y_test))

    Seven of the 16 settings tie at a cross-validation accuracy of 0.971; GridSearchCV takes the first in its list, C=0.1 and gamma=0.01, which scores 0.933 on the test set. With 105 training flowers, one flower moves the cross-validation accuracy by about one point, so cross-validation cannot separate these settings: many are equally good. The remaining errors are in the overlap region, one of them at a point where both species sit, and no choice of C or gamma removes those.

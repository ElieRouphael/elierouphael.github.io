Part 1

From one neuron to a network

A single artificial neuron takes a list of numbers, weighs each one, adds a constant, and passes the result through a fixed function [1]. With an input x \in \mathbb{R}^d (here d = 2: the two coordinates of a point), a weight vector w \in \mathbb{R}^d and a bias b (a single number):

z = w^\top x + b = w_1 x_1 + \dots + w_d x_d + b, \qquad a = g(z).

z is the pre-activation, a weighted vote of the inputs. g is the activation function, and a is the neuron's output. The points where z = 0 form a straight line in the plane (a hyperplane in higher dimensions), so one neuron on its own can only split the plane with a straight cut.

Two activations appear in this chapter:

  • ReLU, \mathrm{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z): it passes positive values and replaces negative ones by 0. Its derivative is 1 for z > 0 and 0 for z < 0.
  • Sigmoid, \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}: it maps any real number into (0, 1), with \sigma(0) = 0.5, \sigma(z) \to 1 as z \to +\infty and \sigma(z) \to 0 as z \to -\infty.

A layer is many neurons at once. Put h neurons side by side, each with its own weights. Their weight vectors form the columns of a matrix W \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times h}, and their biases form a row b \in \mathbb{R}^{1\times h}. The code stores the n points as the rows of a matrix X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d}, so a single matrix product evaluates every neuron on every point:

Z = XW + b ,

with b added to every row. Entry Z_{ik} is neuron k's pre-activation on point i.

The network in this chapter has a hidden layer of 8 ReLU neurons and one sigmoid output neuron:

z_1 = X W_1 + b_1,\qquad a_1 = \mathrm{ReLU}(z_1),\qquad z_2 = a_1 W_2 + b_2,\qquad \hat y = \sigma(z_2).

In total there are 2\cdot 8 + 8 + 8 + 1 = 33 numbers to learn.

Why the nonlinearity is essential. Remove the ReLU, so that a_1 = z_1. Then

z_2 = (XW_1 + b_1)W_2 + b_2 = X\,(W_1W_2) + (b_1W_2 + b_2) = X W' + b' .

W' = W_1W_2 is a 2\times 1 matrix and b' is one number: the two layers collapse into a single neuron, and the boundary is a straight line again. Any stack of linear layers is one linear layer in disguise. The ReLU breaks this. Each hidden unit is flat on one side of its own line and rises linearly on the other, and a weighted sum of 8 such bent pieces can form a curved boundary. In the demo at the top, press no nonlinearity and the boundary straightens, however long you train; press show the hidden units' lines and you will see the 8 hinges the curved boundary is built from.

What is a neural network?

In plain words: a stack of simple units, each adding up weighted inputs and bending the result, whose weights are adjusted until the whole stack maps inputs to the right outputs. The bends are what let it draw curved boundaries.

More precisely: a composition of affine maps and elementwise nonlinearities, \hat y = \sigma\big(\mathrm{ReLU}(X W_1 + b_1)\,W_2 + b_2\big) here, trained by gradient descent on a loss whose gradient backpropagation computes with the chain rule [2], [4].

Part 2

A probability output and the cross-entropy loss

The label y is 0 or 1. The output \hat y = \sigma(z_2) \in (0,1) is read as the network's estimate of the probability that y = 1. To train the network we need a number that says how well such probabilities match the observed labels.

Treat y as a coin flip, a Bernoulli variable, that lands on 1 with probability \hat y. The probability the model gives to the label actually observed is

P(y \mid x) = \hat y^{\,y}\,(1-\hat y)^{1-y} .

This is \hat y when y = 1 and 1-\hat y when y = 0, since any number to the power 0 equals 1. If the n training labels are independent, the probability of all of them together is the product \prod_i P(y_i \mid x_i), called the likelihood. A good model makes it large. Three small moves turn this into a loss: take the logarithm (a product becomes a sum, and the maximiser does not change because \log is increasing), flip the sign so that we minimise, and divide by n to get an average:

L = -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \big[\,y_i\log \hat y_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat y_i)\,\big].

This is the binary cross-entropy, bce_loss in the code. For one point with y = 1 the loss is -\log\hat y: it is 0.105 when \hat y = 0.9, 0.693 when \hat y = 0.5, and 4.6 when \hat y = 0.01. Confident mistakes cost a lot, and the loss grows without bound as \hat y \to 0.

true label 1: -\log \hat y true label 0: -\log(1 - \hat y)

Part 3

Backpropagation: the chain rule, layer by layer

To improve the weights we need the gradient: how L changes when each weight changes slightly. The network is a chain of functions, X \to z_1 \to a_1 \to z_2 \to \hat y \to L, and the chain rule says that the derivative of a chain is the product of the derivatives of its links. Backpropagation [2] applies the chain rule from the loss backward, reusing each result for the layer below. Write \delta_2 = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_2} and \delta_1 = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_1}; in the code they are dz2 and dz1. (Nielsen's online book derives the same four equations at a gentler pace [6].)

Step 1: the output error. For one point the loss is \ell = -[\,y\log\hat y + (1-y)\log(1-\hat y)\,]. Its derivative with respect to \hat y, over a common denominator, is

\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \hat y} = -\frac{y}{\hat y} + \frac{1-y}{1-\hat y} = \frac{-y(1-\hat y) + (1-y)\hat y}{\hat y(1-\hat y)} = \frac{\hat y - y}{\hat y(1-\hat y)} .

The sigmoid's derivative follows from \sigma(z) = (1+e^{-z})^{-1}:

\sigma'(z) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2} = \sigma(z)\big(1-\sigma(z)\big), \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{d\hat y}{dz_2} = \hat y\,(1-\hat y) .

Multiply the two links:

\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z_2} = \frac{\hat y - y}{\hat y(1-\hat y)}\cdot \hat y(1-\hat y) = \hat y - y .

The awkward fractions cancel exactly. The error signal at the output is "prediction minus truth": positive when the model said too much, negative when it said too little, near zero when it is right. Because L averages over n points, row i of \delta_2 is \frac{\hat y_i - y_i}{n}. This is the first line of backward.

Step 2: the output weights. For point i, z_{2,i} = \sum_k a_{1,ik}W_{2,k} + b_2, so \frac{\partial z_{2,i}}{\partial W_{2,k}} = a_{1,ik}. Adding the contributions of all points:

\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{2,k}} = \sum_i a_{1,ik}\,\delta_{2,i} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial W_2} = a_1^\top \delta_2 ,\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b_2} = \sum_i \delta_{2,i} .

A weight's gradient is (the input flowing into that weight) times (the error at its output), summed over points. A hidden unit that was silent on a point (a_{1,ik} = 0) takes no blame for it.

Step 3: back into the hidden layer. Hidden output a_{1,ik} reaches z_{2,i} through the weight W_{2,k}, so \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_{1,ik}} = \delta_{2,i}W_{2,k}, or in matrix form

\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_1} = \delta_2 W_2^\top \qquad (n\times 8).

Then pass through the ReLU, whose derivative is 1 where z_1 > 0 and 0 elsewhere:

\delta_1 = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_1} = \big(\delta_2 W_2^\top\big) \odot \mathbf{1}[z_1 > 0] .

Here \odot is entry-by-entry multiplication and \mathbf{1}[z_1 > 0] is a mask of zeros and ones, (z1 > 0) in the code. Error flows back only through units that were active.

Step 4: the hidden weights. This is Step 2 again, one layer down, with X as the input:

\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_1} = X^\top \delta_1 \quad (2\times 8),\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b_1} = \sum_i \delta_{1,i} \quad (1\times 8).

Every gradient has the same shape as the parameter it belongs to, which is a useful check on the algebra. A sharper check is arithmetic: nudge one weight up and down by a small h, difference the two losses, and compare with what the four steps say.

Backpropagation is not a new idea about learning; it is the chain rule, organised so that nothing is computed twice.

Part 4

Gradient descent, and one neuron by hand

The gradient points in the direction in which L grows fastest. Gradient descent takes a small step the opposite way, for every parameter \theta:

\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta},

where the learning rate \eta > 0 sets the step size (lr in the code). Why this lowers the loss: writing g for the gradient, a first-order Taylor expansion gives L(\theta - \eta g) \approx L(\theta) - \eta\,\lVert g\rVert^2. The change is negative whenever g is not zero and \eta is small enough for the approximation to hold (chapter 02).

Worked example: one sigmoid neuron with two inputs. Take the input x = (1, 2), label y = 1, weights w = (0.5, -0.25), bias b = 0 and \eta = 0.5. For one neuron the output shortcut is the same, \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z} = \hat y - y, and Step 2 gives \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial w_j} = (\hat y - y)\,x_j and \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial b} = \hat y - y. Change any of the numbers, and the table is worked out again.

stepcomputationvalue

With the notebook's numbers, the new parameters give z = 0.75\cdot1 + 0.25\cdot2 + 0.25 = 1.5, so \hat y = \sigma(1.5) \approx 0.818 and the loss is -\log 0.818 \approx 0.201, down from 0.693 after one step. The input x_2 = 2 is twice x_1 = 1, so w_2 moved twice as far as w_1: each weight is adjusted in proportion to the input that flowed through it.

In the code. forward computes z1, a1, z2 and y_hat as in Part 1. backward computes, in order, dz2 = \frac{\hat y - y}{n}, dW2 = a_1^\top\delta_2, db2, da1 = \delta_2 W_2^\top, dz1 (with the ReLU mask), dW1 = X^\top\delta_1 and db1: the four steps of Part 3. The training loop repeats forward pass, loss, backward pass and the update of this section, with lr = 0.5, for 3000 passes over the whole training set.

Part 5

The problem

make_moons: two interleaving crescents. Not linearly separable, no straight line separates the classes, which is exactly why we need a nonlinear model at all. A single-layer (linear) model provably cannot solve this; a network with even one hidden layer of nonlinear units can [3].

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

import mlutils
mlutils.set_style()
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

X, y = make_moons(n_samples=400, noise=0.2, random_state=42)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=42)
y_train_col, y_test_col = y_train.reshape(-1, 1), y_test.reshape(-1, 1)

400 points, 300 for training and 100 held out, 50 of each class in the test set; they are the dots and diamonds of the demo at the top. The noise of 0.2 makes the crescents overlap a little where they interleave, so even the right curve will miss a few points.

Part 6

The code: forward pass, loss, backward pass

The network of Part 1: one hidden layer of 8 ReLU units and a sigmoid output. Parameters start as small random values, scaled by \sqrt{\frac{2}{\text{fan-in}}} (He initialisation [5]); starting them at exactly zero would make every hidden unit compute the same thing forever (a classic bug worth knowing about).

n_in, n_hidden, n_out = 2, 8, 1

W1 = rng.normal(size=(n_in, n_hidden)) * np.sqrt(2 / n_in)
b1 = np.zeros((1, n_hidden))
W2 = rng.normal(size=(n_hidden, n_out)) * np.sqrt(2 / n_hidden)
b2 = np.zeros((1, n_out))

def relu(z):
    return np.maximum(0, z)

def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

def forward(X, W1, b1, W2, b2):
    z1 = X @ W1 + b1
    a1 = relu(z1)
    z2 = a1 @ W2 + b2
    y_hat = sigmoid(z2)
    return z1, a1, z2, y_hat

The loss is the binary cross-entropy of Part 2, and backward codes the four steps of Part 3, starting from the output error \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_2} = \frac{\hat y - y}{n} and working down to the gradients of W1 and b1.

def backward(X, y, z1, a1, y_hat, W2):
    n = len(X)
    dz2 = (y_hat - y) / n                    # dL/dz2, the sigmoid+cross-entropy shortcut
    dW2 = a1.T @ dz2
    db2 = dz2.sum(axis=0, keepdims=True)

    da1 = dz2 @ W2.T
    dz1 = da1 * (z1 > 0)                     # ReLU'(z1): 1 where z1>0, else 0
    dW1 = X.T @ dz1
    db1 = dz1.sum(axis=0, keepdims=True)
    return dW1, db1, dW2, db2

def bce_loss(y, y_hat, eps=1e-9):
    return -np.mean(y * np.log(y_hat + eps) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - y_hat + eps))

The eps keeps the logarithm finite if a probability ever rounds to exactly 0 or 1. The gradient-check widget of Part 3 runs exactly these lines, with the notebook's initial weights, against finite differences.

Part 7

Training

Plain (full-batch) gradient descent: compute the loss and its gradient on the whole training set, take a step, repeat.

lr = 0.5
losses, test_accs = [], []
for epoch in range(3000):
    z1, a1, z2, y_hat = forward(X_train, W1, b1, W2, b2)
    loss = bce_loss(y_train_col, y_hat)
    dW1, db1, dW2, db2 = backward(X_train, y_train_col, z1, a1, y_hat, W2)
    W1 -= lr * dW1; b1 -= lr * db1
    W2 -= lr * dW2; b2 -= lr * db2

    if epoch % 20 == 0:
        losses.append((epoch, loss))
        _, _, _, y_hat_test = forward(X_test, W1, b1, W2, b2)
        test_accs.append(((y_hat_test > 0.5).astype(int) == y_test_col).mean())

print(f"final training loss: {loss:.4f}")
print(f"final test accuracy: {test_accs[-1]:.3f}")
final training loss: 0.0674
final test accuracy: 0.960

The demo at the top replays this run exactly, from the same initial weights, and its epoch slider is the notebook's log every 20 epochs. Two things are worth watching. First the plateau: the test accuracy climbs to 0.84 by epoch 500 and sits there until past epoch 1000 while the loss creeps from 0.25 to 0.21; then, between 1000 and 1500, the hidden units find the second bend, the loss drops to 0.08 and the accuracy jumps to 0.95. A flat stretch is not the end of learning. Second the boundary: it bends around the two moons, something no linear model could produce, however it is trained. That curvature comes entirely from the ReLU nonlinearity between the two layers; remove it (make the hidden layer linear) and the whole network collapses back to an equivalent single linear model, boundary included. The training accuracy ends at 0.973, the test accuracy at 0.960: the few points left are in the overlap of the crescents.

Thirty-three numbers, the chain rule and a step downhill, three thousand times: that is all a neural network is.

Part 8

The chapter on one page

  1. A neuron is a weighted sum and a bend; a layer is many of them, one matrix product.
  2. No bend, no curve: linear layers compose to one linear layer, so the ReLU is what buys a curved boundary.
  3. The loss is the negative log-likelihood of the labels, the binary cross-entropy; confident mistakes cost without bound.
  4. Backpropagation: the chain rule from the loss backward; sigmoid plus cross-entropy gives the output error \hat y - y.
  5. Gradient descent lowers the loss by \eta\lVert g\rVert^2 per step, if the step is small enough; too large, and ReLU units die rather than the loss exploding.
  6. On the moons: 33 numbers reach 0.960 test accuracy, after a plateau at 0.84.

Part 9

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

  1. Remove the nonlinearity. Change relu(z1) to just z1 (the identity, no nonlinearity) in forward and retrain. What does the decision boundary look like now, and why?
    Hint

    The demo's no nonlinearity button does this. Remember to drop the ReLU mask in backward too, or the gradient will be wrong.

    Solution
    def forward_linear(X, W1, b1, W2, b2):
    z1 = X @ W1 + b1
    a1 = z1                                  # no nonlinearity
    z2 = a1 @ W2 + b2
    return z1, a1, z2, sigmoid(z2)
# train with forward_linear, and in backward use dz1 = da1 (no ReLU mask)

    With a linear hidden layer, \hat y = \sigma\big((XW_1 + b_1)W_2 + b_2\big) = \sigma\big(X(W_1W_2) + \dots\big): the composition of two linear layers is still linear, so the boundary is a straight line no matter how the network trains. The best line through the moons gets a training accuracy of 0.843 and a test accuracy of 0.790, with a final loss of 0.278 against 0.067: the price of the missing bend.

  2. Learning rate and hidden width. Try n_hidden = 2 and n_hidden = 32, and try lr = 0.05 and lr = 2.0. Which combination trains fastest? Where does the learning rate start to do harm, and what does the harm look like? Relate it to chapter 02's gradient descent exercise.
    Hint

    The demo's buttons cover all of these and more. Watch the loss curve, and the count of dead hidden units, as \eta grows.

    Solution

    Width first. With 2 hidden units the accuracy plateaus near 0.79: two hinges cannot bend a boundary around two crescents. With 32 units and \eta = 0.5 (restarting the notebook from the top, so that the weights are drawn afresh) the network reaches 0.95 test accuracy by epoch 480 instead of 1380, and ends at 0.960. Learning rate next, with 8 units. At 0.05 the loss is still 0.273 after 3000 epochs, test accuracy 0.80: safe but far too slow. At 2.0, contrary to what one might guess, training is faster: 0.95 by epoch 320, final test accuracy 0.95. Harm begins around 5: the loss rises on about half of the steps, as it did past the curvature limit in chapter 02, and the final test accuracy falls to 0.82. At 10, six of the eight hidden units are dead by the end; at 50 and 100 all eight are: a step that large pushes a unit's pre-activation negative on every point, its ReLU then outputs 0 everywhere, its gradient is 0, and nothing can bring it back. The loss never becomes infinite here; it settles at 0.699, the loss of a constant output. That is what "too large a learning rate" looks like in a ReLU network: not an explosion, but a network that quietly stops having units.

  3. Check the gradient. Pick one weight, say W1[0, 0], nudge it by \pm h with h = 10^{-6}, difference the losses and compare with backward's value. Repeat for a bias and for W2. What happens if h is 10^{-1}, or 10^{-11}?
    Hint

    The widget of Part 3 does this for eight of the 33 parameters. Use the centred difference, \frac{L(\theta+h) - L(\theta-h)}{2h}; its error is of order h^2.

    Solution
    z1, a1, z2, y_hat = forward(X_train, W1, b1, W2, b2)
dW1, db1, dW2, db2 = backward(X_train, y_train_col, z1, a1, y_hat, W2)
h = 1e-6
Wp, Wm = W1.copy(), W1.copy(); Wp[0, 0] += h; Wm[0, 0] -= h
fd = (bce_loss(y_train_col, forward(X_train, Wp, b1, W2, b2)[3])
      - bce_loss(y_train_col, forward(X_train, Wm, b1, W2, b2)[3])) / (2 * h)
print(dW1[0, 0], fd, abs(dW1[0, 0] - fd))

    At the initial weights the two agree to about 10^{-10} for every entry of W1, and likewise for the biases and W2, and they still agree after training. If exercise 1's linear forward were paired with the masked backward, the check would fail by a wide margin: a mismatch like that is exactly the kind of bug it exists to catch. With h = 0.1 the loss curves between the two evaluations and the quotient drifts from the derivative; with h = 10^{-11} the two losses agree to almost all their 16 digits and their difference is mostly rounding noise. A gradient check is the standard way to find a bug in a hand-written backward, and it is worth running once for every new layer.

  4. The plateau. In the notebook's run the test accuracy sits at 0.84 from epoch 500 to past epoch 1000, then jumps to 0.95. What is the network doing during the plateau, and what changes?
    Hint

    Scrub the demo's epoch slider through the plateau with the hidden units' lines showing. Watch which hinges move.

    Solution

    During the plateau the boundary has a single bend: it separates the two moons except where each crescent curls past the other, and that is about what one bend can do; 43 of the 300 training points are still wrong at epoch 500, nearly all of them where the crescents interleave. Because only a seventh of the points are wrong, the gradients are small (the hidden weights move by about a thousandth per step), and the hinges turn slowly and one at a time: between epochs 500 and 1,000 two of the eight units swing by 40° to 50° while the rest barely move, and between 1,000 and 1,400 four more swing by 30° to 45°. Only then do enough hinges cross the tips for a second bend; the loss drops from 0.21 to 0.08, the wrong training points fall from 43 to 10, and the test accuracy jumps to 0.95. Plateaus like this are common in training curves; a flat loss is not proof that the network has finished learning.

Random forest

A single decision tree overfits easily: grown deep enough, it memorises its training set. A random forest grows many trees, each on a resample of the data with a random subset of features at every split, and averages their votes. The randomness decorrelates the trees' mistakes, so the average is steadier than any one tree. Below, 303 cardiac patients: grow the forest yourself, one tree at a time.

Part 1

A decision tree: splitting into purer groups

A decision tree predicts by asking a sequence of yes/no questions, such as "is chol above 250?". Each question is a split: it picks one feature j and a threshold s, and sends a patient left if x_j \le s and right otherwise. Training starts with all training patients in one node, the root. It picks the best split, applies it, and then repeats the same procedure inside each of the two new nodes, and so on (recursive splitting) [3]. A node that is not split any further is a leaf, and it predicts the majority class of the training patients that reached it. Geometrically, the tree cuts the feature space into boxes with sides parallel to the axes.

Impurity. "Best split" needs a score. Consider a node and let p_k be the fraction of its patients in class k, for k = 1, \dots, K (below, K = 2). The Gini impurity is

G = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k^2.

It has a direct reading: draw two patients at random (with replacement) from the node; G is the probability that they belong to different classes. A pure node has G = 0. With two classes, the worst case is a 50/50 mix, where G = 1 - 0.5^2 - 0.5^2 = 0.5. A common alternative is the entropy, measured in bits:

H = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k \log_2 p_k.

It is also 0 for a pure node and reaches 1 bit for a 50/50 mix. Both scores behave very similarly; scikit-learn uses Gini by default.

Impurity decrease. A split sends n_L of the node's n patients left and n_R right. Its quality is the drop in impurity, with each child weighted by its share of the patients:

\Delta G = G_{\text{parent}} - \frac{n_L}{n}\, G_L - \frac{n_R}{n}\, G_R.

The weights matter: a large impure child costs more than a tiny one. At every node the tree tries all features and thresholds and keeps the split with the largest \Delta G. It is greedy: it takes the best single step and never looks ahead.

The tree chooses split B. Entropy agrees: split B lowers it from 1 bit to 0.722 bits, a gain of 0.278 bits, against 0.125 bits for split A. Try other splits of the same ten patients:

What is a random forest?

In plain words: many decision trees, each grown on a slightly different version of the data and allowed to look at only a few features at each question, voting together. No single tree is trusted; their average is.

More precisely: an ensemble of B CART trees, each fitted to a bootstrap sample of the training set with m features drawn at random as candidates at every split, whose class probabilities are averaged [1], [4]. The bootstrap and the feature subsets lower the correlation \rho between trees, and the average's variance falls to \rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2.

Part 2

Why one deep tree overfits

Left alone, a tree keeps splitting until every leaf is pure. It then classifies its training set almost perfectly. But the deepest splits are decided by two or three patients each, so they follow the noise in those particular patients rather than a real pattern. Replace a handful of training patients and the tree can change completely, from the first split down, and its predictions change with it.

This is high variance, in the sense of chapter 00. Think of the training set as one random draw from a population of possible training sets. The prediction \hat f(x) of the fitted tree for a new patient x is then a random quantity too. Its expected squared error splits into three parts: bias squared (how far the average prediction is from the truth), variance (how much the prediction jumps from one training set to another), and irreducible noise. A deep tree has low bias but high variance. Averaging many trees trained on independent training sets would keep the low bias and shrink the variance. We only have one training set, so a random forest manufactures new ones.

Part 3

From trees to a forest: bootstrap, averaging, random features

Bootstrap samples. Each tree gets its own training set: n patients drawn with replacement from the n training patients [2]. Some patients are drawn twice or more, and some are not drawn at all. A given patient is missed by one draw with probability 1 - \frac{1}{n}, so it is missed by all n independent draws with probability

\Big(1 - \frac{1}{n}\Big)^n \;\longrightarrow\; e^{-1} \approx 0.368 \quad \text{as } n \text{ grows}.

For n = 3: (\frac{2}{3})^3 = \frac{8}{27} \approx 0.296. So each tree sees about 63% of the distinct patients. The other 37% are its out-of-bag patients, a free test set for that tree (scikit-learn reports the resulting score with oob_score=True; the demo at the top shows it as the green curve). Draw a sample:

Averaging correlated predictors. Let T_1, \dots, T_B be the predictions of B trees at the same input x. Each has variance \sigma^2, and any two have correlation \rho: they are not independent, because their bootstrap samples overlap. The forest outputs the average \bar T = \frac{1}{B} \sum_b T_b. Its variance is the sum of all B^2 covariances, divided by B^2. There are B diagonal terms, each \sigma^2, and B(B-1) off-diagonal terms, each \rho\sigma^2:

\mathrm{Var}(\bar T) = \frac{1}{B^2}\Big(B\sigma^2 + B(B-1)\,\rho\sigma^2\Big) = \rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2.

The second term shrinks as trees are added; the first does not. More trees always help, but only down to a floor \rho\sigma^2 set by how similar the trees are [4]. With \sigma^2 = 1:

\rho = 0.510.550.5050.5
\rho = 0.210.280.2080.2

Random feature subsets. The only way to lower the floor is to lower \rho. If one feature is very strong, nearly every bootstrap tree splits on it first, and the trees end up alike. A random forest therefore lets each split choose only among m features drawn at random from the p available; a common choice for classification is m \approx \sqrt{p} [1]. Each individual tree gets a little worse, but the trees become much less correlated, and the average wins. The demo's third slider sets m: push it to 13, and the trees differ only through their bootstrap samples.

Voting. Each tree votes for a class and the forest predicts the majority. scikit-learn uses a soft version: it averages the trees' class probabilities and picks the largest, which almost always gives the same answer.

Feature importance. Every split in every tree lowers impurity by some \Delta G. Add up these decreases for all splits that use feature j, weighting each by the fraction \frac{n_t}{N} of training samples that reach that node t, and average over the trees:

\mathrm{Imp}(j) = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \ \sum_{\text{nodes } t \text{ of tree } b \text{ that split on } j} \frac{n_t}{N}\, \Delta G_t.

The scores are then rescaled to sum to 1. This is the mean decrease in impurity. It is computed on the training data, and it tends to favour features with many possible thresholds [5], so it is a quick guide rather than a final verdict; Part 7 sets a second kind of importance beside it.

In the code below. X has p = 13 clinical features and y is output, coded 1 or 0. DecisionTreeClassifier grows one unrestricted Gini tree: the high-variance learner of Part 2. RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300) grows B = 300 trees, each on a bootstrap sample of the 227 training patients and, by default, with m = \lfloor\sqrt{13}\rfloor = 3 candidate features per split, then averages them. The loop over tree_counts traces how test accuracy settles as B grows, and forest.feature_importances_ is the mean decrease in impurity defined above.

Part 4

The data

303 patients referred for a cardiac evaluation, 13 clinical measurements, and output, coded 1 or 0. The columns are the Kaggle release of the Cleveland heart-disease data [6], [7]: a realistic mix of continuous measurements (cholesterol, maximum heart rate) and coded categorical ones (chest-pain type, ECG results). The notebook's structure loosely follows a Kaggle tutorial [8], reworked with this dataset and a focus on feature importance.

What does output = 1 mean? The Kaggle page, and the first version of this chapter, read it as "heart disease found". The measurements say the opposite. Averaged by group, the output = 1 patients have exercise-induced angina 14% of the time against 55%, an ST depression of 0.58 against 1.59, a peak heart rate of 158 against 139, 0.36 blocked vessels on the angiogram against 1.17, and are four years younger: on every known risk sign, output = 1 is the healthier group. Part 5

One tree against a forest

First, a single unrestricted decision tree. It can split on anything, as many times as it wants, so it fits the training set perfectly and pays for it on new data.

tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=42).fit(X_train, y_train)
print(f"single tree — train accuracy: {tree.score(X_train, y_train):.3f}, "
      f"test accuracy: {accuracy_score(y_test, tree.predict(X_test)):.3f}")
single tree — train accuracy: 1.000, test accuracy: 0.724
forest = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=42).fit(X_train, y_train)
acc_forest = accuracy_score(y_test, forest.predict(X_test))
print(f"forest (300 trees) — train accuracy: {forest.score(X_train, y_train):.3f}, "
      f"test accuracy: {acc_forest:.3f}")
forest (300 trees) — train accuracy: 1.000, test accuracy: 0.789

Both score 1.000 on the training set. Each tree alone sees only a bootstrap resample and 3 features per split, but 300 of them together still reproduce every training label, as any patient's in-bag trees vote for it. Memorising the training set is not what separates them; the test set is: the forest gets 60 of the 76 held-out patients right, the tree 55. That is 5 patients on one split. Across 20 other random splits of the same data the forest averages 0.841 against 0.753 for the tree, and beats it on 19 of the 20; ten-fold cross-validation gives 0.832 against 0.775. The gain is real, and larger than this one split shows.

Part 6

How many trees are enough?

Adding trees never raises the variance of the average (Part 3), and a tree costs only compute; the question is how many are needed before the average stops moving.

tree_counts = [1, 2, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200, 300]
accs = []
for n in tree_counts:
    m = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=n, random_state=42).fit(X_train, y_train)
    accs.append(accuracy_score(y_test, m.predict(X_test)))
print({n: round(float(a), 3) for n, a in zip(tree_counts, accs)})
{1: 0.711, 2: 0.724, 5: 0.776, 10: 0.829, 25: 0.776, 50: 0.763, 100: 0.803, 200: 0.789, 300: 0.789}

The curve rises steeply over the first ten trees and then wanders between 0.76 and 0.83. Those wanderings are two to five patients out of 76, and a forest with another random seed wanders differently (over 20 seeds, 300 trees score between 0.763 and 0.803 on this split). The test accuracy at 10 trees, 0.829, is the luckiest point, not the best setting. The demo at the top draws the same curve for every number of trees from 1 to 300, and the out-of-bag curve beside it, which uses the 227 training patients and is steadier.

Part 7

Which features matter?

Part 3 defined each feature's importance as its mean decrease in impurity: the impurity reduction of every split on that feature, weighted by how many samples reach the split, averaged over all trees.

cp (chest-pain type), oldpeak (ST depression on exercise) and thall (the thallium stress test) come out on top, all signs a cardiologist would look at, which is a reasonable check that the model learned something sensible rather than an artefact. Two cautions. Importance says which features the trees split on, not in which direction they act, so it cannot tell whether output = 1 is the sick or the healthy group. And the middle of the list (chol, age, trtbps) holds continuous features with hundreds of thresholds to choose from, exactly the ones this measure flatters. A second measure asks a different question: shuffle one feature's values among the test patients, and see how much accuracy the forest loses.

Exercise 4 runs the permutation test in the notebook. The two measures agree on oldpeak and caa, and disagree on the continuous features and on cp; with 76 test patients, the permutation test is itself noisy. A feature's importance is a property of the forest and the data together, not of the feature alone.

Part 8

The chapter on one page

  1. A tree splits greedily, one feature and one threshold at a time, to lower the Gini impurity; its leaves predict the majority.
  2. A deep tree memorises: training accuracy 1, test accuracy 0.724. Its predictions swing with the training set: high variance, low bias.
  3. Bootstrap samples manufacture new training sets; each leaves out about 37% of the patients, a free test set.
  4. Averaging B trees with correlation \rho gives variance \rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2: a floor set by \rho, which random feature subsets lower.
  5. On the patients: 300 trees get 60 of 76 right against the tree's 55; the gain holds across splits and in cross-validation.
  6. Importance: the mean decrease in impurity is quick and flatters many-threshold features; a permutation test asks what the forest actually relies on.

Part 9

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

  1. Feature ablation. Drop the top feature from X entirely and retrain. Does the test accuracy drop by much? What does that tell you about redundancy between features?
    Hint

    Split the reduced X2 with the same random_state and stratify, so the test patients stay the same. Then try dropping the second and third features too.

    Solution
    top_feature = importances.index[0]
X2 = X.drop(columns=[top_feature])
X2_train, X2_test, _, _ = train_test_split(X2, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=42, stratify=y)
m2 = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=42).fit(X2_train, y_train)
print(accuracy_score(y_test, m2.predict(X2_test)))

    Without cp, the most important feature, the test accuracy is 0.776: one patient fewer than 0.789. Dropping oldpeak instead gives 0.763, dropping thall 0.789, unchanged. Other features carry overlapping information (chest pain, ST depression, the stress test and exercise angina all reflect the same ischaemia), so the forest routes around a missing one. Importance measures what the forest used, not what it needed.

  2. Depth-limited trees. Retrain the single DecisionTreeClassifier with max_depth=3. Compare its train and test accuracy with the unrestricted tree's. Is a shallow single tree competitive with the forest here?
    Hint

    The tree widget of Part 2 draws exactly this tree at depth 3, and the table below it has the whole sweep.

    Solution
    tree3 = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=3, random_state=42).fit(X_train, y_train)
print(tree3.score(X_train, y_train), tree3.score(X_test, y_test), tree3.get_n_leaves())

    The depth-3 tree has 8 leaves, a training accuracy of 0.859 and a test accuracy of 0.737: one patient better than the unrestricted tree's 0.724, and four short of the forest's 0.789. Limiting the depth trades training accuracy for stability, but on these 76 patients the difference between a depth-3 tree and a full tree is within the noise, and neither reaches the forest. Across 20 random splits the depth-3 tree averages 0.774, between the full tree's 0.753 and the forest's 0.841: the forest averages away the variance without giving up any tree's depth.

  3. The free test set. Refit the 300-tree forest with oob_score=True and compare its out-of-bag score with the test accuracy. Why can it be computed at all, and why might it differ from the test accuracy?
    Hint

    Each training patient is out of the bag for about 37% of the trees, 110 or so of the 300. Those trees never saw it, so their vote on it is an honest prediction.

    Solution
    oob = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=42, oob_score=True).fit(X_train, y_train)
print(oob.oob_score_)

    The out-of-bag score is 0.819, against 0.789 on the test set. Each training patient is predicted by the trees whose bootstrap sample missed it, about 110 of the 300, so every one of the 227 training patients gets an honest prediction without any held-out data. The two estimates differ because they are measured on different patients (227 against 76) and the out-of-bag vote uses a third of the forest; with these sample sizes a three-point gap is ordinary. When data are scarce, the out-of-bag score lets the whole data set be used for training and still gives an estimate of the test accuracy.

  4. Permutation importance. Compute the permutation importance of each feature on the test set with sklearn.inspection.permutation_importance, and compare its ranking with feature_importances_. Where do the two disagree, and why?

    The widget of Part 7 has a button for it. Look at the continuous features, and at the sign of the smallest values.

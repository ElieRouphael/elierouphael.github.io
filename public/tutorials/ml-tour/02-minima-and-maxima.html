Part 1

Slopes, critical points and curvature

The calculus that finds a function's minima and maxima is what most of the training in this course runs on, from chapter 01's least squares to the gradient descent and backpropagation of chapters 06 to 09 and the federated training of chapter 11. (A few chapters train differently: chapter 04's trees grow greedily, split by split, and chapter 05's k-means alternates two simple steps.)

Picture walking along the graph of a function f(x). The derivative measures how steep the ground is at each point: the rise over the run of a tiny step h, in the limit where the step shrinks to nothing [1]:

f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}.

The fraction is the slope of the chord between x and x+h; as h shrinks, the chord becomes the tangent. For f(x) = x^2 at x = 3,

\frac{(3+h)^2 - 9}{h} = \frac{6h + h^2}{h} = 6 + h.

For h = 1, 0.1, 0.01 it equals 7, 6.1, 6.01, closing in on f'(3) = 6, which matches the rule f'(x) = 2x. Shrink h yourself:

f(x) = x^2 chord tangent

At the top of a hill or the bottom of a valley the ground is flat. So every smooth local maximum or minimum x^* satisfies

f'(x^*) = 0.

Points where the derivative vanishes are called critical points. They are only candidates: f(x) = x^3 is flat at 0 yet keeps rising on both sides. To sort the candidates, zoom in with a Taylor expansion. Near any point x, a smooth function is well described by its value, its slope and its curvature:

f(x + h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,h + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(x)\,h^2.

Here f''(x), the second derivative, is the slope of the slope: it measures how the graph bends. The three terms are a height, a straight tilt, and a parabola bending up or down. At a critical point the tilt is zero, so

f(x^* + h) - f(x^*) \approx \tfrac{1}{2} f''(x^*)\,h^2.

Since h^2 > 0 for every small step, left or right, the sign of f''(x^*) decides everything. This is the second-derivative test:

  • f''(x^*) > 0: every nearby point is higher, so x^* is a local minimum.
  • f''(x^*) < 0: every nearby point is lower, so x^* is a local maximum.
  • f''(x^*) = 0: the parabola term vanishes and the test is silent. Higher-order terms decide: x^3 has an inflection point at 0, x^4 a minimum.

Part 5 lets you try the test on four functions, these two among them.

What does it mean to train a model?

In plain words: to adjust the model's numbers until its mistakes on the training data are as small as possible. The mistakes are measured by one number, the loss, and training looks for the bottom of the loss.

More precisely: to minimise a loss function L(w) over the parameters w, with the data held fixed. At a smooth minimum the gradient vanishes, \nabla L(w^*) = 0; when that equation cannot be solved on paper, an iterative method such as gradient descent approaches w^* step by step [3], [4].

Part 2

Several variables: gradient and Hessian

A model usually has many parameters, collected in a vector w = (w_1, \dots, w_p); L(w) returns one number. The partial derivative \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_j} is the ordinary derivative along coordinate j, all other coordinates held fixed. Stacking the p of them gives the gradient, a p \times 1 vector:

\nabla L(w) = \Big(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}, \dots, \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_p}\Big)^{\!\top}.

The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is the slope in that direction. The second derivatives form the symmetric p \times p Hessian matrix H, with entries H_{jk} = \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial w_j \partial w_k}: it records how the surface bends in every direction. For a small step vector h (p \times 1), the Taylor expansion keeps its shape:

L(w + h) \approx L(w) + \nabla L(w)^\top h + \tfrac{1}{2}\, h^\top H\, h.

A critical point now means \nabla L(w^*) = 0: all p slopes vanish together. There, L(w^* + h) - L(w^*) \approx \tfrac{1}{2} h^\top H h. If h^\top H h > 0 for every nonzero h (H is positive definite), every direction leads uphill and w^* is a local minimum. If it is negative for every h, a local maximum. If the sign depends on the direction, w^* is a saddle point.

Two examples. L = w_1^2 + 3w_2^2 has gradient (2w_1, 6w_2), zero at the origin, and h^\top H h = 2h_1^2 + 6h_2^2 > 0: a minimum, the bottom of a bowl. L = w_1^2 - w_2^2 gives h^\top H h = 2h_1^2 - 2h_2^2, positive along w_1 and negative along w_2: a saddle, shaped like a mountain pass. Part 3

Training as minimisation: gradient descent

A model's loss function L(w) measures how wrong its predictions are on the training data, as a function of its parameters w; the data stay fixed. Training means finding the w that makes L smallest. Solving \nabla L = 0 on paper is rarely possible, so we walk downhill instead.

Deriving the step. Keep only the first-order Taylor term (one parameter first): L(w + h) \approx L(w) + L'(w)\,h. Choose the step h = -\eta\, L'(w) with a small number \eta > 0. Then

L\big(w - \eta L'(w)\big) \approx L(w) - \eta\, L'(w)^2.

The change -\eta L'(w)^2 is negative whenever the slope is not zero, so the step lowers the loss. This gives gradient descent [2]:

w \leftarrow w - \eta\, L'(w).

The positive number \eta is the learning rate, or step size. With many parameters the rule is w \leftarrow w - \eta \nabla L(w), and the loss drops by about \eta \lVert \nabla L \rVert^2 per step.

How big a step? The Taylor argument only holds for small steps. A quadratic shows exactly what happens. Take L(w) = a\,(w - w^*)^2 with curvature a > 0 and minimum at w^*. Its slope is L'(w) = 2a(w - w^*), so one step gives

w_{\text{new}} - w^* = (w - w^*) - 2a\eta\,(w - w^*) = (1 - 2a\eta)\,(w - w^*).

Each step multiplies the signed distance to the minimum by the same number 1 - 2a\eta, so t steps multiply it by (1 - 2a\eta)^t. Its size, the contraction factor \lvert 1 - 2a\eta \rvert, tells the whole story:

  • \eta tiny: the factor is close to 1 and progress is slow.
  • \eta = \frac{1}{2a}: the factor is 0 and one step lands exactly on w^*.
  • \frac{1}{2a} < \eta < \frac{1}{a}: 1 - 2a\eta lies between -1 and 0, so w overshoots, zig-zags from side to side, and still converges.
  • \eta = \frac{1}{a}: the factor is exactly -1, and w bounces between two points forever, neither converging nor exploding.
  • \eta > \frac{1}{a}: \lvert 1 - 2a\eta \rvert > 1, every step overshoots further than the last, and the loss explodes.

The five buttons of the demo at the top are these five cases. Near any minimum, Taylor says a smooth loss looks like such a quadratic, with a = \tfrac{1}{2} L''(w^*). So the more sharply the loss curves, the smaller the learning rate must be.

Convexity. A function is convex if the chord between any two points of its graph never passes below the graph; in one variable this means L''(w) \ge 0 everywhere. Then every local minimum is a global minimum [5]. Suppose w were a local minimum but some v had L(v) < L(w). On the segment between them convexity gives

L\big(w + \lambda(v - w)\big) \le (1-\lambda)L(w) + \lambda L(v) < L(w)

for every 0 < \lambda \le 1, even for tiny \lambda, right next to w: a contradiction. The squared-error loss of linear regression is convex, so gradient descent cannot get stuck there; the loss of a neural network is not.

Three steps by hand. Take L(w) = (w - 3)^2, so a = 1 and w^* = 3, with \eta = 0.25 and the start w_0 = 0. The slope is L'(w) = 2(w - 3) and the factor is 1 - 2 \times 1 \times 0.25 = 0.5. Change \eta, and the table is worked out again.

The learning rate must respect the curvature: too small and training crawls, too large and it explodes.

Part 4

Critical points, by hand

A smooth function's slope is zero at each of its local peaks and valleys (Part 1). Take a toy cubic:

f(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x + 1

sympy can differentiate it symbolically, the same way you would on paper [6].

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import sympy as sp

import mlutils
mlutils.set_style()

x = sp.symbols("x")
f = x**3 - 6*x**2 + 9*x + 1
f_prime = sp.diff(f, x)
f_double = sp.diff(f_prime, x)
print("f (x) =", f)
print("f'(x) =", f_prime)
print("f''(x) =", f_double)
f (x) = x**3 - 6*x**2 + 9*x + 1
f'(x) = 3*x**2 - 12*x + 9
f''(x) = 6*x - 12
critical_points = sp.solve(sp.Eq(f_prime, 0), x)
critical_points
[1, 3]

f'(x) = 0 has two solutions: the slope is flat at x = 1 and x = 3. By hand, 3x^2 - 12x + 9 = 3(x - 1)(x - 3). Those are the only candidates for a local maximum or minimum: everywhere else the function is strictly increasing or decreasing, so it cannot turn around.

Part 5

The second-derivative test

To tell a peak from a valley, look at how the slope itself is changing (Part 1 derives this from the Taylor expansion):

  • f''(x) < 0: the slope is decreasing through zero, a local maximum.
  • f''(x) > 0: the slope is increasing through zero, a local minimum.
for c in critical_points:
    second = f_double.subs(x, c)
    kind = "local maximum" if second < 0 else "local minimum"
    print(f"x = {c}:  f''(x) = {second}  ->  {kind}  (f({c}) = {f.subs(x, c)})")
x = 1:  f''(x) = -6  ->  local maximum  (f(1) = 5)
x = 3:  f''(x) = 6  ->  local minimum  (f(3) = 1)

The loop trusts that f'' is never zero at a critical point, which holds here. Below, slide the point along the curve: the dashed line is the tangent, the orange curve the Taylor parabola of Part 1, and at a critical point the parabola alone decides. Try x^3 and x^4 to see the test fall silent.

Part 6

The same idea trains a model

Part 3 described training as minimising a loss function, usually a high-dimensional version of the pictures above. Take the simplest case: fitting a single slope w through the origin to minimise the mean squared error

L(w) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (y_i - w x_i)^2.

Expanding the square and completing it shows that this is exactly the quadratic of Part 3, plus a constant that does not affect the steps:

L(w) = a\,w^2 - 2b\,w + c = a\Big(w - \frac{b}{a}\Big)^2 + c - \frac{b^2}{a}, \qquad a = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i^2,\ \ b = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i y_i,\ \ c = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i y_i^2.

So the minimum is w^* = \frac{b}{a}, a parabola in w with a closed form: set L'(w) = 0 and solve. The notebook does exactly that with sympy, for n = 50 simulated points scattered around a line of slope 2.3:

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 50
x_data = rng.uniform(0, 10, n)
y_data = 2.3 * x_data + rng.normal(0, 2, n)

w = sp.symbols("w")
L = sum((y_data[i] - w * x_data[i])**2 for i in range(n)) / n
L_prime = sp.diff(L, w)
w_star = sp.solve(sp.Eq(L_prime, 0), w)[0]
print(f"closed-form minimiser: w* = {float(w_star):.4f}")
closed-form minimiser: w* = 2.3342

Then it walks downhill instead, with \eta = lr = 0.002, from w = 0:

# gradient descent: repeatedly step "downhill" instead of solving analytically
L_prime_num = sp.lambdify(w, L_prime, "numpy")
w_gd, lr = 0.0, 0.002
path = [w_gd]
for _ in range(200):
    w_gd = w_gd - lr * L_prime_num(w_gd)
    path.append(w_gd)
print(f"gradient descent after 200 steps: w = {w_gd:.4f}  (target {float(w_star):.4f})")
gradient descent after 200 steps: w = 2.3342  (target 2.3342)

For these data a \approx 36, so each step multiplies the error by about 1 - 2 \times 36 \times 0.002 \approx 0.86, and 200 steps shrink it to less than 10^{-13} of its starting size. The demo at the top of the page runs this same loop, with the same numbers; its defaults reproduce the notebook's run.

Both routes land in the same place: solving L'(w) = 0 directly, or taking small steps opposite the gradient until it flattens out. Closed-form solutions exist for linear regression; almost nothing else in this course has one, which is why gradient descent and backpropagation matter: chapter 06 builds both by hand, and chapters 07 to 09 use them through Keras.

Part 7

The chapter on one page

  1. Derivative: the limit of chord slopes; it is zero at every smooth local maximum or minimum.
  2. Second-derivative test: at a critical point, f'' > 0 is a minimum, f'' < 0 a maximum, and f'' = 0 says nothing.
  3. Several variables: the gradient vanishes at a critical point; the Hessian tells a bowl from a saddle.
  4. Training is minimisation: a loss over the parameters, with the data held fixed.
  5. Gradient descent: w \leftarrow w - \eta \nabla L(w); on a quadratic each step multiplies the error by 1 - 2a\eta, so it converges only for \eta < \frac{1}{a}.
  6. Convexity: every local minimum is global; linear regression's loss is convex, a neural network's is not.

The vocabulary, in one table

WordMeaning
DerivativeThe slope of a function at a point: the limit of the slopes of shrinking chords.
Critical pointA point where the derivative, or the whole gradient, is zero.
Taylor expansionA function near a point, as its value plus a tilt plus a parabola.
GradientThe vector of partial derivatives; it points uphill, most steeply.
HessianThe matrix of second derivatives: how the surface bends in every direction.
Saddle pointA critical point that is a minimum along some directions and a maximum along others.

Part 8

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

  1. A function with two local minima. Let g(x) = x^4 - 4x^2. Find all critical points and classify each with the second-derivative test. Is the test ever silent here?
    Hint

    Factor g'(x). The demo of Part 5 has this function: slide to each critical point and read g''.

    Solution
    g = x**4 - 4*x**2
g_prime = sp.diff(g, x)
g_double = sp.diff(g_prime, x)
pts = sp.solve(sp.Eq(g_prime, 0), x)
for c in pts:
    print(c, g_double.subs(x, c))
# x = -sqrt(2), sqrt(2): f''>0 -> local minima; x = 0: f''<0 -> local maximum

    g'(x) = 4x^3 - 8x = 4x(x^2 - 2) vanishes at x = 0 and x = \pm\sqrt{2}. There g''(x) = 12x^2 - 8 is -8 at 0, a local maximum with g(0) = 0, and 16 at \pm\sqrt{2}, two local minima with g(\pm\sqrt{2}) = -4. The test is never silent here. Both minima are also global, since g(x) = (x^2 - 2)^2 - 4 \ge -4: a function can have several global minima, and gradient descent finds the one on its side of the hill.

  2. Learning rate too large. Rerun the gradient-descent cell with lr = 0.02 (10 times larger), then with lr = 0.03. Before running, predict what happens from the contraction factor 1 - 2a\eta of Part 3, with a = np.mean(x_data**2). Relate the result to what a diverging loss looks like when a neural network trains, in later chapters.
    Hint

    Compute a, then \frac{1}{2a} and \frac{1}{a}: which of the five cases of Part 3 is each learning rate in? The demo at the top has buttons for both.

    Solution

    Here a \approx 36.3, so the threshold is \eta = \frac{1}{a} \approx 0.028. With lr = 0.02 the factor is about -0.45: the path jumps from one side of the minimum to the other but still converges, and the loss falls fast (201.5, 44.0, 12.1, 5.7, …) towards its minimum, 4.03. With lr = 0.03 the factor is about -1.18: each step overshoots further than the last and the loss increases every iteration instead of decreasing (201.5, 276.7, 380.5, 523.8, …), the numerical analogue of an unstable difference equation: after 200 steps w is about -2.4 \times 10^{14}. A training loss that grows step after step is the classic sign of a learning rate that is too large; chapter 06's exercise 2 meets it in a neural network.

  3. Newton's method. Newton's method replaces the fixed learning rate by the curvature: w \leftarrow w - \frac{L'(w)}{L''(w)}. Apply one step to the loss of Part 6, from w = 0. Where does it land, and why? Which learning rate \eta would make gradient descent take the same step?
    Hint

    The loss is a quadratic, a(w - w^*)^2 plus a constant. What are L' and L''?

    Solution
    L_double = sp.diff(L, w, 2)
w_newton = 0 - L_prime.subs(w, 0) / L_double
print(float(w_newton), float(L_double), 1 / float(L_double))

    One step lands exactly on w^* = 2.3342, from any start. The loss is a quadratic, so L'(w) = 2a(w - w^*) and L''(w) = 2a, and the Newton step subtracts exactly w - w^*. It is gradient descent with \eta = \frac{1}{L''} = \frac{1}{2a} \approx 0.0138, the one-step rate of Part 3 (the demo's one step button) [4]. On a loss that is not quadratic, Newton's method needs several steps, but it uses the curvature to choose each one.

  4. A long, narrow valley. Run gradient descent on L(w_1, w_2) = w_1^2 + 10\,w_2^2 from (5, 1). For which learning rates does it converge? How many steps does it need, with \eta = 0.01, 0.05 and 0.09, to bring both coordinates within 0.01 of the minimum?
    Hint

    The gradient is (2w_1, 20w_2): each coordinate is its own one-variable quadratic, with its own contraction factor. The narrow valley of Part 2's demo is this surface.

    Solution
    for lr in (0.01, 0.05, 0.09, 0.1, 0.11):
    w1, w2, t = 5.0, 1.0, 0
    while max(abs(w1), abs(w2)) >= 0.01 and t < 1000:
        w1, w2, t = w1 - lr * 2 * w1, w2 - lr * 20 * w2, t + 1
    print(lr, t, w1, w2)

    At every step w_1 is multiplied by 1 - 2\eta and w_2 by 1 - 20\eta. Both shrink only when 0 < \eta < 0.1: the steep direction, w_2, sets the limit. The flat direction, w_1, then sets the speed: 308 steps at \eta = 0.01, 59 at 0.05 and 32 at 0.09, close to the best possible, 31 steps at \eta = \frac{1}{11}, where both factors have size 0.82. At \eta = 0.1, w_2 bounces between 1 and -1 forever; at 0.11 it explodes. This is why a long, narrow valley, like the elongated bowl of chapter 01, slows gradient descent down.

What machine learning is

Every chapter of this tour learns a function from examples, and every one of them fights the same enemy: a model that fits its training data better and better while predicting new data worse and worse. Below are fifteen noisy points from a smooth curve. Raise the degree of the polynomial fitted through them, and watch the two errors part ways: the error on the training points keeps falling, the error on new points does not.

Part 1

Learning a function from examples

A conventional program is written by hand: the rules go in, the answers come out. A machine-learning program is given examples instead, and finds the rules itself. In Mitchell's classic formulation [1], a program learns from experience E at a task T, as measured by a performance P, if its performance at T, measured by P, improves with E.

The examples are pairs (x_i, y_i), for i = 1, \dots, n: an input x_i (a car's weight, a flower's measurements, a window of sensor readings) and, when it is known, the desired output y_i. The tour's chapters fall into a few families:

  • Supervised learning. Every example comes with its answer y_i, and the goal is to predict the answer for new inputs. When y is a number it is regression (chapter 01's CO2 emissions, chapter 08's next value of a sequence); when y is a category it is classification (chapters 03, 04, 06, 07 and 11).
  • Unsupervised learning. There are no answers, and the goal is to find structure. Chapter 05 groups customers into clusters; chapter 09 learns what normal sensor data look like, and flags what does not fit.
  • The machinery underneath. Chapter 02 is the calculus every training loop runs on, and chapter 10 builds the attention mechanism of transformers by hand, with no training at all.

Reinforcement learning, where an agent learns from rewards rather than from examples, is a third family that this tour does not cover. Here are the three kinds of question the tour asks:

What is machine learning?

In plain words: instead of writing the rules, you show the computer examples, and it adjusts a flexible formula until the formula reproduces them. The test of success is not the examples it has seen, but new ones.

More precisely: choosing, from a family of functions f_\theta, the parameters \theta that minimise a loss on a set of examples, in such a way that the result performs well on new examples drawn from the same distribution [2], [3].

Training set

Test set

Part 2

The shared recipe

Almost every chapter follows the same five steps.

  1. Data: examples (x_i, y_i), split into a part to learn from and a part held back.
  2. Model: a family of functions f_\theta, indexed by parameters \theta: a line in chapter 01, a tree ensemble in chapter 04, a neural network in chapter 06.
  3. Loss: a number that says how badly f_\theta fits the training data,
    L(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell\big(y_i, f_\theta(x_i)\big),
    with a per-example loss \ell such as the squared error (y - \hat y)^2.
  4. Optimiser: a method to find the \theta that makes L small, in closed form (chapter 01) or step by step, by gradient descent (chapters 02 and 06).
  5. Evaluation: the fitted model is judged on data it has never seen.

Step 5 is the subtle one. A model is not useful because it fits the examples it was given; it is useful if it predicts the ones it was not given. The rest of this chapter shows why those two goals part ways.

Part 3

A first experiment: when more flexibility hurts

The data come from a smooth curve, f(x) = \sin(2\pi x) on [0, 1], observed with noise: y = f(x) + \varepsilon, with \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.3^2). There are only n = 15 training examples, and a large set of 5,000 fresh test examples from the same distribution. The models are polynomials of degree d, fitted by least squares [4]. Degree 0 is a constant, degree 1 a line, and each extra degree adds one coefficient, so the family grows more flexible.

def sample(n, rng):
    x = rng.uniform(0, 1, n)
    return x, f(x) + rng.normal(0, SIGMA, n)

def features(x, degree):                       # Legendre polynomials of x mapped to [-1, 1]
    return legendre.legvander(2*x - 1, degree)

x_train, y_train = sample(15, np.random.default_rng(1))
x_test, y_test = sample(5000, np.random.default_rng(99))

The polynomials are written in the Legendre basis, which keeps the least-squares problem well conditioned even at high degree; the fitted curves are the same as with plain powers of x.

The training error falls at every step: a more flexible family can always fit the 15 points at least as well. The test error tells a different story. It falls from degree 0 to degree 3, where it is lowest (0.379), then rises, slowly at first and then explosively. At degree 12, the fitted curve passes close to every training point and swings wildly between them, and its test error is over 100.

  • At low degree the model underfits: it is too rigid to follow the curve, and errs even on its own training data.
  • At high degree it overfits: it bends to follow the noise in the 15 points, which will not be there next time.
  • Neither error can fall far below the noise level, 0.3: no model can predict the random part of y. A training error below it, as at degree 12, is a warning sign, not an achievement.

Fitting the examples is easy; predicting new ones is the point.

Part 4

Bias and variance

Why does the test error first fall and then rise? Imagine repeating the whole experiment on many independent training sets of 15 points. For a fixed input x, the prediction \hat f(x) changes from one training set to the next. Its expected squared error splits into three parts [2], [7]:

\mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat f(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)] - f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}\big[\hat f(x)\big]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}.

The bias is how far the average prediction is from the truth: large for a rigid model. The variance is how much the prediction moves with the training set: large for a flexible model fitted to few points. The noise, \sigma^2 = 0.09, is the floor. See them: each thin line below is the fit to a different training set.

The notebook measures the two on 2,000 training sets, averaged over x. From degree 0 to degree 3 the bias collapses, from 0.50 to 0.005, while the variance grows only a little, from 0.04 to 0.07: the extra flexibility is spent on following the curve. From degree 3 to degree 5, the bias has little left to give, and the variance jumps from 0.07 to 2.6: the flexibility is now spent on following the noise. The sum is smallest at degree 3.

That 2.6 deserves a closer look. It is an average over the 2,000 training sets, and at degree 5 a few of them dominate it:

The worst 1% of the training sets carry 61% of the variance. They are the unlucky draws that leave a wide empty stretch at one end of the interval (median 0.28, against 0.08 over all sets); there the degree-5 polynomial has no data to hold it, and it shoots off. Because a few rare sets dominate, the estimate itself is unsteady: the ten other batches give anything from 2.20 to 21.01 at degree 5, while degree 3 stays between 0.06 and 0.11. The exact number moves, but in every batch the degree-5 variance is dozens to hundreds of times the degree-3 one. A flexible model fitted to few points does not just err a little more on average: now and then, it fails badly. The widget's caption reports the same share for its own batch; press new training sets a few times at degree 5 to watch the variance jump from one batch to the next.

This bias-variance trade-off is the shape behind every model choice in the tour: the depth of chapter 04's trees, the number of chapter 05's clusters, the size of chapter 06's hidden layer.

Part 5

Choosing honestly: validation, and regularisation

Part 3 picked degree 3 by looking at the test error. That is cheating: once the test set has been used to make a choice, its error is no longer an honest estimate of the error on new data (exercise 3 measures how optimistic it becomes). The honest way is to choose using the training data alone.

Cross-validation [6] splits the training set into k folds, fits on k - 1 of them, measures the error on the one left out, and averages over the k choices. It costs k fits, but it never touches the test set.

Regularisation is the other lever: instead of limiting the degree, keep it high and penalise large coefficients. Ridge regression [5] minimises

\sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(y_i - f_\theta(x_i)\big)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j \ge 1} \theta_j^2,

the sum of squared errors plus \lambda times the squared size of the coefficients (all but the constant, which only shifts the curve up or down). The penalty pulls the wild degree-12 fit back towards a smooth curve. Try it:

training error 5-fold cross-validation test error 

def cv_rmse(x, y, degree, k=5, lam=0.0):
    idx = np.arange(len(x)); errors = []
    for fold in np.array_split(idx, k):
        train = np.setdiff1d(idx, fold)
        w = fit(x[train], y[train], degree, lam)
        errors.append(np.mean((features(x[fold], degree) @ w - y[fold])**2))
    return np.sqrt(np.mean(errors))

Cross-validation, using the 15 training points alone, picks degree 3, the same degree the test set favoured. And ridge regression turns the degree-12 disaster (test error 111.6) into a sensible model: with \lambda = 0.1, its test error is 0.390, close to degree 3's 0.379. Too much penalty underfits again (\lambda = 10: 0.619). The penalty's strength is itself a choice, made the same honest way (exercise 2).

The same two tools recur throughout the tour. Chapter 03's soft-margin parameter, chapter 04's limits on tree depth and chapter 06's network size are all knobs on the same trade-off, and every chapter judges its model on held-out data.

Part 7

The chapter on one page

  1. Learning from examples: supervised learning has the answers, unsupervised learning looks for structure; the tour visits both.
  2. One recipe: data, a model family f_\theta, a loss, an optimiser, and an evaluation on held-out data.
  3. Overfitting: training error always falls as a model grows more flexible; test error falls and then rises.
  4. The floor: no model beats the noise; a training error below it is a warning sign.
  5. Bias and variance: flexibility trades bias for variance; more data cuts the variance.
  6. Honest choices: choose with cross-validation, never with the test set, and regularise to keep a flexible model stable.

Part 8

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

  1. More data. Repeat the experiment of Part 3 with n = 100 training points instead of 15. How do the two curves change, and which degree is best now?
    Hint

    The hero demo has a slider for the number of points. In the notebook, draw sample(100, np.random.default_rng(1)) and loop over the degrees.

    Solution

    With 100 points, the test error is lowest at degree 5 (0.309, against 0.310 at degree 3), and degree 12 costs only a little (0.373). More data shrinks the variance, so a more flexible model can be afforded before it starts following the noise. The training error still falls with every degree, and both errors now sit close to the noise level, 0.3.

  2. Choosing the penalty. At degree 12, with the 15 training points, choose the ridge penalty \lambda by 5-fold cross-validation over a grid from 10^{-6} to 10^{2}, and report the test error of the chosen model.
    Hint

    cv_rmse accepts lam; try 10.0**np.arange(-6, 2.01, 0.5), and refit on all 15 points with the winner.

    Solution

    Cross-validation picks \lambda = 0.3, and its test error is 0.387, almost exactly that of the best unregularised model (degree 3, 0.379). The cross-validation curve is U-shaped in \lambda, as the test error was in the degree: too small a penalty lets the polynomial overfit (a CV error of 14.2 at 10^{-6}), too large a penalty flattens it (0.82 at 10^{2}).

  3. Leakage. Simulate the cheat of Part 5. On each of 1,000 runs, draw 15 training points and a separate small test set of 15 points, pick the degree (0 to 9) with the lowest error on that test set, and report that error. Compare it with the same model's error on 5,000 fresh points.
    Hint

    Keep two lists per run: the error you would report, and the error on the fresh points. Compare their medians, and count how often the report is lower.

    Solution

    The reported error has a median of 0.339 (mean 0.353); the same models' true error, on fresh data, has a median of 0.357 (mean 0.422), and the report is optimistic in 64% of the runs. Choosing the best of ten scores on a small test set picks, in part, the degree that was lucky on those 15 points. Once a test set has been used to choose, it has become part of the training, and a fresh test set is needed to report honestly.

  4. Bias and variance with more data. Repeat the bias-variance measurement of Part 4 with n = 60, for degrees 0, 1, 3, 5 and 7. Which part of the error does more data reduce, and which degree is best now?
    Hint

    The widget of Part 4 has a slider for the points per training set. Watch the spread of the thin lines, and the gap between their average and the true curve, separately.

    Solution

    With 60 points the bias is unchanged, since it measures how far the average fit is from the truth: 0.50 at degree 0, 0.20 at degree 1. The variance falls sharply: at degree 5, from 2.58 to 0.012. So the expected test MSE is now 0.102 at both degree 3 and degree 5 (a tie: 0.1019 against 0.1016, and other batches of training sets can swap the two) and 0.112 at degree 7, all close to the noise floor of 0.09. More data does not make a model less biased; it makes it more stable, and so lets you afford more flexibility.

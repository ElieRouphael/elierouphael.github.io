Advanced MPC and Learning MPC

Model predictive control solves an optimisation problem at every sample. This course does not treat that optimiser as a black box: for every controller it asks what the decision variables, objective and constraints are, what makes the problem feasible at the next step, why the closed loop is stable, and which assumptions a real system would break. Part A teaches the concepts one at a time, each on the same small system, with the function that computes it read line by line. Part B assembles them into complete designs, from a collision decision to Learning MPC, which learns its terminal ingredients from its own successful executions, and to the connection with dynamic programming and reinforcement learning. Part C goes beyond the nominal case: disturbances, setpoints, a nonlinear plant, and the racing controller that finally drives laps.

Elie Rouphael · Assumes linear algebra, basic control (state-space models, stability) and Python

How to read a chapter. Every concept chapter has the same shape: a live demo under the title, the idea in plain words and then precisely, the function of the course package that computes it read line by line, the pitfalls, questions with folded answers, exercises whose solutions run, and the chapter on one page. Every number quoted on a page is printed by that chapter's script, code/<chapter>_numbers.py, and the browser demos use the same algorithms as the package.

How to study each controller. Always find four layers. The model: what are the state x, the input u and the dynamics f? The optimisation: what are the decision variables, the objective and the constraints? The guarantees: what gives feasibility, safety or stability? The limitations: which assumptions would fail on a real platform? The labs are built around these four questions, and they are a good structure for reviewing any controller design.

Part A · Concepts

One idea per chapter, each on the same constrained double integrator: what the idea says in plain words and precisely, a live demo, the function of the course package that computes it read line by line, exercises whose solutions run, and a script that reproduces every number on the page.

  1. A1 State-space models and the double integrator States, inputs and dynamics, the four model classes, the course's double integrator from Newton's law with its two discretisations, and why the plant and the prediction model are different things. You find Held accelerations of 1, 1, 0, −1, −1 take the cart from (−3, 0) to (3, 0) in five moves inside its box; the same moves at half size for twice as long leave the box at step 7. Euler's position lags the exact one by Tsv/2, a whole metre at a one-second sample and a 2 m/s velocity.
  2. A2 Prediction matrices and condensing The stacked prediction identity, the condensed quadratic program it produces (its Hessian is always positive definite), state, input, rate and terminal constraints as linear inequalities in the inputs, and the sparse form that keeps the states as variables. You find For N = 6 the condensed Hessian has eigenvalues from 0.51 to 290 and the unconstrained optimum from (−3, 0) opens with a push of 2.22, twice the input bound. The sparse and condensed forms of an N = 8 problem agree to 3 × 10⁻¹⁴ with 24 and 8 variables respectively, each in 13 solver iterations.
  3. A3 Convexity, quadratic programs and the KKT conditions Convex sets and functions, the problem classes, the Lagrangian, weak and strong duality, the four KKT conditions with a QP solved by hand, and multipliers as the price of a constraint. You find For the hand QP the KKT conditions give z* = (0.7, 0.3) with λ* = 1 and a dual gap of zero; the numerical derivative of the optimal cost with respect to the bound is −1.000, the multiplier exactly. The quadratic z'Pz/2 with P = ((2, α), (α, 1)) is convex exactly when |α| ≤ √2.
  4. A4 Solving a QP you can read The course's own interior-point QP solver read line by line: stacked inequalities, the residuals as the KKT conditions, the Newton system with the slacks eliminated, the fraction-to-the-boundary rule, the stopping test and the active-set polish; then an independent feasibility certificate, and a comparison with SciPy's SLSQP. You find The hand QP converges in 10 Newton steps with the complementarity shrinking by a factor of 10 per step, the centring parameter; a 20-variable MPC QP takes 13 steps and about a millisecond, against 16 for SLSQP in four times the time, and an infeasible problem is detected by a one-line linear program.
  5. A5 The finite-horizon problem The constrained finite-time optimal control problem, hard and soft constraints with an exact penalty, a terminal equality, one complete linear-quadratic instance with its active constraints and multipliers, a certified infeasible one, the nonlinear case on a unicycle, and the habits that go beyond "solver converged". You find From (−3, 0) with N = 8, rate bound 0.5 and x_N = 0 the cost is 22.225, four rate bounds are active and the first one is worth 9.85 per unit; dropping the terminal equality saves 0.0003. With N = 4 no plan exists and an LP certifies it. A comfort bound |v| ≤ 1 with multiplier 2.51 is reproduced by a soft penalty of at least 2.51.
  6. A6 Receding horizon: from a plan to feedback The receding-horizon law with its two-index notation, predicted against closed-loop trajectories, the four properties that must be kept apart, how feasibility is lost, and the horizon-one pathology with its Riccati repair. You find From (−3.6, 2.4) with N = 5 all three designs drive the cart home in 9 moves at a closed-loop cost of 17.64, but only the two with the Riccati terminal cost have a value that never rises. With the weaker motor and N = 1, the start (−4, 2) is feasible for four steps and then has no admissible plan at all; the terminal-set design refuses it at once.
  7. A7 Lyapunov stability and LQR Schur stability, the discrete Lyapunov condition and equation, the infinite-horizon LQR, the Riccati equation and the recursion that converges to it, and the identity that makes the LQR value a terminal cost. You find For the canonical weights the LQR gain is K = (0.7396, 1.2604) with closed-loop eigenvalues 0.185 ± 0.274i, spectral radius 0.331, and the identity Acl⊤PAcl − P = −(Q + K⊤RK) holds to 8 × 10−16: along the loop the value falls from 17.49 to 3.35 to 0.135, by exactly the stage cost each step. The Riccati recursion from any terminal weight reaches P to 10−12 in thirteen or fourteen steps, shrinking by about ρ² = 0.109 per step.
  8. A8 Polytopes and set operations H- and V-representations, membership and redundancy, intersections, affine images, linear preimages, Minkowski sums, projection as "there exists", and why exact sets get expensive. You find A five-vertex polygon becomes five half-spaces and back; stacking the rows of a box and a diamond gives their intersection with 6 of 8 rows kept; the preimage of a box under A is a parallelogram with the same right-hand sides; 16 vertex sums reduce to an 8-vertex Minkowski sum. A naive redundancy test that compares a row with all the others drops both copies of a duplicated row and enlarges the set.
  9. A9 Pre, Post and controllable sets The forward reachable set, the controlled predecessor as a projection and as a Minkowski sum, Fourier–Motzkin elimination for a scalar input, a witness input, and the N-step controllable sets with their minimum-step map. You find From the small target box, six backward steps reach a set of area 38.09 of the box's 48 with 4, 6, 8, 8, 6, 10, 10 vertices along the way, nested because the target is control invariant; 4088 of 19 481 grid states are inside the box but cannot reach the target in six steps. Fourier–Motzkin turns 4 target rows and 2 input bounds into 9 rows, of which 6 survive.
  10. A10 Positive and control invariance Two definitions that differ by a quantifier, the admissible region of a fixed law, the two fixed-point iterations that compute the maximal sets, why one is five times the other, and what membership certifies. You find Under the LQR law the maximal positively invariant set is a parallelogram of area 7.31, found in two iterations inside the 12.66 where the law respects the input bound; the maximal control-invariant set has ten vertices and area 39.25 of the box's 48, found in four, 5.4 times larger. Two of four states that satisfy the box now are doomed, and from (3, 0.8) the least input that stays safe is −0.067.
  11. A11 Terminal ingredients and the stability theorem The three terminal conditions, the shifted sequence, the Lyapunov argument with its upper bound, the zero terminal constraint as the conservative special case, the LQR ingredients checked at the vertices, the horizon as a lever, and a closed loop with its residual at every step. You find With the weaker motor and N = 5, the invariant terminal set certifies a region of area 20.1 against 15.1 for xN = 0 and 30.3 for the largest possible; from (−3.5, −0.5) the zero design is infeasible at once while the set design drives the cart home in 12 steps with a Lyapunov residual of at most 10−14. Inside the terminal set the MPC is the LQR to 10−14.
  12. A12 Sampled and convex safe sets What a successful run certifies, the sampled safe set and why it is control invariant, its convex hull with barycentric coordinates, the invariance proof that uses linearity once and convexity twice, and the counter-example that shows what breaks for a nonlinear model. You find Three runs from (−3.5, 2.5) store 43 safe states whose hull has area 10.25 of the box's 48; the slowest run, which overshoots to p = 2, sets the whole hull on its own. A probe at (−1, 1.8) is 0.05, 0.83 and 0.12 of three stored states, its mixed input −1 is admissible, and its successor equals the same mixture of the stored successors to 10⁻¹⁵.
  13. A13 The learned terminal value and the LMPC problem Realised costs-to-go, the sampled value and its convex extension by a barycentric linear program, the decrease it satisfies, the Learning MPC problem, its recursive feasibility, and the classical and learned terminal ingredients side by side. You find On the scalar integrator the envelope at x = 2.5 is 9.75, half of 5.2 and half of 14.3, and the mixed input −1 lowers it by 6.6 against a stage cost of 6.35. On the cart, the first LMPC solve with N = 3 and the slow seed stored applies −0.84 where the seed applied 0, ends its plan on a stored state eleven steps down the seed, and the next problem is feasible with a value decrease of 3.0199 against a stage cost of 3.0191.
  14. A14 Bellman: dynamic programming, value and policy iteration The principle of optimality and its proof, the backward recursion and its domains as controllable sets, the Riccati recursion as its linear-quadratic case, the Bellman operator's contraction, value and policy iteration, and the constrained double integrator solved exactly on a lattice. You find Dynamic programming reproduces the brute-force optimum of the integer chain with 525 stage-cost evaluations instead of 328 125. On the lattice the undiscounted value iteration stops after 10 sweeps with 655 viable states, exactly the lattice points of C∞; policy iteration finds the optimal policy in 4 improvement steps out of about 10150 possible policies; inside O∞ the lattice value exceeds xTPx by a median 0.6 percent.
  15. A15 Disjunctive constraints and Big-M Why "this or that" is nonconvex, one binary variable, the Big-M encoding, the smallest valid M from a position envelope, what a relaxed binary allows, and when enumerating the branches beats a mixed-integer solver. You find The car's centre must stay below 93.75 m or above 106.25 m, a 12.5 m gap; for positions within 300 m the smallest valid constant is M = 206.25, 24 times less than the round 5000 the lab uses, and at a half-relaxed binary any M lets the car sit in the middle of the crossing. From the nominal state only the yield branch is feasible, at cost 2582.56; with more grip both are, and a thousandfold input weight flips the choice.

Part B · Labs

The concepts assembled into complete designs: a collision-avoidance decision, Learning MPC on a repeated task, minimum-time racing, local models learned from laps, the dynamic-programming view that connects MPC to reinforcement learning, and one design checked end to end.

  1. B1 Vehicle–bike collision avoidance A yield-or-pass decision as two convex QPs, friction-limited braking, closed-form reachability and the critical friction, feasibility maps over initial conditions, what the weights change, why yield is not stop, and the occupancy window. You find From the nominal state only yielding is feasible; the critical friction coefficient is 0.106, and halving the friction leaves 36 of 660 initial states with no safe strategy instead of 7. A full stop is feasible from 75 states where a geometric yield is feasible from 268.
  2. B2 Learning MPC on a repeated task A short-horizon MPC produces a poor first run; Learning MPC learns the terminal ingredients from it and improves, iteration after iteration: the seed, the database, the first learned move, the iteration table, what is guaranteed against observed, hull growth against value learning, the window, and the plateau that a short horizon cannot leave. You find From a seed 68.8 percent above the optimum 16.636215, Learning MPC with N = 4 reaches 16.636970, 16.636224 and 16.636215 in three iterations and never increases; with N = 2 and N = 3 it stalls at 0.30 and 0.13 percent above, and a brute-force search confirms the plateau. The hull never changes after the seed; the stored values do all the learning.
  3. B3 Frenet coordinates and minimum-time racing Curvilinear coordinates, the factor 1 − Ke_y and its singularity, the progress rate, space as the independent variable, the reduced problem in squared speed, the exact segment time, the forward and backward envelopes, the closed-lap planner, and the friction-circle diagnostic that shows what the reduction leaves out. You find The minimum-time lap of the 252 m track takes 17.09 s, with the speed set by a 7.7 m-radius corner taken at 8.7 m/s; dropping the friction coefficient from 1.0 to 0.6 costs 3.05 s, and the decoupled planner exceeds the friction circle on 66 of 240 segments.
  4. B4 Data-driven local models for racing The dynamic bicycle model with Pacejka axles and the full raceline problem it defines, kernel-weighted local regression of its velocities from recorded laps, exact Frenet rows, and a time-varying prediction model validated on a lap it never saw, one step and fourteen steps ahead. You find On the held-out lap the local model's one-step error is 1.7 to 2.9 times smaller than a global affine fit's, and over a 14-step horizon its RMSE is about half (0.021 against 0.041); over 30 steps it loses, and a query far from the data is flagged on every lap.
  5. B5 MPC, Learning MPC and reinforcement learning MPC as truncated dynamic programming, what a terminal cost buys, three terminal designs run from every feasible lattice state, Learning MPC as a learned upper bound of the value, tabular Q-learning against the model-based solution, and what the contraction no longer covers once a network replaces the table. You find With LQR terminal ingredients every one of 313 to 655 feasible starts converges at every horizon, while the design without them loses feasibility from 702 of 703 starts at N = 1. Learning MPC from a first run 92.6 percent above optimal reaches the optimum in one iteration with N = 3 and stalls 11.2 percent above it with N = 2. Q-learning finds the optimal policy after 40 000 transitions and 972 crashes where value iteration needed 6 sweeps and none.
  6. B6 Capstone: one design, end to end One MPC design followed from the terminal ingredients to a grid certificate of every guarantee, the same controller stripped of them, Learning MPC from a poor first run, the derivations the proofs rest on with hints, and the engineering questions outside the theorems. You find With LQR terminal ingredients, all 177 feasible starts of a 221-point grid stay feasible and converge, with a decrease residual at round-off and feasibility certified by an independent linear program; without them, N = 1 loses feasibility from 100 starts and N = 2 costs up to 2.15 times the optimum. Learning MPC from a first run 69 percent above optimal reaches the optimum with N = 4 and stalls 0.30 percent above it with N = 2.

Part C · Beyond the nominal case

What the concept chapters assumed away, one chapter each: a bounded disturbance and the tube that absorbs it, a setpoint and the disturbance model that removes the steady-state offset, a nonlinear plant handled by linearising along the plan, and the racing controller that closes the loop with everything the labs built.

  1. C1 Tube MPC for a bounded disturbance A bounded disturbance, why the nominal proofs fail, the error dynamics under the tube feedback, the minimal robust positively invariant set from Minkowski sums, the tightened constraints, the tube MPC law with its theorem, and the experiment against the nominal controller. You find With |w| ≤ 0.1 the error set has area 0.177, the box shrinks to 3.77 × 2.74 and the nominal input to 0.69; the tube controller's region loses a quarter of its area, and in forty disturbed steps from (−3, 2) it never leaves the box while the nominal controller of A11 fails from 19 of 531 boundary starts.
  2. C2 Reference tracking and offset-free MPC Steady-state targets, the tracking problem in deviation variables with its shifted terminal set, the offset a constant disturbance leaves, the augmented model and its observer, offset-free MPC and why its disturbance model must be detectable. You find On a slope that pushes the cart with −0.2, plain tracking MPC settles 0.27 short of every setpoint; with an integrating disturbance state and an observer whose error eigenvalues are 0.5 to 0.6 the estimate reaches −0.2 in thirty steps and the offset vanishes, within 0.01 in 17 steps from rest and 6 after a setpoint change. An output-disturbance model fails the detectability test on this cart and the loop diverges.
  3. C3 Nonlinear MPC by successive linearisation The unicycle's finite-horizon problem solved by linearising along the current plan and solving a QP in the step, repeated to convergence: the Jacobians, time-varying prediction matrices, the linearised terminal pose with its defect, linear convergence against SLSQP, the elastic mode that keeps every QP feasible, and the real-time iteration that solves one QP per sample in closed loop. You find From chapter A5's guess, fifteen linearise-and-solve steps reach cost 0.316697 with the pose met to 5·10⁻¹³, the terminal residual shrinking by a factor of about six per step; SLSQP needs 27 iterations and three times the time for the same inputs to 7·10⁻⁶. In closed loop, one QP per sample recovers exactly from a push at sample 10 and ends 0.14 off from a push at sample 25, which five remaining samples cannot undo.
  4. C4 Learning MPC racing: closing the loop The racing controller assembled from labs B3 and B4 and chapters A12 and A13, lap after lap on the RC-car track: a local time-varying model refitted along the horizon, a local convex safe set with costs-to-go in samples to the finish line, one QP per sample, and the lap time falling from 30.4 to 7.9 seconds. You find From seed laps of 30.4 s the learned controller drives 21.9 s on its first lap and 8.3 s by lap 18, faster every lap, inside the lane and the input bounds, with no infeasible QP. On lap 19 it leaves the track: the convexification that chapter A12 proves for a linear plant is only local for the car, and the fallback rule of lab B4 brings it back; lap 20 is the fastest of all at 7.9 s.

Running it yourself

Every chapter page runs its demos in the browser and quotes numbers computed in Python. To reproduce them, download the course: the advanced_mpc_lmpc package with its tests, the lab notebooks, and in code/ one script per chapter that prints every number quoted on that chapter's page. Then, from the unzipped folder:

pip install -r requirements.txt
python -m pytest                      # the package's tests
python code/a07_numbers.py            # every number on chapter A7's page
jupyter lab                           # the lab notebooks, in notebooks/

No commercial solver is needed: the package's quadratic programs are solved by its own interior-point method, a hundred lines of NumPy that chapter A4 reads line by line. Each script runs in seconds; the lab notebooks in under a minute each.

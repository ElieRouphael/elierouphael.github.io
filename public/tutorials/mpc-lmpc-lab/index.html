Advanced MPC and Learning MPC

Model predictive control solves an optimization problem at every sample. This course does not treat that optimizer as a black box: for every controller it asks what the decision variables, objective and constraints are, what makes the problem feasible at the next step, why the closed loop is stable, and which assumptions a real system would break. It goes from convex optimization and invariant sets to MPC stability, then to Learning MPC, which learns its terminal ingredients from its own successful executions, and to the connection with dynamic programming and reinforcement learning.

Elie Rouphael · Assumes linear algebra, basic control (state-space models, stability) and Python

Chapters

Chapters 00–03 build the theory: optimization, constraint geometry, and why a finite-horizon controller can be recursively feasible and stable. Chapters 04–07 apply it to collision avoidance, Learning MPC and racing. Chapter 08 places everything in the dynamic-programming picture, and chapter 09 runs one complete design end to end.

  1. 00 Roadmap and Mathematical Primer States, inputs and discrete-time models, Schur stability, Lyapunov functions, the DARE, and a first constrained MPC on the double integrator. You find The LQR terminal ingredients check out to machine precision (DARE identity residual 2.3e-15), and a constrained MPC brings the double integrator to the origin in 11 moves.
  2. 01 Convex Optimization and Constrained Finite-Time Optimal Control Convex sets and functions, KKT conditions and duality, then a constrained finite-time optimal-control problem condensed into a QP. You find The KKT conditions hold to machine precision (primal-dual gap 1e-15), and the condensed CFTOC QP is solved with a largest constraint violation of 2.6e-13.
  3. 02 Polytopes, Reachability, and Invariant Sets H- and V-polytopes, predecessor and reachable sets, and the fixed-point algorithms for positively invariant and control-invariant sets. You find The maximal control-invariant set is found in 4 fixed-point iterations (10 vertices) and strictly contains the invariant set of the LQR closed loop (4 vertices).
  4. 03 Model Predictive Control: Receding Horizon, Feasibility, and Stability The receding-horizon law, recursive feasibility by the shifted sequence, the Lyapunov argument with its three conditions, and LQR terminal ingredients. You find With N = 5, an invariant terminal set gives a feasible region of area 34.5, against 16.1 with the terminal constraint x_N = 0 and 37.3 for the largest possible.
  5. 04 Vehicle–Bike Collision Avoidance as a Constrained Finite-Time Optimal-Control Problem A disjunctive yield-or-pass decision as two convex QPs or one mixed-integer problem, friction-limited braking, and feasibility maps over initial conditions. You find From the nominal state only yielding is feasible; the critical friction coefficient is about 0.106, and halving the friction leaves 36 of 660 initial states with no safe strategy instead of 8.
  6. 05 Learning Model Predictive Control on a Repeated Double-Integrator Task Sampled and convex safe sets, a terminal cost learned from realized costs-to-go, and the guarantees of Learning MPC, iteration after iteration. You find Starting from a seed trajectory 20.3 above the long-horizon optimum, Learning MPC closes the gap to 5e-5 in 9 iterations, and its cost never increases.
  7. 06 Frenet Coordinates and Minimum-Time Racing Curvilinear coordinates, a dynamic bicycle model in space rather than time, and a minimum-time speed planner with friction and acceleration limits. You find The minimum-time lap of the 252 m track takes 17.09 s, with speed set by a 7.7 m-radius corner; dropping the friction coefficient from 1.0 to 0.6 costs 3.05 s.
  8. 07 Data-Driven Local Models for Autonomous Racing Kernel-weighted local regression of the vehicle dynamics from recorded laps, structured affine models and time-varying predictions for MPC. You find Over a 14-step horizon, the local model's prediction error is about half the global model's (RMSE 0.021 vs 0.041), and a query far from the data is flagged on every lap.
  9. 08 From Dynamic Programming to Reinforcement Learning Bellman's principle, the Riccati recursion, contraction and value and policy iteration, where MPC and LMPC fit, and Q-learning on to DQN. You find Policy iteration finds the exact optimal policy on a 215-state lattice in 4 improvement steps, out of about 10^150 possible policies.
  10. 09 Capstone: Connect the Ideas One MPC design followed end to end with numerical checks of every guarantee, Learning MPC from a poor first lap, and derivations with worked answers. You find With LQR terminal ingredients, all 177 feasible starts of a 221-point grid stay feasible and converge; Learning MPC starting from a first lap 69% above optimal reaches the optimum.

Running it yourself

Every chapter page shows the notebook with its outputs. To run and change the code, download the course (the notebooks, the small advanced_mpc_lmpc package they use, its tests and the requirements), then from its folder run:

pip install -r requirements.txt
python -m pytest            # optional: the package's tests
jupyter lab                 # then open notebooks/00_roadmap_and_math_primer.ipynb

No commercial solver is needed: the package uses SciPy, chosen for readability rather than speed. Each notebook runs in under a minute on a laptop.

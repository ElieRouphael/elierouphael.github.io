Big idea. Classical feedback tells us how to react to the current state. MPC adds two ingredients that become essential for complex systems: prediction and explicit constraint handling.

Part 1

The language of dynamical systems

State, input, dynamics, output. A deterministic discrete-time system is written

x_{k+1} = f_k(x_k, u_k), \qquad x_k \in \mathbb R^n,\quad u_k \in \mathbb R^m .

The state x_k is a sufficient internal description: once it and the future inputs are known, the model can predict every future state. An output equation y_k = h_k(x_k, u_k) may be added, but MPC is most naturally written in state space.

Four model classes recur in the course. LTI (linear time-invariant): x_{k+1} = A x_k + B u_k. Affine: x_{k+1} = A x_k + B u_k + c. LTV (linear time-varying): x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + B_k u_k + c_k. Nonlinear: x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k). The racing chapters move between them deliberately: the simulated vehicle is nonlinear, while the optimiser uses a locally identified or linearised prediction model.

Important. The plant (the system being controlled, or its simulation) and the prediction model (what the optimiser uses) do not have to coincide. MPC optimises with a model; the real system evolves according to physics. Model mismatch is a central practical issue, and the gap between the two is a recurring theme of chapters 07 and 09.

The running example: a sampled double integrator. Let p be position and v velocity. With a constant acceleration a over one sample of duration T_s,

p_{k+1} = p_k + T_s v_k + \tfrac{1}{2} T_s^2 a_k, \qquad v_{k+1} = v_k + T_s a_k .

So with the state x_k = (p_k, v_k) and the input u_k = a_k,

A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & T_s \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} T_s^2 / 2 \\ T_s \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{and with } T_s = 1:\quad A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},\ B = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}.

This tiny system already contains the main difficulties of MPC: the input affects velocity immediately but position only indirectly, and physical limits naturally constrain both states and inputs. The widget rolls it out from x_0 = (-5, 0) under a few input sequences.

Notation and conventions. x^\top y is the inner product and \lVert x \rVert_2 = \sqrt{x^\top x} the Euclidean norm. P = P^\top \succ 0 means symmetric positive definite, P \succeq 0 positive semidefinite; the quadratic form V(x) = x^\top P x is strictly positive for every x \ne 0 exactly when P \succ 0. \lambda_i(A) are the eigenvalues of A and \rho(A) = \max_i |\lambda_i(A)| its spectral radius. A polyhedral state constraint is written \mathcal X = \{x : H_x x \le h_x\} and an input set \mathcal U = \{u : H_u u \le h_u\}, with inequalities between vectors read componentwise.

Feedback sign convention. Throughout the course, linear feedback is written u = -Kx, hence the closed-loop matrix is A_{\mathrm{cl}} = A - BK. If another text writes u = Fx, then F = -K and the same loop appears as A + BF. Check the convention before copying a stability formula; exercise 3 shows what the wrong sign does.

What is model predictive control?

In plain words: a controller that, at every sampling instant, uses a model to predict what a sequence of future inputs would do, picks the sequence that minimises a cost while respecting limits on states and inputs, applies only its first move, and then starts over from the newly measured state.

More precisely: the feedback law \kappa_N(x) = u_0^*(x), where u_0^*(x) is the first element of the minimiser of a constrained finite-horizon optimal control problem (Part 3) solved at the current state x. For linear dynamics, quadratic cost and polyhedral constraints that problem is a convex quadratic program; with a suitable terminal cost and terminal set, the closed loop is recursively feasible and asymptotically stable (Part 7).

Part 2

Prediction: turning dynamics into algebra

MPC needs to know how an input sequence affects future states. For the LTI model the first steps are

\begin{aligned} x_1 &= A x_0 + B u_0, \\ x_2 &= A^2 x_0 + AB u_0 + B u_1, \\ x_3 &= A^3 x_0 + A^2 B u_0 + AB u_1 + B u_2 . \end{aligned}

Stack the predicted states and the inputs into X = (x_1, \dots, x_N) and U = (u_0, \dots, u_{N-1}). Then

\boxed{X = \mathcal S_x x_0 + \mathcal S_u U}, \qquad \mathcal S_x = \begin{pmatrix} A \\ A^2 \\ \vdots \\ A^N \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathcal S_u = \begin{pmatrix} B & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ AB & B & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ A^{N-1} B & A^{N-2} B & \cdots & B \end{pmatrix}.

\mathcal S_u is lower block-triangular with constant diagonals (block-Toeplitz): causality, since u_j cannot affect x_i for j \ge i. This one identity is the backbone of condensed linear MPC.

Checkpoint: what is really being optimised? In the condensed formulation the future states are eliminated with X = \mathcal S_x x_0 + \mathcal S_u U, and the only optimisation variable is U. In the sparse formulation both X and U remain variables and the dynamics are imposed as equality constraints. Neither changes the control problem; they change its numerical representation. Sparse formulations scale better for long horizons and structured solvers; condensed QPs are convenient for small and medium linear MPC problems, and are what this course's package uses.

Part 3

Optimisation and finite-horizon optimal control

Anatomy of a constrained problem. A generic optimisation problem is

\min_z\ f(z) \quad \text{s.t.}\quad g_i(z) \le 0,\ i = 1, \dots, m, \qquad h_j(z) = 0,\ j = 1, \dots, p .

z is the decision variable; in optimal control it may hold a whole input trajectory, a state trajectory, logical variables, or all of them. If f and every g_i are convex and every h_j is affine, the problem is convex: any local optimum is global [1]. A quadratic program (QP) is the case

\min_z\ \tfrac{1}{2} z^\top H z + q^\top z \quad \text{s.t.}\quad G z \le b, \quad E z = d,

convex when H \succeq 0. Convexity is not elegance for its own sake: it decides what can be solved reliably online, at every sampling instant, with a bound on the time it takes.

From a quadratic cost to a QP. Consider the horizon-N linear-quadratic cost

J_N = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \left( x_k^\top Q x_k + u_k^\top R u_k \right) + x_N^\top P x_N, \qquad Q \succeq 0,\ R \succ 0,\ P \succeq 0 .

Define \bar Q = \mathrm{blkdiag}(Q, \dots, Q, P) (one block per predicted state, P for the last) and \bar R = I_N \otimes R. Substituting X = \mathcal S_x x_0 + \mathcal S_u U,

J_N(U; x_0) = (\mathcal S_x x_0 + \mathcal S_u U)^\top \bar Q\, (\mathcal S_x x_0 + \mathcal S_u U) + U^\top \bar R U + x_0^\top Q x_0 = \tfrac12 U^\top H U + f(x_0)^\top U + c(x_0),
\boxed{H = 2 \left( \mathcal S_u^\top \bar Q \mathcal S_u + \bar R \right)}, \qquad \boxed{f(x_0) = 2\, \mathcal S_u^\top \bar Q \mathcal S_x x_0}, \qquad c(x_0) = x_0^\top \left( \mathcal S_x^\top \bar Q \mathcal S_x + Q \right) x_0 .

The constant c(x_0) does not affect the minimiser, so solvers drop it; the notebook does too, which is why its reported objective values are negative. Keep it in mind when comparing values at different states, as Part 7 does: the value function V_N^*(x_0) is the full J_N, constant included.

Is H positive definite? Yes, always, not just typically: for any U \ne 0, U^\top H U = 2\,(\mathcal S_u U)^\top \bar Q (\mathcal S_u U) + 2\, U^\top \bar R U \ge 2\, U^\top \bar R U > 0, because \bar Q \succeq 0 and R \succ 0. So the unconstrained minimiser U^* = -H^{-1} f(x_0) is unique. For the double integrator with Q = \mathrm{diag}(1, 0.2), R = 0.1, P = Q and N = 6, the eigenvalues of H are

0.3137,\ 0.3697,\ 0.5483,\ 1.3056,\ 7.9579,\ 289.60, \qquad \text{condition number } \approx 923,

and from x_0 = (-5, 0) the unconstrained optimum is

U^* = (4.20,\ -3.23,\ -1.04,\ -0.01,\ 0.07,\ 0.02),

a first push of 4.2 and a hard brake. The hero's controller may only push with |u| \le 1, four times less, which is why constraints are not an afterthought.

How constraints become linear inequalities. If each predicted state must satisfy H_x x_k \le h_x, stacking gives \bar H_x X \le \bar h_x, and substituting the prediction identity,

\boxed{\bar H_x \mathcal S_u\, U \le \bar h_x - \bar H_x \mathcal S_x x_0}, \qquad \text{while input constraints are already linear in } U:\ \bar H_u U \le \bar h_u .

Hence the most useful fact of linear MPC: linear dynamics + quadratic objective + polyhedral constraints = convex QP. Exercise 2 works one such constraint out by hand.

Hard versus soft. A hard constraint H_x x_k \le h_x must always hold. If a temporary violation is physically tolerable, add a slack \epsilon_k \ge 0, write H_x x_k \le h_x + \epsilon_k, and penalise it, for example J \leftarrow J + \rho \sum_k \lVert \epsilon_k \rVert_1. Softening protects against infeasibility; it does not make a safety-critical constraint safe to violate. The modelling decision must reflect physics.

Constrained finite-time optimal control (CFTOC). For a general system, the horizon-N problem is

\begin{aligned} V_N^*(x_0) = \min_{u_0, \dots, u_{N-1}}\ & V_f(x_N) + \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \ell(x_k, u_k) \\ \text{s.t. } & x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k),\quad x_k \in \mathcal X,\quad u_k \in \mathcal U,\quad x_N \in \mathcal X_f,\quad x_0 = x(0). \end{aligned}

\ell(x, u) is the stage cost: what is expensive now. V_f(x_N) is the terminal cost: the estimated price of the future beyond the horizon. \mathcal X_f is the terminal set: where the prediction must end so that the future can still be handled safely. The solution is an entire open-loop sequence U^*(x_0) = (u_0^*, \dots, u_{N-1}^*). CFTOC by itself is an open-loop planning problem; MPC turns it into feedback by solving it again and again [2].

Part 4

Receding horizon control

MPC is a feedback law generated by optimisation. At time t: measure or estimate the state x(t); solve the horizon-N problem; obtain u_{0|t}^*, \dots, u_{N-1|t}^*; apply only

\boxed{u(t) = u_{0|t}^*}\,;

discard the unused tail, move to t + 1, and solve again. This is the receding horizon principle, and the resulting law \kappa_N(x) = u_0^*(x) is defined implicitly by the optimiser, which is therefore part of the controller rather than an offline trajectory generator. Why re-optimise? Because the next measured state is rarely exactly the predicted one: re-optimisation absorbs disturbances, estimation errors, model mismatch and changing references.

The demo at the top is the notebook's run. From (-7, 0) with N = 8, the loop takes 11 moves to bring the norm of the state under 10^{-3}; the browser's solution ends at (0.0002, -0.0001) and the notebook's at (0.0003, -0.0001), the difference being the notebook's SLSQP solver tolerance. The input saturates at its bound of 1 on 4 of the 11 moves and the velocity peaks at 2.49, under its bound of 3. Drag the plan slider: the dashed orange curve is the 8-step prediction made at that step, and the solid curve is what was actually applied, one first move at a time. Here they almost coincide (the plan made at step 0 is off by at most 0.012), and that is instructive: the model is exact and nothing disturbs the cart, so each re-solve finds the previous plan still optimal. On a real system the next measured state differs from the predicted one, and re-optimising from it is what receding horizon control is for.

Four notions that must not be conflated.

  1. Feasibility. For a state x, the finite-horizon problem is feasible if at least one admissible input sequence exists. The N-step feasible set is \mathcal X_N = \{x : \exists U \text{ admissible}\}, the states from which some sequence satisfies all the horizon constraints.
  2. Recursive feasibility. The law is recursively feasible if x(t) \in \mathcal X_N \Rightarrow x(t+1) \in \mathcal X_N under the applied MPC input. A controller can be feasible now and fail to remain feasible later; recursive feasibility is the stronger closed-loop property. The hero shows the failure: choose the terminal set with N = 3 and the problem is infeasible from (-7, 0) at the first step, since no three moves reach the terminal set.
  3. Constraint satisfaction. If the model is exact and the applied input is the first element of a feasible sequence, the current step's hard constraints hold. Under uncertainty this needs robust, tube or chance-constrained arguments; nominal MPC alone is not a robust guarantee.
  4. Stability. Feasibility says a legal trajectory exists; stability speaks about the asymptotic behaviour of the closed loop. A feasible MPC controller need not stabilise the desired equilibrium [3].

A common pitfall. "The optimiser solved successfully" is not a stability proof. The demo's default setting converges, and the readout shows that its optimal value nevertheless fails to decrease by the stage cost on some steps: the convergence is a property of this example, not a theorem. Part 7 says what makes it one.

Part 5

Stability and LQR

Continuous and discrete time test differently. For \dot x = Ax, asymptotic stability requires A to be Hurwitz: \operatorname{Re} \lambda_i(A) < 0. For x_{k+1} = A x_k it requires A to be Schur:

\boxed{\rho(A) < 1}.

The discrete-time Lyapunov condition. With V(x) = x^\top P x and P \succ 0, one step changes V by \Delta V = V(x_{k+1}) - V(x_k) = x_k^\top (A^\top P A - P)\, x_k. If some P \succ 0 makes A^\top P A - P \prec 0, the origin is asymptotically stable [6], and in fact exponentially: \lVert x_k \rVert \le c\, \alpha^k \lVert x_0 \rVert for some c > 0 and 0 < \alpha < 1.

Infinite-horizon LQR and the DARE. For x_{k+1} = A x_k + B u_k and the cost J_\infty = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (x_k^\top Q x_k + u_k^\top R u_k) with Q \succeq 0, R \succ 0, and under the usual stabilisability and detectability assumptions, the optimal stationary feedback is u = -Kx with

K = (R + B^\top P B)^{-1} B^\top P A, \qquad \boxed{P = A^\top P A - A^\top P B (R + B^\top P B)^{-1} B^\top P A + Q},

the second being the discrete algebraic Riccati equation (DARE). The widget solves it by iterating it from P = Q until it stops changing, which is the finite-horizon value function's limit as the horizon grows. The closed-loop matrix is A_{\mathrm{cl}} = A - BK, and the fundamental identity is

\boxed{A_{\mathrm{cl}}^\top P A_{\mathrm{cl}} - P = -(Q + K^\top R K)}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad V(x^+) - V(x) = -\ell(x, -Kx) < 0 \quad \text{for } x \ne 0 .

The LQR value decreases, at every step, by exactly the stage cost paid. This identity is why the LQR value function is the natural terminal cost for linear-quadratic MPC (Part 7).

Worked example. For the double integrator with Q = \mathrm{diag}(1, 0.2) and R = 0.1,

K = (0.8399,\ 1.3494), \qquad P = \begin{pmatrix} 1.6066 & 0.3873 \\ 0.3873 & 0.5286 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda(A_{\mathrm{cl}}) = 0.1153 \pm 0.2393\,i, \qquad \rho(A_{\mathrm{cl}}) = 0.2656 .

The identity holds to 2 \times 10^{-15}, machine precision. Starting the loop at (-5, 1.5), V falls from 35.5 to 9.6 to 0.24 to 0.02: not by a fixed factor each step, since the eigenvalues are complex and the loop spirals, but never upward. The sliders change the weights; watch the eigenvalues and the first move.

Part 6

Reachability and invariant sets

Predecessors: from where can I enter this set in one step? For x^+ = Ax + Bu and a target set \mathcal S, the one-step predecessor is

\boxed{\mathrm{Pre}(\mathcal S) = \{x : \exists u \in \mathcal U \text{ such that } Ax + Bu \in \mathcal S\}}.

Repeated predecessors \mathrm{Pre}^2(\mathcal S), \mathrm{Pre}^3(\mathcal S), \dots answer finite-horizon reachability questions: from which initial states can I reach a terminal or safe set within N steps without violating constraints? That is closely related to the MPC feasible set \mathcal X_N, and chapter 02 computes both.

Positive invariance versus control invariance. The definitions look alike; their quantifiers differ. For autonomous dynamics x^+ = F(x), a set \mathcal O is positively invariant if

\boxed{x \in \mathcal O \ \Rightarrow\ F(x) \in \mathcal O}.

When a feedback is fixed, say u = -Kx, invariance is analysed under x^+ = (A - BK)x. For controlled dynamics, a set \mathcal C is control invariant if

\boxed{\forall x \in \mathcal C,\ \exists u \in \mathcal U:\ Ax + Bu \in \mathcal C}.

Positive invariance: the future is safe under the fixed closed loop. Control invariance: at least one admissible action can keep the state safe. The "for all x, there exists u" is one of the most important quantifier distinctions in constrained control [5].

A terminal invariant set for constrained LQR. With state constraints x \in \mathcal X and the LQR input required to be admissible, -Kx \in \mathcal U, define

\mathcal X_{\mathrm{cl}} = \{x : x \in \mathcal X,\ -Kx \in \mathcal U\}.

The maximal positively invariant subset of \mathcal X_{\mathrm{cl}} under x^+ = (A - BK)x is the natural terminal set \mathcal X_f. Inside it, the LQR controller respects the constraints, the next state stays inside, and the LQR value decreases: exactly the three ingredients of Part 7. The widget computes it by the fixed-point iteration \Omega_{i+1} = \mathcal X_{\mathrm{cl}} \cap \mathrm{Pre}(\Omega_i), starting from \Omega_0 = \mathcal X_{\mathrm{cl}}, where for the autonomous loop \mathrm{Pre}(\Omega) = \{x : A_{\mathrm{cl}} x \in \Omega\} is just \{x : H A_{\mathrm{cl}} x \le h\}; it stops when the set stops changing. With the notebook's box |p| \le 8, |v| \le 3 and |u| \le 1, it stops after 2 iterations with a 4-vertex set.

Why does \mathcal X_{\mathrm{cl}} lose its top and bottom corners? At the corner (-8, 3) the LQR move would be -Kx = 0.8399 \cdot 8 - 1.3494 \cdot 3 = 2.67, far over the bound of 1. And why is \mathcal X_f smaller still? Because from the sloped edge of \mathcal X_{\mathrm{cl}}, one LQR step lands outside \mathcal X_{\mathrm{cl}}: the input is admissible now but not next time. Invariance is a statement about every future step, and the fixed point enforces it.

Part 7

Why terminal ingredients matter in MPC

The standard mechanism. Consider the MPC problem with value V_N^*(x) = \min_U \big[ \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \ell(x_k, u_k) + V_f(x_N) \big] and the terminal constraint x_N \in \mathcal X_f. Assume a local terminal controller \kappa_f such that for every x \in \mathcal X_f:

  1. invariance: f(x, \kappa_f(x)) \in \mathcal X_f;
  2. constraint admissibility: x \in \mathcal X and \kappa_f(x) \in \mathcal U;
  3. terminal Lyapunov decrease:
    \boxed{V_f\big(f(x, \kappa_f(x))\big) - V_f(x) \le -\ell(x, \kappa_f(x))}.

These three assumptions are the conceptual heart of stabilising MPC [4].

Recursive feasibility: the shifted sequence. Suppose at time t the optimal sequence is U_t^* = (u_{0|t}^*, \dots, u_{N-1|t}^*) with predicted terminal state x_{N|t}^* \in \mathcal X_f. MPC applies u_{0|t}^*. At t + 1 consider the candidate

\widetilde U_{t+1} = \big( u_{1|t}^*, \dots, u_{N-1|t}^*,\ \kappa_f(x_{N|t}^*) \big).

It is feasible in the nominal case: the shifted predicted states were already feasible; x_{N|t}^* \in \mathcal X_f; the terminal controller respects the constraints there; and invariance keeps the new final state in \mathcal X_f. Hence

\boxed{\text{feasible at } t \ \Rightarrow\ \text{feasible at } t + 1}

under the theorem's assumptions. This is a proof, not a numerical observation.

Stability: the optimal value as a Lyapunov function. The same candidate gives a cost comparison. The optimiser at t + 1 can only do better than a feasible candidate, so

V_N^*(x_{t+1}) \le V_N^*(x_t) - \ell(x_t, u_t^*) + \underbrace{V_f(x_{N+1}) - V_f(x_N) + \ell(x_N, \kappa_f(x_N))}_{\le 0},
\text{hence}\qquad \boxed{V_N^*(x_{t+1}) - V_N^*(x_t) \le -\ell(x_t, u_t^*)}.

The bracketed term is at most zero by condition 3. With a positive-definite stage cost, the optimal value decreases along the closed loop and acts as a Lyapunov function; the origin is asymptotically stable on the feasible set.

Linear-quadratic ingredients. For u = -Kx and V_f = x^\top P x from the DARE, Part 5's identity gives V_f((A - BK)x) - V_f(x) = -x^\top (Q + K^\top R K)\, x = -\ell(x, -Kx): condition 3 holds with equality. Conditions 1 and 2 are what the maximal invariant set of Part 6 was built to provide. So the LQR pair (K, P) and the set \mathcal X_f are the terminal ingredients, and chapter 03 assembles them.

Try it on the demo. The last readout counts the steps on which V_N^*(x_{t+1}) - V_N^*(x_t) + \ell(x_t, u_t) > 0, that is, where the boxed inequality fails. With the notebook's setting, P = Q and no terminal set, it fails on 3 of 10 steps from (-7, 0), and with N = 4 on 7 of 10. With the LQR cost alone it fails on none at N = 8 but still on one at N = 4: the terminal controller would violate the input bound from where the plan ends. With both ingredients it fails on none, for every horizon and start you can set, which is the theorem, and when the horizon is too short to reach \mathcal X_f the problem is honestly infeasible rather than quietly unproven.

What this theorem does not say. It does not say every MPC formulation is stable. It does not say a long horizon proves stability. It does not make nominal guarantees robust to disturbances or model error. It does not remove the need to check that the initial condition is feasible. These distinctions become crucial on real autonomous systems.

Part 8

Dynamic programming and value functions

Bellman's principle of optimality. For x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k) and the finite-horizon cost J = g_N(x_N) + \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} g_k(x_k, u_k), define the optimal cost-to-go J_k^*(x) from stage k onward. The terminal condition is J_N^*(x) = g_N(x), and the backward recursion is

\boxed{J_k^*(x) = \min_{u \in \mathcal U_k(x)} \Big[ g_k(x, u) + J_{k+1}^*\big(f(x, u)\big) \Big]}.

This is Bellman's equation for deterministic finite-horizon optimal control [7]. For the unconstrained linear-quadratic case it is exactly the Riccati iteration of Part 5: J_k^*(x) = x^\top P_k x, and the DARE is its fixed point.

The deep connection to MPC. An ideal infinite-horizon value function contains the exact cost of the entire future. MPC cannot compute it online in general, so it truncates the horizon and inserts a terminal cost V_f as an approximation of the missing tail. Terminal costs are not arbitrary tuning terms: conceptually they approximate a value function, and when V_f is the exact infinite-horizon value, as the LQR P is for the unconstrained problem, the finite-horizon MPC is infinite-horizon optimal wherever the constraints are inactive.

Dynamic programming solves low-dimensional decision problems recursively, but the value function must be represented over the whole state space: a grid with M points per dimension needs M^n samples in n dimensions. That exponential growth is what motivates approximation, learning and structured optimisation, including MPC itself, which replaces a global value function by an online optimisation from the current state.

Part 9

From designed terminal ingredients to Learning MPC

Why learn a terminal set or value? Classical stabilising MPC asks for a terminal set \mathcal X_f that is invariant and admissible, and a terminal cost V_f with the decrease property. For constrained nonlinear systems these are hard to compute. Learning MPC (LMPC) [9] targets a special but common situation: the same task is repeated over iterations, in regulation runs, repeated path following, manufacturing cycles or racing laps. If an earlier iteration completed the task safely, every state it visited comes with two valuable facts: a known feasible continuation to the goal, the rest of that trajectory, and an observed cost-to-go along it.

The sampled safe set. Let iteration i produce a successful trajectory \mathbf x^i = (x_0^i, \dots, x_{T_i}^i) with admissible inputs \mathbf u^i. After iterations 0, \dots, j,

\boxed{\mathcal{SS}^j = \bigcup_{i=0}^{j} \bigcup_{t=0}^{T_i} \{x_t^i\}} \qquad (\text{possibly augmented by a terminal region } \mathcal O).

It is "safe" because from each stored state at least one admissible sequence that completes the task is known: the remainder of its own history. For deterministic repeated tasks this is a data-driven certificate that a feasible continuation exists, for the sampled points.

The convex safe set and barycentric coordinates. For a linear system with convex constraints, convexify: \mathcal{CS}^j = \mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{SS}^j). A point x \in \mathcal{CS}^j is x = \sum_{r=1}^{M} \lambda_r x_r with \lambda_r \ge 0 and \sum_r \lambda_r = 1; the \lambda_r are its barycentric coordinates. Why does this help? If each stored x_r has an admissible continuation, the same convex combination of the stored inputs drives x along the same combination of the stored next states, by linearity, and convex constraints keep the combination admissible. Under those assumptions the convex safe set is control invariant.

Learning a terminal value from stored cost-to-go. For a stored trajectory i, the realised cost-to-go from time t is J^i_{t \to T_i} = \sum_{k=t}^{T_i - 1} \ell(x_k^i, u_k^i) + V_{\mathcal O}(x_{T_i}^i). For a point written as a convex combination of stored states, a learned terminal value is the cheapest such combination:

\boxed{\widehat V^j(x) = \min_{\lambda} \sum_r \lambda_r J_r} \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \sum_r \lambda_r x_r = x, \qquad \sum_r \lambda_r = 1, \qquad \lambda_r \ge 0 .

An LMPC then uses x_N \in \mathcal{CS}^j and V_f(x_N) = \widehat V^j(x_N): the classical pair (\mathcal X_f, V_f) is replaced by the data-driven pair (\mathcal{CS}^j, \widehat V^j). Chapter 05 runs it on this very double integrator and watches the lap cost fall iteration by iteration.

LMPC is not deep reinforcement learning. In the formulation studied here the "learning" is entirely geometric and optimisation-based: successful trajectories supply terminal feasibility and terminal performance information. No neural network is involved.

Part 10

Preview: autonomous racing in Frenet coordinates

Why change coordinates? For path following and racing, the global Cartesian coordinates (X, Y) are not the most natural ones for optimisation. If the track centreline is parameterised by arc length s, define the Frenet-type errors (s, e_y, e_\psi): s is the progress along the path, e_y the lateral deviation from the centreline, and e_\psi the heading error relative to the path tangent. A track boundary becomes the simple constraint

|e_y(s)| \le W(s),

a bound on one coordinate, where in Cartesian coordinates it would be a curve. A minimum-time raceline problem then trades off progress against track curvature \kappa(s), vehicle dynamics, tyre and input limits, and the boundaries [10]. Chapters 06 and 07 build on this geometry.

Part 11

Common conceptual pitfalls

Ten things worth catching before an implementation review.

  1. Discrete stability is not \operatorname{Re}\lambda < 0. That is continuous time. Discrete time requires |\lambda| < 1.
  2. With u = -Kx, the closed-loop matrix is A - BK. A plus sign is a different convention, and for the notebook's gain it gives eigenvalues 0.61 and 3.16, an unstable loop (exercise 3).
  3. A feasible MPC problem is not automatically stable. Feasibility and stability are different properties.
  4. A long horizon is not a proof. It can improve behaviour, but a guarantee needs assumptions and analysis.
  5. A terminal cost is not just an extra weight. It stands for the unmodelled tail of the horizon and can carry a Lyapunov proof.
  6. A terminal set is not merely a final box. In stabilising MPC it is designed to be admissible and invariant under a local controller.
  7. Nominal constraint satisfaction is not robust safety. Disturbances and model error need additional design.
  8. Control invariance has an existential input quantifier. \forall x \in \mathcal C,\ \exists u.
  9. LMPC does not require a neural network. Safe sets and value functions are learned directly from successful trajectories.
  10. The prediction model is not the plant. MPC performance and robustness depend on the gap between them.
Part 12

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question before opening it.

1. Why is MPC a feedback controller if it solves an open-loop optimisation?

At each sampling instant the optimisation is re-solved from the newly measured or estimated state. The applied law is u(t) = u_{0|t}^*(x(t)): the first element of the optimiser's answer depends on the current state and so defines an implicit feedback map.

2. What is the algebraic backbone of condensed linear MPC?

The stacked prediction identity X = \mathcal S_x x_0 + \mathcal S_u U, which eliminates the predicted states and expresses them as an affine function of the initial state and the future inputs.

3. When is a linear MPC problem a convex QP?

With linear or affine dynamics, a convex quadratic objective (Q, P \succeq 0, R \succ 0) and affine or polyhedral constraints. The condensed Hessian is then positive definite, because R \succ 0 (Part 3), and the constraints are linear.

4. What is the difference between feasible and recursively feasible?

Feasible: an admissible sequence exists at the current state. Recursively feasible: applying the controller keeps the next state inside the set of states from which the problem is still feasible.

5. What makes the standard terminal-set proof work?

A local terminal controller that keeps \mathcal X_f invariant while satisfying the constraints, and a terminal cost whose decrease under that controller dominates the stage cost. The shifted optimal sequence plus one terminal move is then feasible at the next step, and the optimal value decreases.

6. Why does LQR naturally provide an MPC terminal cost?

The DARE solution gives V_f = x^\top P x and u = -Kx with V_f((A - BK)x) - V_f(x) = -x^\top (Q + K^\top R K) x = -\ell(x, -Kx): the terminal decrease condition, with equality.

7. Why is a sampled LMPC state "safe" in the deterministic repeated-task setting?

It lies on a previously successful admissible trajectory. The remainder of that stored trajectory is a known feasible continuation from the state to the task's completion region.

8. Why can convexification preserve safety in the linear-convex LMPC setting?

Linearity maps convex combinations of states and inputs to the same convex combination of next states, and convex constraints preserve admissibility. Barycentric combinations of known safe transitions therefore remain admissible under the standard assumptions.

9. How are Bellman value functions related to MPC terminal costs?

The exact infinite-horizon value function is the optimal cost of the entire future tail. Finite-horizon MPC truncates the future and uses V_f as an approximation of, or a certificate for, the omitted tail.

10. What changes first when moving from textbook MPC to a real vehicle?

The assumptions: the state is estimated rather than known, the prediction model is imperfect, disturbances exist, constraints may be uncertain, actuators have dynamics and delays, and computation is finite. These motivate observers, robust and stochastic MPC, model adaptation, safety monitors and real-time implementation analysis.

    Solution

    (1) Feasibility, and precisely recursive feasibility, since it is claimed at every closed-loop step. (2) Stability, specifically attractivity of the origin.

Vehicle–bike collision avoidance

A vehicle approaches a crossing that a bicycle will occupy at a known time. Should it stay on the near side, or accelerate and clear the crossing first? The vehicle model is a point mass; the difficulty is the word "or", which chapter A15 turned into one binary variable and two convex programs. This lab runs that decision to the end: both strategies are solved as quadratic programs for every one of 660 initial states, live, and you can change the friction, the horizon, the cost, the discretisation and the meaning of "yield".

Part 1

The scene

Symbol Value Meaning
d_{\mathrm{bike}} 100 m centre of the bicycle's conflict region, along the road
w_{\mathrm{bike}} 8 m width of that region measured along the vehicle's travel direction
l_{\mathrm{car}} 4.5 m vehicle length
t_{\min} 4 s when the bicycle enters the region
v_{\max}, T_s, g 32 m/s, 1 s, 9.81 m/s² speed limit, sample time, gravity

The two strategies, from chapter A15. The vehicle centre d is collision-free at the conflict time if its front stays short of the near edge, d \le d_{\max} = d_{\mathrm{bike}} - (l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}) / 2 = 93.75 m (yield), or its rear has cleared the far edge, d \ge d_{\min} = 106.25 m (pass). The forbidden centre positions form a gap of l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}} = 12.5 m, and the safe set, the union of two half-lines, is not convex: the midpoint of two safe positions is the middle of the crossing. Chapter A15 showed that with a single binary variable the mixed-integer program is solved exactly by solving the two convex branches and keeping the cheaper feasible one. That is what this lab does, 1320 times per map.

What is a strategy map?

In plain words: for every starting position and speed on a grid, the answer to two questions: how many of the two manoeuvres can be executed at all, and which of them is cheaper.

More precisely: on the grid d_0 \in \{0, 5, \dots, 95\}, v_0 \in \{0, 1, \dots, 32\}, 660 states, the feasible count \in \{0, 1, 2\} of the yield and pass QPs, and the code of the cheaper feasible one. Feasibility depends on the model, the horizon and the constraints; preference depends on the cost as well. The map keeps the two apart.

Yield, pass
Stay short of the region; be beyond it, while the bicycle is present.
Feasible count
How many manoeuvres exist from a state, before any cost is consulted.
Critical friction
The \mu at which the reachable interval of terminal positions equals the forbidden gap.
Occupancy window
The conflict instants over which safety must hold.
Part 2

Vehicle model, constraints and cost

Longitudinal point mass. \dot d = v, \dot v = a, state x = (d, v), input u = a. Discretised with explicit Euler and T_s = 1 s,

d_{k+1} = d_k + T_s v_k, \qquad v_{k+1} = v_k + T_s a_k, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & T_s \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ T_s \end{pmatrix}.

A subtle consequence, from chapter A1: a_k changes the next velocity but does not enter d_{k+1} directly. The exact zero-order-hold discretisation has d_{k+1} = d_k + T_s v_k + \tfrac12 T_s^2 a_k, so B_{\mathrm{ZOH}} = (\tfrac12 T_s^2, T_s). The lab keeps Euler, as the published exercise does and because Part 3 has closed-form reachability for it; with a one-second sample the choice visibly moves feasibility boundaries, which the demo's last toggle and exercise 7 show.

Constraints. Speed 0 \le v_k \le 32 m/s, the lower bound forbidding reversing in this one-dimensional scene. Acceleration is friction-limited,

-\mu g \le a_k \le \mu g: \qquad \mu = 0.10 \Rightarrow |a_k| \le 0.981 \text{ m/s}^2, \qquad \mu = 0.05 \Rightarrow |a_k| \le 0.4905 \text{ m/s}^2,

an intentionally simplified model: symmetric limits, a known constant \mu, no drag or powertrain limits, no combined longitudinal and lateral tyre usage.

Cost. With R = 1, Q = 3, P = 3 and reference speed v_{\mathrm{ref}} = 20 m/s,

J = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} R a_k^2 + \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} Q (v_k - v_{\mathrm{ref}})^2 + P (v_N - v_{\mathrm{ref}})^2 .

Effort, speed tracking during the manoeuvre, terminal speed error. The position does not appear in the cost at all; it enters only through the collision-avoidance constraints. Each fixed strategy is then the finite-horizon problem of chapter A5: linear dynamics, a convex quadratic objective, linear state and input constraints, plus d_k \le d_{\max} during the bicycle's presence for yield or d_k \ge d_{\min} for pass.

Part 3

The nominal scenario

Which yield condition? The geometric condition d_k \le d_{\max} includes the vehicle and crossing geometry. A looser centre-line variant asks only that the vehicle centre has not passed the centre of the crossing, d_N \le d_{\mathrm{bike}}, which admits positions where the front already overlaps the region. Neither imposes a terminal speed, so yielding does not mean stopping. The lab uses the geometric bound by default and labels the centre-line one explicitly when used.

A horizon that ends when the bicycle arrives. The nominal scenario sets T = t_{\min} = 4 s, so the strategy constraint is imposed only at k = N; Part 8 extends it to a whole occupancy window. From x_0 = (60, 5):

\text{yield: feasible, } J = 2582.56,\ x_4 = (85.886,\ 8.924); \qquad \text{pass: infeasible.}

Yield is selected. One thing the plots do not say out loud: the selected yield plan accelerates at the full +0.981 m/s² for all four steps and ends at d_4 = 85.9 m, short of d_{\max} = 93.75 with room to spare. The yield constraint is not active, and its multiplier (chapter A3) is zero. From this state the vehicle cannot reach the region in four seconds whatever it does, so "yield" here is simply speed tracking towards 20 m/s, and the only thing the collision logic did was rule passing out.

Why passing is impossible from here. Even maximal acceleration cannot move the centre beyond d_{\min} = 106.25 m by t = 4 s under the Euler model. Part 3 shows this from the dynamics rather than from the solver.

Part 4

Analytic reachability under the Euler model

Closed-form velocity and terminal position. From v_{k+1} = v_k + T_s a_k, v_k = v_0 + T_s \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} a_j; from d_{k+1} = d_k + T_s v_k, d_N = d_0 + T_s \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} v_k, and substituting,

d_N = d_0 + N T_s v_0 + T_s^2 \sum_{j=0}^{N-2} (N - 1 - j)\, a_j .

Notice that a_{N-1} does not affect d_N: it changes v_N, but the final position was already computed from v_{N-1}. A discretisation-specific property worth knowing before reading the optimiser's output.

Extrema when the speed bounds are inactive. Setting every position-relevant acceleration to \pm\mu g and using \sum_{r=1}^{N-1} r = N(N-1)/2,

d_N^{\max / \min} = d_0 + N T_s v_0 \pm \mu g T_s^2 \frac{N(N-1)}{2}.

For x_0 = (60, 5), N = 4, \mu = 0.10: d_4 \in [74.114, 85.886]. The maximum is below d_{\min} = 106.25, so passing is dynamically impossible; the yield bound 93.75 is above the maximum, so yielding costs nothing. That is the analytic explanation of the solver's verdict, and of the inactive constraint.

Width of the controllable interval and the critical friction. The reachable terminal positions span d_N^{\max} - d_N^{\min} = \mu g T_s^2 N(N-1), while the forbidden gap is l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}. A necessary condition for some initial state to have both strategies reachable is therefore \mu g T_s^2 N(N-1) \ge l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}, with the critical value

\mu_{\mathrm{crit}} = \frac{l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}}{g T_s^2 N(N-1)} = \frac{12.5}{9.81 \cdot 12} = 0.1062 .

At \mu = 0.10 the width is 11.77 m, just short of the 12.5 m gap, so no initial state has both strategies feasible, which the map at the top confirms with zero "both" cells; at \mu = 0.12 the width is 14.13 m and overlap becomes possible.

Part 5

Friction and feasibility

A set-inclusion argument. Let \mathcal U(\mu) = [-\mu g, \mu g]. If 0 < \mu_1 < \mu_2 then \mathcal U(\mu_1) \subset \mathcal U(\mu_2): every input sequence admissible at low friction is admissible at high friction. With everything else unchanged,

\mathcal F(\mu_1) \subseteq \mathcal F(\mu_2),

where \mathcal F(\mu) is the set of initial states from which at least one modelled strategy is feasible. "Lower friction enlarges the infeasible region" is a theorem about sets, not an observation from a plot.

Strategy maps. The numbers script solves both QPs on the 660-state grid and keeps two maps: how many strategies are feasible (0, 1 or 2) and which feasible one is cheaper. That separates feasibility from preference. At \mu = 0.10 there are 7 states with no strategy; at \mu = 0.05, 36, an increase of 29 that the inclusion argument guarantees for every state, not only grid points: the script checks that no state loses a strategy when the friction rises. The infeasible states lie along the line d_0 + 4 v_0 \approx 100: too fast to stop short, too slow to clear. The state (85, 3) is the closest call: its optimal yield plan brakes hard, then accelerates, and ends exactly on the bound d_4 = 93.75, an active constraint that a less careful solver can mistake for infeasibility. The demo recomputes both maps on every slider change.

Part 6

What do Q, R and P actually change?

Objective weights do not change feasibility. For a fixed model, horizon, state, input and collision constraints, changing Q, R, P changes only the objective; it cannot create or destroy feasible trajectories. In exact optimisation,

\text{the feasible set is independent of } Q, R, P.

What the weights can change is which feasible trajectory is optimal and, when both branches are feasible, which is cheaper.

A state where both are feasible. With \mu = 0.15 and x_0 = (30, 17), both strategies are feasible, and only R is varied:

R yield cost yield \sum a^2 pass cost pass \sum a^2 chosen
0.1 220.80 6.92 36.69 5.78 pass
1 226.88 6.23 41.76 5.52 pass
1000 1563.44 1.29 5165.38 5.13 yield

With a small effort penalty, speed tracking dominates and passing, which keeps the speed near 20, is preferred. With R = 1000 the optimiser prefers the branch needing less acceleration, and yield, which can coast, wins. Both branches stay feasible throughout. The correct separation: \mu changes control authority and feasibility; Q, R, P change preference among feasible solutions. Set the demo's friction to 0.15, the state to (30, 17) and sweep R to watch the colour flip.

Part 7

"Yield" is not automatically "stop"

Three distinct statements that casual language conflates. A, geometric yield: d_N \le d_{\max}, the vehicle is on the safe near side at the conflict time. B, full stop: d_N \le d_{\max} and v_N = 0, strictly more restrictive. C, centre-line condition: d_N \le d_{\mathrm{bike}}, looser than A because d_{\mathrm{bike}} > d_{\max}. From x_0 = (60, 5): A feasible, C feasible, B infeasible: the vehicle can stay short of the region but cannot brake from 5 m/s to zero in four seconds at 0.981 m/s² (it needs 5.1 s). Hence

\text{yield feasible} \ \not\Rightarrow\ \text{full stop feasible}.

Toggle "yield must end at a full stop" in the demo: the yield-feasible region collapses from 268 grid states to 75, roughly those with v_0 \le N \mu g = 3.9 m/s and enough room (exercise 5).

Part 8

The general bicycle-occupancy window

From a terminal-time condition to a time-indexed one. Suppose the bicycle occupies the region over t \in [t_{\min}, t_{\max}] = [4, 6] and the horizon continues beyond, N = 8. Yield must then impose d_k \le d_{\max} for k = 4, 5, 6, not merely at the first instant; pass must impose d_k \ge d_{\min} over the same steps, although with v_k \ge 0 the later pass inequalities are redundant, since a vehicle that has cleared the region cannot come back. For yield, enforcing the whole window is essential. From (60, 5), the window problem selects yield with cost 4730.0: the vehicle brakes briefly, holds its centre at exactly d_6 = 93.75 as the bicycle leaves, then accelerates to reach 112 m at k = 8.

Why one binary variable is meaningful. One strategy variable for the whole window commits to one topological manoeuvre: near side throughout, or far side throughout. Independent binaries s_k at every step would let the optimiser be yield-side at one conflict instant and pass-side at a later one, which physically means driving through the forbidden interval while the bicycle is there. Logical variables must encode the manoeuvre-level logic, not individual inequalities.

Part 9

Interpreting infeasibility, and the model's limitations

"Neither strategy is feasible" has a precise but limited meaning. It says that under this model no admissible acceleration sequence satisfies either manoeuvre. It does not prove a collision is physically unavoidable: the model excludes steering around the bicycle, the bicycle reacting, leaving the lane, and emergency manoeuvres outside the acceleration envelope. The correct statement is that neither modelled longitudinal strategy is feasible under the stated assumptions, and that qualification matters in safety analysis.

Limitations. The bicycle's timing is exactly known, where real systems have uncertainty in detection, speed, intent and arrival time; a robust formulation would require safety for every arrival time in a set, a chance-constrained one a probabilistic risk bound. The friction is a known scalar, where a conservative robust option replaces \mu by a guaranteed lower bound \underline\mu (exercise 8); richer models use friction circles and combined-slip tyres [4]. The vehicle is a one-dimensional point mass without steering, lateral position, yaw, tyre slip, actuator delay or estimation error. This is deliberate: it isolates one idea,

\text{simple dynamics} + \text{logical environment constraint} \Rightarrow \text{hybrid, nonconvex optimal control},

and lab B3 moves to the Frenet coordinates that make lateral constraints tractable along a track.

Part 10

The code behind it

The strategy maps are a loop over the grid that calls choose_collision_strategy, which in turn solves the two branch QPs with the package's interior-point solver (chapter A4).

advanced_mpc_lmpc/collision.py · strategy_grid

def strategy_grid(d_values, v_values, **kwargs):
    """
    Evaluate strategy feasibility and optimal choice on an initial-state grid.

    Returns integer arrays with codes:
        feasible_count: 0,1,2
        best_code: -1 infeasible, 0 yield, 1 pass
    """
    d_values = np.asarray(d_values, float)
    v_values = np.asarray(v_values, float)

    feasible_count = np.zeros((len(v_values), len(d_values)), dtype=int)
    best_code = -np.ones((len(v_values), len(d_values)), dtype=int)

    for iv, v0 in enumerate(v_values):
        for idd, d0 in enumerate(d_values):
            best, sols = choose_collision_strategy(
                np.array([d0, v0]),
                **kwargs,
            )

            feasible_count[iv, idd] = sum(sol.success for sol in sols)

            if best is not None:
                best_code[iv, idd] = 0 if best.strategy == "yield" else 1

    return feasible_count, best_code

Two integer arrays, one per question. feasible_count counts the branches whose QP converged with every constraint satisfied; best_code records the cheaper one, and stays -1 when none is feasible. The keyword arguments are passed straight through to the branch solver, which is how the demo's toggles (the centre-line bound, the full stop, the zero-order hold) reach the QP without any other code changing. Feasibility is decided by the solver's success flag, which for the interior-point method means that the KKT residuals are below tolerance; an independent linear-programming certificate for the doubtful cases is in lab B6.

The reachability bounds of Part 3 are two lines of arithmetic, and the function says in its docstring why the last acceleration is absent.

advanced_mpc_lmpc/collision.py · terminal_position_extrema_no_speed_limits

def terminal_position_extrema_no_speed_limits(
    x0,
    *,
    N=4,
    Ts=1.0,
    mu=0.10,
    g=9.81,
):
    """
    Analytic terminal-position extrema for the *Euler* workshop model
    when speed constraints are inactive.

    d_N = d_0 + N Ts v_0
          + Ts^2 sum_{j=0}^{N-2} (N-1-j) a_j.

    The last acceleration a_{N-1} affects v_N but not d_N under the
    explicit Euler position update.
    """
    d0, v0 = np.asarray(x0, float).reshape(2)
    amax = float(mu) * float(g)

    leverage = Ts**2 * N * (N - 1) / 2.0
    nominal = d0 + N * Ts * v0

    return (
        float(nominal - amax * leverage),
        float(nominal + amax * leverage),
    )

leverage is T_s^2 N(N-1)/2, the sum \sum_{j=0}^{N-2} (N - 1 - j) times T_s^2: how far one unit of acceleration, held for all the position-relevant steps, moves the terminal position. The bounds are the nominal coasting position plus and minus \mu g times that leverage, and they are exact only while the speed bounds 0 \le v \le 32 stay inactive, which is the caveat in the function's name.

Part 11

Common pitfalls

  1. "The double-integrator dynamics make the problem convex." No. Each fixed strategy is a convex QP; the union "d \le d_{\max} or d \ge d_{\min}" is not. The logic, not the plant, creates the nonconvexity.
  2. "Lower \mu only changes comfort." No. It bounds the input, so it changes the reachable set and feasibility.
  3. "Changing Q or R changes the feasible region." Not with the constraints and model unchanged. Weights change optimality.
  4. "Yield means v_N = 0." Only if imposed. The nominal formulation imposes no terminal speed.
  5. "d_N \le d_{\mathrm{bike}} is geometric collision avoidance." No. It admits d_N \in (d_{\max}, d_{\mathrm{bike}}], where the front already overlaps the region.
  6. "Safe at the instant the bicycle arrives means safe." No. The yield constraint must hold at every conflict instant; Part 8 has a state that is safe at k = 4 and inside the crossing at k = 5.
  7. "Solver infeasibility means a collision is unavoidable." It means no solution exists within the modelled dynamics, bounds, strategies and prediction assumptions.
Part 12

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question before opening it.

1. Why is each fixed-strategy problem convex?

Linear dynamics, linear state, input and strategy constraints once the branch is fixed, and a convex quadratic objective.

2. Why can two QPs replace the mixed-integer program here?

There is one binary decision. Enumerating s = 0 and s = 1 gives exactly the minimum of the two branch-optimal costs, as chapter A15 showed.

3. What does \mu do mathematically?

It scales the admissible input interval a \in [-\mu g, \mu g]. Reducing it gives a subset of the former control set, so the set of feasible initial states cannot grow.

4. Do Q, R and P change feasibility?

No, not when only the weights change. They change which feasible trajectory or strategy is optimal; feasibility is set by the dynamics and constraints.

5. What is unusual about the last acceleration under explicit Euler?

a_{N-1} changes v_N but not d_N, because the position update uses v_{N-1} and has no direct acceleration term.

6. Why is "yield" different from "stop"?

Yield can mean only that the vehicle remains before the region. A full stop additionally requires v_N = 0 or an equivalent condition.

7. Why must the yield constraint be enforced for the whole occupancy window?

Being safe at the instant the bicycle enters does not stop the vehicle from entering the region a step later while the bicycle is still there.

8. Why is the yield constraint inactive at the nominal state?

Because even full acceleration ends the horizon at 85.9 m, short of the 93.75 m bound: the optimiser's answer is pure speed tracking and the multiplier of the yield row is zero.

9. What is the critical friction a condition for?

It is necessary for the two strategy regions to overlap anywhere: the reachable interval of terminal positions must be at least as wide as the forbidden gap. It is not sufficient, and it says nothing about preference.

10. What does "both strategies infeasible" actually prove?

Only that neither modelled longitudinal manoeuvre is possible within the chosen horizon and constraints; not that every physically possible avoidance has failed.

Part 13

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution; the code runs with the course package installed. Try first, then peek.

  1. Euler prediction. From d_{k+1} = d_k + T_s v_k and v_{k+1} = v_k + T_s a_k, derive d_N = d_0 + N T_s v_0 + T_s^2 \sum_{j=0}^{N-2} (N - 1 - j) a_j.

    Hint

    Write v_k as a sum of accelerations, then sum the velocities and count how many times each a_j appears.

    Solution

    v_k = v_0 + T_s \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} a_j, and d_N = d_0 + T_s \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} v_k = d_0 + N T_s v_0 + T_s^2 \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} a_j. The acceleration a_j appears in v_k for every k \ge j + 1, that is N - 1 - j times for j \le N - 2 and zero times for j = N - 1. Swapping the sums gives the formula.

  2. Critical friction. Ignoring active speed constraints, derive \mu_{\mathrm{crit}} = (l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}) / (g T_s^2 N(N-1)). What does it mean, and what does it not guarantee?

    Hint

    Compare the width of the reachable terminal interval with the forbidden gap.

    Solution

    The reachable terminal positions span \mu g T_s^2 N(N-1); both strategies can only be reachable from one state if that span is at least the gap l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}, which gives \mu_{\mathrm{crit}} = 0.1062 for N = 4. It is a necessary condition for the two feasible regions to overlap anywhere, and only that: it does not say overlap occurs, since the speed bounds may bind and the interval must also be placed so that both bounds fall inside it, and it says nothing about which strategy is preferred.

  3. Build the strategy map yourself. Without strategy_grid, loop over the 660 states, call solve_collision_strategy for both branches, and count the states with no feasible strategy at \mu = 0.10 and \mu = 0.05. Then check the inclusion \mathcal F(0.05) \subseteq \mathcal F(0.10) state by state.

    Hint

    A state is in \mathcal F(\mu) when at least one branch's success is true; inclusion means no state is feasible at 0.05 and infeasible at 0.10.

    Solution

    7 states have no strategy at \mu = 0.10 and 36 at \mu = 0.05; the inclusion holds at every one of the 660 states, as the set argument of Part 5 promises for all states, grid or not.

    import numpy as np
from advanced_mpc_lmpc.collision import solve_collision_strategy
D = np.arange(0, 100, 5); V = np.arange(0, 33)
def feasible(mu):
    return np.array([[any(solve_collision_strategy(np.array([d0, v0]), s, N=4, mu=mu).success
                          for s in ("yield", "pass")) for d0 in D] for v0 in V])
F10, F05 = feasible(0.10), feasible(0.05)
print((~F10).sum(), (~F05).sum())          # 7 36
print(np.all(F05 <= F10))                  # True: feasible at low friction implies feasible at high friction

    Part 6's state and friction.

    Solution

    At \mu = 0.15 and (30, 17), both branches are feasible for R = 0.1, 1, 1000 (the solver succeeds every time, with unchanged constraints), and the choice flips from pass to yield at large R. The demo reproduces the table of Part 6.

  5. Full stop. Add v_N = 0 to the yield branch, map the initial states for which a full stop is feasible, and compare with the geometric-yield feasible set.

    Hint

    terminal_stop=True; a stop needs v_0 \le N \mu g and enough distance.

    Solution

    On the 660-state grid, geometric yield is feasible from 268 states and a full stop from 75. A stop in four seconds needs v_0 \le 4 \cdot 0.981 = 3.92 m/s, so only v_0 \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\} qualify at all, and the car must also not cross d_{\max} while braking: with v_0 = 3 the closest admissible start is d_0 = 85, with v_0 = 0 it is 95. Yield-feasible states lie along and below the line d_0 + 4v_0 \approx 100; stop-feasible ones in a thin strip at the bottom of the map.

  6. Occupancy window. With t_{\min} = 4, t_{\max} = 6, N = 8, compare enforcing yield only at t_{\min} with enforcing it over the whole window, and find a state where the first is feasible but the second is not.

    A fast car far away can be short of the region at k = 4 and inside it at k = 5.

    Solution

    From (0, 19): the single-instant version is feasible and its plan has d_4, d_5, d_6 = 78.7, 98.7, 118.7, driving straight through the crossing at steps 5 and 6 while the bicycle is there.   7. Discretisation. Repeat the feasibility map with the exact zero-order-hold discretisation and explain why the boundary moves.

    Hint

    Under ZOH the current acceleration already moves the next position by \tfrac12 T_s^2 a_k.

    Solution

    Under ZOH every acceleration acts on the position half a step earlier, so the reachable terminal interval widens: from (60, 5) full throttle reaches d_4 = 87.85 instead of 85.89. On the grid, the states with no strategy fall from 7 to 1, and 14 states gain both strategies where Euler had none, because the wider interval can straddle the gap. The physics did not change; the discretisation's one-step lag in the position update did, which is why a one-second sample is coarse for this problem (chapter A1).

count_zoh, best_zoh = strategy_grid(D, V, N=4, mu=0.10, discretization="zoh")
print((count_zoh == 0).sum(), (count_zoh == 2).sum())    # 1 14, against 7 0 for Euler

  8. Friction uncertainty. With \mu \in [0.05, 0.10], formulate a robust acceleration bound that guarantees admissibility for every value in the interval, and discuss its conservatism.

    Hint

    Plan for the worst friction.

    Solution

    Impose |a_k| \le \underline\mu g = 0.4905 m/s². Every such sequence is admissible for every \mu \ge 0.05, so the plan is safe whatever the road turns out to be. The price is the \mu = 0.05 map: 36 instead of 7 grid states with no strategy, 29 states given up although most of them would be fine on the actual road. A friction estimator that narrows the interval buys those states back.

  9. Beyond one dimension. List the states, inputs and constraints needed to let the vehicle steer around the region rather than only brake or accelerate.

    Hint

    What does a lane change need that a longitudinal model lacks?

    Solution

    States: lateral position and heading (and yaw rate for a dynamic model), so at least (d, y, \psi, v). Inputs: steering angle or lateral acceleration alongside a. Constraints: lane and road boundaries on y, a steering-rate limit, a combined tyre limit replacing the scalar bound, for instance a_x^2 + a_y^2 \le (\mu g)^2, and a two-dimensional collision constraint, the car's rectangle against the bicycle's region, which is again disjunctive: pass in front, behind, left or right, four binaries or a convex outer approximation. The nonconvex logic multiplies; lab B3 moves to the Frenet coordinates that make such constraints tractable along a track.

Part 14

The chapter on one page

  1. Geometry: safe centre positions are d \le 93.75 or d \ge 106.25, a nonconvex union with a 12.5 m gap (chapter A15).
  2. Model: Euler point mass, 0 \le v \le 32, |a| \le \mu g, speed-tracking cost with no position term.
  3. Logic: each strategy is a convex QP; the choice is one binary variable; two QPs solve the MIQP exactly.
  4. Nominal: from (60, 5), yield at cost 2582.6 with its bound inactive; pass impossible because d_4 \le 85.9 < 106.25.
  5. Reachability: d_N = d_0 + N v_0 \pm \mu g N(N-1)/2; \mu_{\mathrm{crit}} = 0.106, just above the nominal 0.10.
  6. Friction: \mathcal F(\mu_1) \subseteq \mathcal F(\mu_2); 7 and 36 of 660 states without a strategy at \mu = 0.10 and 0.05.
  7. Weights: change preference only; at (30, 17), \mu = 0.15, the choice flips from pass to yield as R grows.
  8. Yield is not stop: a full stop is infeasible from the nominal state and feasible from 75 states against 268; the window must be enforced at every conflict step.
  9. Meaning: infeasibility is a statement about the model, not about the world.

The vocabulary, in one table

Word Meaning
Conflict region, forbidden interval Where the bicycle will be; the centre positions that would overlap it.
Yield, pass Stay short of the region; be beyond it, during the occupancy.
Strategy map Feasible count and cheaper strategy for every initial state.
Critical friction The \mu at which the reachable interval equals the forbidden gap.
Centre-line bound The looser yield condition d \le d_{\mathrm{bike}}, not collision-free.
Occupancy window The conflict instants over which safety must hold.
Set inclusion \mathcal F(\mu_1) \subseteq \mathcal F(\mu_2) Less friction, fewer feasible starts, for every state.

Scope. A deterministic one-dimensional finite-horizon problem: longitudinal point mass under explicit Euler, symmetric friction-limited acceleration, bounded speed, a speed-tracking cost, and a bicycle whose occupancy of the crossing is known in advance (one instant in Parts 3 to 7, a window in Part 8). The disjunction is solved exactly by enumerating two convex QPs; the Big-M formulation is chapter A15's. The geometric yield bound is the default; the centre-line condition, a terminal full stop and the exact ZOH discretisation are explicit options.

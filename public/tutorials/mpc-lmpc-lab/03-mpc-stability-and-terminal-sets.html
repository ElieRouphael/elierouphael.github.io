MPC stability and terminal sets

A finite-horizon problem computes a sequence of inputs; MPC applies only the first, measures, shifts and solves again. Two questions follow at once. If the problem is feasible now, why is it still feasible after the first move? And if it stays feasible forever, why does the state converge? The answers are two designed ingredients, a terminal constraint and a terminal cost. Below, both designs run on the constrained double integrator: click a start state and watch which design can handle it, how the feasible regions compare, and whether the optimal value really falls by at least the stage cost at every step.

Part 1

From finite-horizon optimal control to feedback

The ideal infinite-horizon problem. For x_{k+1} = Ax_k + Bu_k, the constrained objective one would like to solve is

J_\infty^\star(x(0)) = \min_{\{u_k\}_{k=0}^{\infty}} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} q(x_k, u_k) \quad \text{subject to}\quad x_k \in \mathcal X,\ u_k \in \mathcal U,

with the stage cost q(x, u) pricing the state and the input. It contains infinitely many decisions. MPC replaces it by a finite-horizon approximation and repeats that approximation in feedback.

The problem solved at real time t. Predicted quantities carry two indices, x_{k|t} and u_{k|t}: t is when the prediction is made, k the future time it refers to, and x_{t|t} = x(t) is the measurement. The horizon-N problem is

\begin{aligned} J_t^\star(x(t)) = \min_{\{u_{k|t}\}}\ & p(x_{t+N|t}) + \sum_{k=t}^{t+N-1} q(x_{k|t}, u_{k|t}) \\ \text{s.t. } & x_{k+1|t} = A x_{k|t} + B u_{k|t}, \quad x_{k|t} \in \mathcal X, \quad u_{k|t} \in \mathcal U, \quad x_{t+N|t} \in \mathcal X_f, \quad x_{t|t} = x(t). \end{aligned}

The receding-horizon law. Of the optimal sequence U^\star_{t \to t+N|t} = (u_{t|t}^\star, \dots, u_{t+N-1|t}^\star), MPC applies only

\boxed{u(t) = u_{t|t}^\star}.

The real system moves to x(t+1) = Ax(t) + Bu(t), the old plan is discarded, and a new problem is solved from the new state. The feedback law u(t) = \kappa_N(x(t)) is defined implicitly by the optimisation, and for a time-invariant model, cost and constraints it is itself time invariant.

Predicted versus closed-loop trajectory. At time t the optimiser predicts x_{t|t}, x_{t+1|t}, \dots, x_{t+N|t}; at t+1 it predicts a new sequence x_{t+1|t+1}, x_{t+2|t+1}, \dots. Only the first transition of each plan is executed. MPC is not "optimise once and execute the whole sequence"; it is

\boxed{\text{optimise} \to \text{apply one action} \to \text{measure} \to \text{optimise again}}.

What are terminal ingredients?

In plain words: a region where the plan must end and a price put on ending there, chosen so that a known local controller could always take over from that region, safely and at a cost the price already accounts for.

More precisely: a terminal set \mathcal X_f \subseteq \mathcal X, a local law v(x) and a terminal cost p with, for every x \in \mathcal X_f: v(x) \in \mathcal U and Ax + Bv(x) \in \mathcal X_f (invariance and admissibility), and p(Ax + Bv(x)) - p(x) \le -q(x, v(x)) (terminal decrease). With a positive-definite stage cost, these make MPC recursively feasible and the optimal value J_N^\star a Lyapunov function on the feasible set \mathcal X_N. For linear-quadratic problems they are the LQR pair (K, P) and the maximal invariant subset of the states where u = -Kx is admissible.

Part 2

What exactly must be guaranteed?

Feasibility. At state x, the horizon-N problem is feasible if at least one admissible input sequence satisfies all predicted constraints and the terminal constraint. The feasible set is \mathcal X_N = \{x : \text{feasible from } x\}. A statement about the optimisation now.

Recursive feasibility. MPC is recursively feasible if x(t) \in \mathcal X_N implies x(t+1) \in \mathcal X_N after the MPC action: feasible now implies feasible again at the next instant, so the optimisation never paints itself into a corner. Current feasibility alone does not imply it.

Constraint satisfaction. If the model is exact, the state is known and the problem stays recursively feasible, the executed first input and state inherit the constraints imposed on the prediction. Distinct from stability: a trajectory may satisfy every constraint and never converge, and a stable unconstrained controller may violate limits.

Stability and the region of attraction. Asymptotic stability of the origin means x(t) \to 0 from every initial state in some set, with small starts staying small. The region of attraction is the set of initial states from which the closed loop converges while keeping the required properties. For designs with stabilising terminal ingredients, the feasible set \mathcal X_N becomes a certified region of attraction.

Part 3

Why a short horizon can fail

Reducing the horizon can destroy stability even when every finite-horizon problem is solved exactly. A scalar example shows the mechanism in one line.

One-step MPC without a terminal cost. Take the unstable plant x^+ = ax + u with a = 1.2 and the stage cost q(x, u) = x^2 + ru^2, r > 0. For N = 1 and no terminal cost the problem is \min_{u_0} x_0^2 + ru_0^2. The future state x_1 does not appear, so u_0^\star = 0, and the closed loop is x(t+1) = 1.2\,x(t): unstable. The optimiser solves its problem perfectly; the design of the problem is the issue.

Add the infinite-horizon LQR terminal cost. With p(x) = Px^2, where P solves the discrete algebraic Riccati equation, the one-step problem is \min_u x^2 + ru^2 + P(ax + u)^2. Differentiating, 2ru + 2P(ax + u) = 0, hence

u^\star = -\frac{Pa}{r + P}\, x,

which is exactly the scalar LQR feedback when P is the Riccati solution. With r = 4: P = 3.8099, K = 0.5854, closed-loop eigenvalue a - K = 0.6146. A carefully chosen terminal cost injects the infinite future into a short horizon.

Part 4

The zero terminal constraint

MPC with x_N = 0.

J_N^\star(x) = \min_U \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} q(x_k, u_k) \quad \text{s.t.}\quad x_{k+1} = Ax_k + Bu_k, \quad x_k \in \mathcal X, \quad u_k \in \mathcal U, \quad \boxed{x_N = 0}, \quad x_0 = x.

The simplest stabilising terminal constraint [4]: theoretically clear, and conservative, since reaching exactly zero within N steps can make the feasible set small.

Recursive feasibility for x_N = 0. Suppose the problem at x(0) is feasible with optimal inputs u_0^\star, \dots, u_{N-1}^\star and predicted states x_0 = x(0), x_1, \dots, x_N = 0. Apply u(0) = u_0^\star; under the nominal model x(1) = x_1. At the next instant consider the shifted candidate

\tilde U = (u_1^\star, u_2^\star, \dots, u_{N-1}^\star, 0).

The old prediction already guarantees that all shifted intermediate states satisfy their constraints; because x_N = 0 and the equilibrium satisfies A0 + B0 = 0, the appended zero input keeps the new terminal state at zero. So the shifted sequence is feasible at x(1):

\boxed{x(0) \in \mathcal X_N \ \Longrightarrow\ x(1) \in \mathcal X_N}.

The value decreases. At x_1 the shifted sequence is feasible, so the new optimum cannot cost more than that candidate: J_N^\star(x_1) \le \sum_{k=1}^{N-1} q(x_k, u_k^\star) + q(0, 0). With q(0, 0) = 0,

\boxed{J_N^\star(x_1) - J_N^\star(x_0) \le -q(x_0, u_0^\star)}.

With a positive-definite stage cost the optimal value decreases strictly away from the equilibrium: a Lyapunov-function candidate. Decrease alone, however, does not prove asymptotic stability; J_N^\star must also be bounded below and above by functions of \lVert x \rVert.

From value decrease to asymptotic stability. A function \alpha : [0, \infty) \to [0, \infty) is of class \mathcal K if it is continuous, strictly increasing and \alpha(0) = 0. Suppose there are class-\mathcal K functions \alpha_1, \alpha_2 and a radius r > 0 with:

  1. Lower bound. q(x, u) \ge \alpha_1(\lVert x \rVert) for all admissible pairs; since J_N^\star(x) \ge q(x, u_0^\star), this gives J_N^\star(x) \ge \alpha_1(\lVert x \rVert) on \mathcal X_N.
  2. Upper bound near the origin. J_N^\star(x) \le \alpha_2(\lVert x \rVert) for x \in \mathcal X_N with \lVert x \rVert \le r; since J_N^\star \ge 0 and J_N^\star(0) = 0, this is continuity of J_N^\star at the origin.
  3. Decrease. J_N^\star(x^+) - J_N^\star(x) \le -q(x, \kappa_N(x)) \le -\alpha_1(\lVert x \rVert) on \mathcal X_N: the boxed inequality.

Then the origin is asymptotically stable with region of attraction \mathcal X_N, which is positively invariant by the feasibility argument [2]. If moreover \alpha_1(s) = c_1 s^2 and J_N^\star(x) \le c_2 \lVert x \rVert^2 on \mathcal X_N, the origin is exponentially stable there:

J_N^\star(x(t+1)) \le (1 - c_1/c_2)\, J_N^\star(x(t)), \qquad \lVert x(t) \rVert \le \sqrt{c_2/c_1}\,(1 - c_1/c_2)^{t/2} \lVert x(0) \rVert .

The conditions do different jobs. Summing the decrease along the loop gives \sum_{t \ge 0} \alpha_1(\lVert x(t) \rVert) \le J_N^\star(x(0)) < \infty, so x(t) \to 0 from every feasible start. The upper bound adds Lyapunov stability, small starts staying small: for \lVert x(0) \rVert \le r, \alpha_1(\lVert x(t) \rVert) \le J_N^\star(x(t)) \le J_N^\star(x(0)) \le \alpha_2(\lVert x(0) \rVert), and the right-hand side vanishes as x(0) \to 0.

Why the upper bound needs an extra assumption when x_N = 0. The lower bound comes from the stage cost and the decrease from the shifted sequence, but nothing gives the upper bound. With x_N = 0 the only admissible plans reach the origin exactly in N steps, and for some systems, typically nonlinear ones, that forces large inputs and a large cost even from tiny states: J_N^\star is then discontinuous at the origin. The extra assumption is local controllability with small inputs: every x with \lVert x \rVert \le r can be steered to the origin in N admissible steps with \lVert u_k \rVert \le \gamma(\lVert x \rVert) for a class-\mathcal K function \gamma; that plan's cost bounds J_N^\star. For the linear model it holds when (A, B) is controllable, N \ge n, and the origin is interior to \mathcal X and \mathcal U: the minimum-norm plan U = -\mathcal C_N^{\dagger} A^N x, with the controllability matrix \mathcal C_N = (A^{N-1}B, \dots, AB, B), reaches zero, is linear in x, hence admissible for small x, and with a quadratic stage cost gives J_N^\star(x) \le c \lVert x \rVert^2 near the origin.

A terminal set provides the upper bound. With a terminal set \mathcal X_f containing the origin in its interior, a local controller v and a terminal cost p as in Part 6, applying v for N steps from any x \in \mathcal X_f is a feasible plan, and summing the terminal decrease along it bounds its cost by p(x):

J_N^\star(x) \le p(x) \qquad \text{for all } x \in \mathcal X_f .

Any terminal cost with p(x) \le \alpha_2(\lVert x \rVert), such as x^\top P x, gives the upper bound on a ball around the origin with no extra controllability assumption. One reason the terminal-set design is preferred to x_N = 0.

Part 5

Horizon length and the feasible region

The double-integrator example. x^+ = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} x + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} u with -5 \le x_i \le 5 and -0.5 \le u \le 0.5. The set of states that can reach x_N = 0 is a finite-step controllability calculation of chapter 02 with the singleton target \{0\}. The notebook finds areas 16.13 for N = 5, 34.95 for N = 10 and 37.29 for N = 20, against 37.29 for the maximal control-invariant set \mathcal C_\infty: fractions 0.432, 0.937 and 1.000.

Interpretation. For the zero terminal condition, the horizon is how much time the optimiser has to drive the state exactly to the origin. A small N excludes states that are perfectly manageable over a longer time scale; as N grows the feasible set approaches the constrained controllability limit. The horizon has a strong effect on the region of attraction, and its price is computational: more decision variables and constraints online.

Part 6

General terminal sets

Why replace x_N = 0 by x_N \in \mathcal X_f? The exact equality is conservative. Choose instead a terminal region with a local controller u = v(x): the online optimiser only has to reach a region from which a known local controller can safely complete the infinite tail. The region enlarges the feasible set; the controller and the terminal cost provide what the proof needs.

The three assumptions [3].

  1. Positive-definite stage cost: q(x, u) > 0 away from the equilibrium and q(0, 0) = 0; quantitatively, q(x, u) \ge \alpha_1(\lVert x \rVert), which for x^\top Qx + u^\top Ru with Q \succ 0 holds with \alpha_1(s) = \lambda_{\min}(Q) s^2.
  2. Terminal invariance and admissibility: a local law v with v(x) \in \mathcal U and Ax + Bv(x) \in \mathcal X_f for all x \in \mathcal X_f, and \mathcal X_f \subseteq \mathcal X.
  3. Terminal Lyapunov decrease:
    \boxed{p(Ax + Bv(x)) - p(x) \le -q(x, v(x))} \qquad \text{for all } x \in \mathcal X_f .

These mimic the missing infinite-horizon tail.

Recursive feasibility with a general terminal set. With the optimal sequence at x_0 ending at x_N \in \mathcal X_f, after applying u_0^\star use the shifted candidate

\boxed{\tilde U = (u_1^\star, \dots, u_{N-1}^\star, v(x_N))}.

The shifted part is admissible because the old predictions were; v(x_N) is admissible and x_{N+1} = Ax_N + Bv(x_N) \in \mathcal X_f by invariance. Feasible again.

The value decreases. At x_0, J_N^\star(x_0) = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} q(x_i, u_i^\star) + p(x_N). At x_1 the candidate gives

J_N^\star(x_1) \le \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} q(x_i, u_i^\star) + q(x_N, v(x_N)) + p(x_{N+1}),

and subtracting the old value,

J_N^\star(x_1) - J_N^\star(x_0) \le -q(x_0, u_0^\star) + \underbrace{q(x_N, v(x_N)) + p(x_{N+1}) - p(x_N)}_{\le 0 \text{ by assumption 3}}.

The bracket is at most zero by assumption 3. Hence

\boxed{J_N^\star(x_1) - J_N^\star(x_0) \le -q(x_0, u_0^\star)}.

The same decrease inequality reappears. The other two conditions of Part 4 hold as well: the lower bound is assumption 1, and the upper bound is J_N^\star \le p on \mathcal X_f when the origin is interior to \mathcal X_f and p(x) \le \alpha_2(\lVert x \rVert). Under assumptions 1 to 3 and that condition, the origin is asymptotically stable with region of attraction \mathcal X_N; in the linear-quadratic design, q \ge \lambda_{\min}(Q) \lVert x \rVert^2 and p \le \lambda_{\max}(P) \lVert x \rVert^2, so the stability is exponential on the bounded set \mathcal X_N.

The two ingredients have different jobs. The terminal set gives a feasible tail: x_N \in \mathcal X_f means v can continue admissibly. The terminal cost gives a cost bound on that tail: p(x^+) - p(x) \le -q(x, v(x)).

\boxed{\text{terminal set} \Rightarrow \text{recursive-feasibility mechanism}}, \qquad \boxed{\text{terminal cost} \Rightarrow \text{Lyapunov-decrease mechanism}}.
Part 7

Linear-quadratic terminal ingredients

Quadratic costs. q(x, u) = x^\top Qx + u^\top Ru with Q \succ 0, R \succ 0, and the terminal cost \boxed{p(x) = x^\top Px} with P the infinite-horizon Riccati solution.

DARE and the sign convention. The course writes u = -Kx; texts that write the local law as v = F_\infty x agree with F_\infty = -K. With

P = A^\top PA - A^\top PB(R + B^\top PB)^{-1} B^\top PA + Q, \qquad \boxed{K = (R + B^\top PB)^{-1} B^\top PA}, \qquad A_{\mathrm{cl}} = A - BK,

discrete-time stability requires \rho(A_{\mathrm{cl}}) < 1. For Q = I, R = 10: K = (0.2054, 0.7835), P = \begin{pmatrix} 3.815 & 4.869 \\ 4.869 & 13.704 \end{pmatrix}, eigenvalues 0.608 \pm 0.228\,i, spectral radius 0.6495.

The LQR terminal-cost identity. Under u = -Kx, p(x^+) - p(x) = x^\top [(A - BK)^\top P (A - BK) - P]\, x, and the DARE implies

\boxed{(A - BK)^\top P (A - BK) - P = -(Q + K^\top RK)}, \qquad \text{so}\qquad \boxed{p(x^+) - p(x) = -q(x, -Kx)}.

The terminal cost satisfies the required decrease with equality for the nominal LQR loop; the notebook's residual matrix is zero to 4 \times 10^{-14}. Exercise 5 proves the identity.

Input constraints restrict the LQR terminal region. The LQR law does not respect actuator bounds by itself. Require -0.5 \le -Kx \le 0.5, define \mathcal X_K = \{x \in \mathcal X : -0.5 \le -Kx \le 0.5\}, and compute the maximal positively invariant set of x^+ = (A - BK)x inside \mathcal X_K [5]. That set is the terminal set \mathcal X_f: for the notebook's R = 10, two fixed-point iterations and six vertices, (\pm 5, \mp 1.926), (\pm 5, \mp 0.673), (\pm 4.842, \mp 1.907).

Verifying the assumptions numerically. For a convex polytope and linear maps it suffices to check the vertices: each lies in \mathcal X, its LQR input is within the bounds, its successor lies in \mathcal X_f, and the terminal decrease residual is zero. The widget does this for any R.

Part 9

Receding-horizon simulation with stabilising terminal ingredients

The controller solves at every step

\min\ x_N^\top Px_N + \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} (x_k^\top Qx_k + u_k^\top Ru_k) \quad \text{s.t.}\quad x_{k+1} = Ax_k + Bu_k, \quad x_k \in \mathcal X, \quad u_k \in \mathcal U, \quad x_N \in \mathcal X_f,

with N = 5, applies the first input and re-solves. From x_0 = (4.5, 0.5) the first problem succeeds with first input -0.5 and predicted terminal state (2.359, -0.966), inside \mathcal X_f.

Use the full theoretical cost. The QP solver drops terms that are constant in the decision vector, which is legitimate for optimisation and wrong for a Lyapunov check. For diagnostics, reconstruct

J_N(x) = x_0^\top Qx_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{N-1} x_k^\top Qx_k + \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} u_k^\top Ru_k + x_N^\top Px_N,

the quantity in the proof. The notebook's loop runs 30 steps, reaching (-0.00005, 0.00001); that is the loop's cap of 30 iterations, not its stopping tolerance of 10^{-5}, which the state's norm of 5 \times 10^{-5} had not yet met. The input saturates at -0.5 for the first three steps and every predicted terminal state lies in \mathcal X_f.

The Lyapunov inequality, checked. The theorem predicts J_N^\star(x(t+1)) - J_N^\star(x(t)) + q(x(t), u(t)) \le 0. Along the notebook's trajectory the residual lies between -1.2 \times 10^{-9} and +1.2 \times 10^{-9}, which is its SLSQP solver's tolerance; the interior-point solver of this page gives residuals within 10^{-11}. The value falls from 125.38 to 102.38 in the first step, by exactly the first stage cost 23.00.

Why the inequality is nearly an equality here. The appended terminal controller is the infinite-horizon LQR and the Riccati cost satisfies p(x^+) - p(x) = -q(x, -Kx) exactly, so the terminal tail term is zero. And once no constraint is active, the MPC with this terminal cost is the LQR, whose value drops by exactly q at every step. The theorem requires only the inequality; the example happens to attain it.

Try it on the demo. Switch to "no terminal constraint, cost P only": from the notebook's start the loop still converges and the residual stays tiny, because the LQR region is reached quickly. Now start at (-4, 2.5), outside design B's region: the unconstrained design runs and converges, but its value rises on one step, which design B never allows. Then set N = 3 and start at (-5, 3): the unconstrained design is feasible for the first steps and then finds no plan at all, a loss of recursive feasibility; design B reports the start infeasible at once. A start such as (4, 4) is infeasible for every design, because the next position x_1 + x_2 = 8 violates the box whatever the input. The unconstrained-terminal design has the larger region, but no theorem stands behind it.

Part 10

What the terminal theorem does and does not say

It is a sufficient design mechanism. The assumptions provide a constructive route to recursive feasibility and stability. They are sufficient conditions, not a claim that every stable MPC must contain exactly these ingredients. MPC without a terminal constraint may have a larger actual region of attraction, but characterising it can be much harder.

The zero terminal set is simple but conservative. \mathcal X_f = \{0\} makes the proof almost immediate, and forces the finite horizon to do all the work of reaching the equilibrium exactly; for small N the certified region is much smaller than necessary.

Increasing the horizon is another lever. A longer horizon sees farther and enlarges the region, at more online cost. Terminal ingredients encode reliable information about the tail instead of forcing the horizon to optimise it explicitly.

Nominal versus robust. The feasibility proof uses the predicted successor x^+ = Ax + Bu as the actual one. If disturbances or model errors move the real system elsewhere, the shifted sequence may no longer be feasible. The guarantee here is a nominal MPC guarantee for the stated model and constraints; robust MPC needs constructions that account for uncertainty explicitly.

Nonlinear extension. The same problem can be posed for x_{k+1} = g(x_k, u_k) with the same terminal structure, and the logic survives: invariance gives the shifted feasible tail, terminal decrease gives the cost argument, and J_N^\star \le p on \mathcal X_f gives the upper bound when the origin is interior and p is continuous with p(0) = 0. The hard part is constructive: a useful nonlinear invariant set and terminal Lyapunov function are much harder to compute than in the linear-quadratic case, which is why the chapter keeps the full numerical construction linear.

Part 11

Common conceptual pitfalls

  1. "Feasible now means recursively feasible." No. A feasible plan can end in a state from which the next optimisation has no feasible continuation; the terminal construction exists to prevent this.
  2. "A long horizon automatically proves stability." No. It can improve behaviour and enlarge the region, but "large enough" is not a Lyapunov proof unless further conditions are established.
  3. "P from the DARE is enough by itself." Not under constraints. The terminal cost may decrease as required while the LQR action violates the actuator limits; hence the admissible invariant terminal region.
  4. "The terminal set must be the origin." No. The point of the general theorem is to replace x_N = 0 by a nontrivial invariant region.
  5. "Continuous- and discrete-time stability conditions are interchangeable." No. For x_{k+1} = A_{\mathrm{cl}} x_k the condition is \rho(A_{\mathrm{cl}}) < 1, not negative real parts.
  6. "The solver objective is the Lyapunov value." Not in software. QP implementations drop constants; for diagnostics, reconstruct the complete cost.
Part 12

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question before opening it.

1. Why is MPC a feedback controller if the optimisation computes an open-loop sequence?

Because the sequence is not executed. Only its first element is applied, the state is measured again and a new optimisation is solved; the first optimised action is an implicit state-feedback map.

2. Define recursive feasibility.

If the MPC problem is feasible at the current state and the MPC action is applied, the problem is also feasible at the next state. Repeated, this prevents future infeasibility under the nominal assumptions.

3. Why can a horizon-one MPC be unstable?

If the cost sees only the current state and input and carries no terminal information, the state term is constant in the input, and for an unstable plant the optimiser takes no corrective action because its effect lies outside the horizon.

4. How does the zero terminal constraint prove recursive feasibility?

Shift the old optimal sequence by one step and append zero. The old terminal state is the origin and zero input keeps it there, so the shifted sequence is feasible.

5. Why does the optimal cost decrease for xN = 0?

The shifted candidate at the next state costs exactly the old cost minus the first stage cost, and the new optimum can only be cheaper than that candidate.

6. State the three general terminal assumptions.

A positive-definite stage cost; a terminal set invariant under an admissible local controller; a terminal cost that decreases under that controller by at least the local stage cost.

7. What sequence is used in the general shifted-sequence proof?

(u_1^\star, \dots, u_{N-1}^\star, v(x_N)): the local input replaces the zero appended in the special case.

8. What are the roles of the terminal set and the terminal cost?

The set supplies a feasible infinite tail through an invariant local controller; the cost supplies a Lyapunov bound on the cost of that tail.

9. Why use the DARE solution as terminal cost for a linear-quadratic MPC?

With the LQR feedback it satisfies the exact decrease identity p(x^+) - p(x) = -q(x, -Kx), the terminal Lyapunov condition.

10. Why compute an invariant terminal set after computing the LQR?

LQR stabilises the unconstrained system, but its input may exceed the actuator bounds. The invariant terminal set restricts operation to a region where the state and the LQR action remain admissible indefinitely.

11. Why does a nonzero terminal region enlarge feasibility relative to xN = 0?

The horizon only has to reach a state from which the local controller can safely continue, rather than the exact equilibrium.

12. What is the discrete-time closed-loop matrix under u = −Kx?

A_{\mathrm{cl}} = A - BK, and asymptotic stability requires all its eigenvalues strictly inside the unit disk.

13. Why is the decrease inequality alone not a proof of asymptotic stability?

With the lower bound it gives convergence, but Lyapunov stability needs an upper bound near the origin so that small starts keep the value, and the state, small. With x_N = 0 this needs an extra assumption, local controllability with small inputs, because J_N^\star can be discontinuous at the origin; with a terminal set containing the origin in its interior it follows from J_N^\star \le p on \mathcal X_f.

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution; the code runs after the notebook's cells. Try first, then peek.

  1. Horizon-one pathology. For x^+ = 1.1x + u and q = x^2 + 5u^2, show that horizon-one MPC with no terminal cost chooses u = 0. What is the closed-loop eigenvalue?
    Hint

    Which terms of the objective depend on u_0?

    Solution

    The objective x_0^2 + 5u_0^2 contains u_0 only through 5u_0^2 \ge 0, minimised at u_0 = 0; x_1 never enters. The loop is x^+ = 1.1x, eigenvalue 1.1, unstable. Set a = 1.1, r = 5 in the Part 3 widget.

  2. One-step terminal cost. For the same system add p(x_1) = Px_1^2 and derive the optimal feedback u = -K(P)\,x.
    Hint

    Differentiate x^2 + 5u^2 + P(1.1x + u)^2 in u.

    Solution

    10u + 2P(1.1x + u) = 0 gives u = -\frac{1.1P}{5 + P}\,x, so K(P) = \frac{1.1P}{5 + P}. With the Riccati P = 3.4848, K = 0.4518 and the eigenvalue is 1.1 - 0.4518 = 0.6482. Any P > 0 lowers the eigenvalue, but the loop is stable only when K(P) > 0.1, that is P > 0.5; and only the Riccati P makes the one-step law optimal for the infinite horizon.

    K11, P11, e11 = dlqr(np.array([[1.1]]), np.array([[1.0]]), np.array([[1.0]]), np.array([[5.0]]))
print(P11.item(), K11.item(), 1.1 * P11.item() / (5.0 + P11.item()), e11.item())   # 3.4848 0.4518 0.4518 0.6482
  3. Zero-terminal shifted sequence. Write the predicted state sequence at time t and construct the candidate at t + 1. Where exactly is the equilibrium assumption used?
    Hint

    Follow the last predicted state one more step under the appended input.

    Solution

    At t: states x_{t|t} = x(t), x_{t+1|t}, \dots, x_{t+N|t} = 0 under u_{t|t}^\star, \dots, u_{t+N-1|t}^\star. Apply u(t) = u_{t|t}^\star, so nominally x(t+1) = x_{t+1|t}. Candidate at t+1: inputs (u_{t+1|t}^\star, \dots, u_{t+N-1|t}^\star, 0), states x_{t+1|t}, \dots, x_{t+N|t} = 0 and then x_{t+N+1} = A\cdot 0 + B\cdot 0. The equilibrium assumption A0 + B0 = 0 is used exactly there, to conclude x_{t+N+1} = 0, and 0 \in \mathcal U is needed for the appended input to be admissible. The intermediate states are feasible because they were feasible in the old plan.

  4. General-terminal cost proof. Derive J_N^\star(x_1) - J_N^\star(x_0) \le -q(x_0, u_0^\star) line by line, keeping the terminal terms.
    Hint

    Write the candidate's cost at x_1 with its own terminal term p(x_{N+1}).

    Solution
    \begin{aligned} J_N^\star(x_0) &= \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} q(x_i, u_i^\star) + p(x_N), \\ J_N^\star(x_1) &\le \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} q(x_i, u_i^\star) + q(x_N, v(x_N)) + p(x_{N+1}) \quad \text{(the candidate is feasible, optimality)}, \\ J_N^\star(x_1) - J_N^\star(x_0) &\le -q(x_0, u_0^\star) + \big[ q(x_N, v(x_N)) + p(x_{N+1}) - p(x_N) \big] \le -q(x_0, u_0^\star) \quad \text{(assumption 3)}. \end{aligned}

    The first inequality uses feasibility of the shifted candidate (assumption 2) and the definition of the minimum; the last uses the terminal decrease with x_{N+1} = Ax_N + Bv(x_N).

  5. DARE identity. From the DARE, prove (A - BK)^\top P(A - BK) - P = -(Q + K^\top RK).
    Hint

    Expand the left side and use B^\top PA = (R + B^\top PB)K.

    Solution

    Let S = R + B^\top PB, so K = S^{-1} B^\top PA and B^\top PA = SK. Expand: (A - BK)^\top P (A - BK) = A^\top PA - A^\top PBK - K^\top B^\top PA + K^\top B^\top PB K = A^\top PA - K^\top SK - K^\top SK + K^\top (S - R) K = A^\top PA - K^\top SK - K^\top RK. The DARE reads P = A^\top PA - A^\top PB S^{-1} B^\top PA + Q = A^\top PA - K^\top SK + Q. Subtracting, (A - BK)^\top P(A - BK) - P = -K^\top RK - Q. The notebook's residual is 4 \times 10^{-14}.

  6. Input-admissible LQR region. For |u| \le u_{\max} and u = -Kx, derive the two half-spaces to add to the state constraints.
    Hint

    Two linear inequalities in x.

    Solution

    -Kx \le u_{\max} and Kx \le u_{\max}: rows -K and K, both with right-hand side u_{\max}. Stacked under the box rows they define \mathcal X_K; for K = (0.2054, 0.7835) and u_{\max} = 0.5 they are the two sloped edges of the strip in the Part 7 widget.

  8. Terminal-set effect. Change R = 10 to a smaller and a larger value; recompute K, P, \mathcal X_f and the N = 5 region, and explain the trade-off.
    Hint

    Wrap the Part VII cells in a function of R.

    Solution

    R = 1: K = (0.422, 1.244), spectral radius 0.42, \mathcal X_f of area 5.6 (4 vertices), N = 5 region 31.8. R = 10: 12.8 and 34.5.     def terminal_design(Rval, N=5):

  9. Numerical Lyapunov inequality. For several feasible initial states, simulate the terminal MPC and compute J_N^\star(x(t+1)) - J_N^\star(x(t)) + q(x(t), u(t)). Investigate the effect of solver tolerance.
    Hint

    Solution

    From (4.5, 0.5), (-3, 2), (2, -2) and (0.5, 0.5) the largest residuals are 1.2 \times 10^{-9}, 6.3 \times 10^{-8}, 3.9 \times 10^{-9} and 1.4 \times 10^{-9}: all of the order of the SLSQP tolerance (10^{-8} in the course's solver, with the worst case under 10^{-7}), never genuinely positive.   10. Model mismatch thought experiment. Suppose the real plant is x^+ = (A + \Delta A)x + Bu while the optimiser uses A. Which step of the recursive-feasibility proof fails, and why does the nominal proof no longer suffice?
    Hint

    Find the one line of the proof that says "under the nominal model".

    Solution

    The step "x(1) = x_1", the claim that the measured next state equals the predicted one. With mismatch, x(1) = x_1 + \Delta A\, x(0), so the shifted candidate starts from a state it was not planned for: its intermediate states are no longer the old predictions, may leave \mathcal X, and its terminal state need not land in \mathcal X_f. Nothing in the nominal argument bounds the effect of \Delta A; feasibility at x(1) is then a hope, not a theorem. Robust MPC repairs this by planning for the whole set of states the mismatch can produce, for instance by tightening the constraints by a tube around the nominal prediction.

Part 14

The chapter on one page

  1. The law: solve the horizon-N problem at x(t), apply u_{t|t}^\star, measure, repeat; the plan is replaced, not executed.
  2. Four properties: feasibility, recursive feasibility, constraint satisfaction, stability; none implies another.
  3. Short horizons fail: with N = 1 and no terminal cost, u = 0 and x^+ = 1.2x; the Riccati cost restores the LQR law with eigenvalue 0.615.
  4. x_N = 0: the shifted sequence plus a zero proves recursive feasibility and J_N^\star(x_1) - J_N^\star(x_0) \le -q(x_0, u_0^\star); the upper bound needs local controllability.
  5. The horizon lever: zero-terminal areas 16.1, 34.9, 37.3 for N = 5, 10, 20, filling \mathcal C_\infty at N = 13.
  6. Terminal ingredients: invariant admissible \mathcal X_f gives feasibility, decreasing p gives the Lyapunov argument, and J_N^\star \le p on \mathcal X_f gives the upper bound.
  7. Linear-quadratic: (K, P) from the DARE with the exact identity, \mathcal X_f the maximal invariant set inside \mathcal X_K: six vertices, two iterations.
  8. The payoff: with N = 5, region area 16.1 for x_N = 0 and 34.5 for x_N \in \mathcal X_f; the loop from (4.5, 0.5) has Lyapunov residuals within 10^{-9}.
  9. The fine print: sufficient conditions, nominal model, no disturbances.

Scope. Nominal MPC for linear systems with polytopic constraints and a measured state: the receding-horizon law; feasibility, recursive feasibility, constraint satisfaction and stability told apart; the zero terminal constraint and the conditions under which its value function proves asymptotic stability; general terminal sets and costs; the linear-quadratic ingredients from the DARE and the maximal positively invariant set; and numerical checks of the decrease. An exact model and no disturbances are assumed; robust and output-feedback MPC are outside the scope, and the nonlinear case is discussed only qualitatively.

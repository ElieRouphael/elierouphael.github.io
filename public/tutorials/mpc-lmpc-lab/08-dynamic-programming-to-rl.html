Advanced MPC and Learning MPC · Chapter 08 of 09

From dynamic programming to reinforcement learning

Every controller in this course rests on one object: the optimal cost-to-go, the price of acting optimally from a state onward. Dynamic programming computes it for every state. MPC approximates it online at the current state, with a finite horizon and a terminal cost on a terminal set. Learning MPC learns that terminal cost from its own trajectories. Reinforcement learning estimates it, or its state-input version, from sampled transitions. This chapter puts the four side by side on problems small enough to solve exactly: an integer chain, the linear-quadratic case, and the double integrator restricted to a lattice that its own dynamics map into itself, so that every number below is exact and recomputed in your browser.

Part 1

Bellman's principle of optimality

The finite-horizon problem. For x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k) with x_k \in \mathcal X, u_k \in \mathcal U, x_N \in \mathcal X_f, an input sequence costs J(x_0; \mathbf u) = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} q(x_k, u_k) + V_f(x_N). Fold the constraints into the costs: \bar q = q on \mathcal X \times \mathcal U and +\infty elsewhere, \bar V_f = V_f on \mathcal X_f and +\infty elsewhere; a sequence is admissible exactly when its extended cost is finite. The tail problem at stage k optimises the remaining inputs from x_k = x, with value J_k^\star(x) and J_N^\star = \bar V_f. (Minima are assumed attained, automatic for finite input sets as in every computation here.)

Principle of optimality (Bellman)

If \mathbf u^\star is optimal from x_0, with finite cost and trajectory x_0^\star, \ldots, x_N^\star, then for every k the tail (u_k^\star, \ldots, u_{N-1}^\star) is optimal for the tail problem from x_k^\star.

Proof. If a tail from x_k^\star were strictly cheaper, splicing it onto the head (u_0^\star, \ldots, u_{k-1}^\star) would give an admissible sequence (the head still reaches x_k^\star, and each constraint involves a single stage) with strictly lower total cost. The proof uses an additive cost and a state that summarises the past. A constraint coupling all stages, such as \sum_k \|u_k\|^2 \le E, breaks it until the state is augmented with the remaining budget.

The DP recursion. Split the minimisation into the first input and the tail; the first stage cost does not depend on the tail inputs, and the tail depends on u_k only through x_{k+1}:

\boxed{J_N = \bar V_f, \qquad J_k(x) = \min_u \big\{ \bar q(x, u) + J_{k+1}\big( f(x, u) \big) \big\}, \quad k = N-1, \ldots, 0,}

and any minimiser \mu_k(x) is an optimal feedback law (by backward induction every sequence from x at stage k costs at least J_k(x), and \mu_k, \mu_{k+1}, \ldots attains it). If q is finite on \mathcal X \times \mathcal U, the domains follow \operatorname{dom} J_k = \mathcal X \cap \operatorname{Pre}(\operatorname{dom} J_{k+1}), \operatorname{dom} J_N = \mathcal X_f: chapter 02's recursion for the controllable set \mathcal K_{N-k}(\mathcal X_f), which is DP applied to functions with values in \{0, +\infty\}.

Reading the result. With N = 5 the notebook enumerates all 5^5 = 3125 input sequences from every initial state and finds that DP and brute force agree on every state, including the infeasible ones, with 525 stage-cost evaluations against 328 125: linear rather than exponential in N. The values at x = 0, \ldots, 10 are 0, 2, 7, 15, 27, 44, 67, 97, 135, 182, 240. DP used no linearity or convexity, only evaluations of f and q; its price is a table over all states. The ways out are structure (Part 2), optimisation at the current state only (MPC, Part 5), and estimation from samples (Part 7).

Part 2

The linear-quadratic case

Quadratic cost-to-go is preserved by the Bellman step. Let f = Ax + Bu, q = x^\top Q x + u^\top R u with Q \succeq 0, R \succ 0, V_f = x^\top P_f x, no constraints. If J_{k+1} = x^\top P_{k+1} x, completing the square in u gives \varphi(u) = (u + K_k x)^\top H_k (u + K_k x) + x^\top P_k x with H_k = R + B^\top P_{k+1} B \succ 0, K_k = H_k^{-1} B^\top P_{k+1} A and

\boxed{P_k = Q + A^\top P_{k+1} A - A^\top P_{k+1} B\, (R + B^\top P_{k+1} B)^{-1} B^\top P_{k+1} A.}

So \mu_k = -K_k x and J_k = x^\top P_k x: DP over functions collapses to the Riccati recursion over matrices. Evaluating \varphi at u = -Kx for an arbitrary gain gives the inequality (\star): P_k = Q + K_k^\top R K_k + (A - BK_k)^\top P_{k+1}(A - BK_k) \preceq Q + K^\top R K + (A - BK)^\top P_{k+1}(A - BK). With constraints this structure is lost (J_k becomes piecewise quadratic), one reason MPC optimises online for the current state only.

Why the recursion converges. Write \operatorname{Ric}(X) for the boxed map, so P_{N-j} = \operatorname{Ric}^j(P_f); let P be the stabilising DARE solution with gain K and A_{\mathrm{cl}} = A - BK, and K_X, A_X the gain and closed loop computed from X. Applying (\star) twice gives the sandwich

A_X^\top (X - P) A_X \;\preceq\; \operatorname{Ric}(X) - P \;\preceq\; A_{\mathrm{cl}}^\top (X - P) A_{\mathrm{cl}}.

If P_f \succeq P, every iterate stays above P and \|\operatorname{Ric}^j(P_f) - P\|_2 \le \|A_{\mathrm{cl}}^j\|_2^2\, \|P_f - P\|_2: the error decays at the rate \rho(A_{\mathrm{cl}})^2 per step. From P_f \preceq P the iterates rise towards P at the same asymptotic rate, since K_X \to K: the closed loop propagates the cost-to-go error. (Stabilisability of (A, B) and detectability of (Q^{1/2}, A) guarantee the limit from every P_f \succeq 0.)

The notebook runs the recursion from P_f \in \{0, Q, 10P\}: all three reach the DARE solution to about 10^{-15} after 25 steps, gains included, with mean rates per step of 0.114, 0.112 and 0.104 over j = 2, \ldots, 12 against \rho(A_{\mathrm{cl}})^2 = 0.109, and both sandwich inequalities hold to rounding error.

Part 3

Infinite horizon: the Bellman operator

The Bellman equation. Let f and q be stationary, \mathcal U(x) \ne \emptyset the admissible inputs, and discount by \gamma \in [0, 1). A stationary policy \mu costs J_\mu(x) = \sum_{k \ge 0} \gamma^k q(x_k, \mu(x_k)), and J^\star is the infimum over input sequences. The Bellman operators are

(TJ)(x) = \min_{u \in \mathcal U(x)} \big\{ q(x, u) + \gamma J\big( f(x, u) \big) \big\}, \qquad (T_\mu J)(x) = q\big( x, \mu(x) \big) + \gamma J\big( f(x, \mu(x)) \big).

Part 1 is J_k = T J_{k+1} with \gamma = 1, so value iteration J_{k+1} = TJ_k is DP with an ever longer horizon, and J^\star satisfies J^\star = TJ^\star. Assumptions: (A1) bounded costs |q| \le M; (A2) \gamma < 1; (A3) the minimum is attained. We work in the space \mathcal B of bounded functions with the sup-norm.

Why T is a γ-contraction

In plain words: applying one Bellman step to two guesses of the cost-to-go brings them closer by the factor \gamma, uniformly over all states. Everything else follows from that one fact.

More precisely: two properties suffice, monotonicity (J \le J' implies TJ \le TJ') and the constant shift T(J + c\mathbf 1) = TJ + \gamma c \mathbf 1. Under (A1) to (A3), T maps \mathcal B into itself and \|TJ - TJ'\|_\infty \le \gamma \|J - J'\|_\infty; the same holds for T_\mu. Proof. |(TJ)(x)| \le M + \gamma\|J\|_\infty. With c = \|J - J'\|_\infty we have J' - c\mathbf 1 \le J \le J' + c\mathbf 1, and monotonicity and the shift give TJ' - \gamma c\mathbf 1 \le TJ \le TJ' + \gamma c\mathbf 1. Boundedness makes J - J' meaningful (with +\infty constraint costs it is not), and \gamma < 1 is the contraction factor: for \gamma = 1 the proof only gives non-expansiveness, and fixed points can be non-unique.

Unique fixed point
By Banach, J^\star is the unique fixed point of T in \mathcal B, bounded by M / (1 - \gamma).
Convergence and stopping rule
\|J_k - J^\star\| \le \gamma^k \|J_0 - J^\star\| from any bounded start, and \|J_k - J^\star\| \le \frac{\gamma}{1 - \gamma}\|J_k - J_{k-1}\|.
Greedy policies
A greedy policy for J^\star is optimal; one greedy for an approximation \tilde J satisfies \|J_{\tilde\mu} - J^\star\| \le \frac{2\gamma}{1 - \gamma}\|\tilde J - J^\star\|. Good approximate costs-to-go, terminal costs or learned values, give good policies.

A finite test problem. Choose h with 1/h an integer and keep only v \in h\mathbb Z, p + v/2 \in h\mathbb Z, u \in h\mathbb Z within the bounds of chapter 02. Since x^+ = (p + v + u/2,\ v + u), v^+ \in h\mathbb Z and p^+ + v^+/2 = (p + v/2) + v + u \in h\mathbb Z: the lattice is mapped into itself exactly, so every lattice trajectory is a true trajectory of the double integrator and lattice results are exact statements about a restricted problem. Here h = 0.5, q is the LQ cost of Part 2, \gamma = 0.95, and to satisfy (A1) leaving the box costs c_{\mathrm{crash}} = 100 and ends in an absorbing, cost-free crash state, the usual RL encoding of a constraint.

Policy iteration. From an initial policy alternate evaluation, solving (I - \gamma P_{\mu_k}) J = q_{\mu_k} for J_{\mu_k} (P_\mu is the 0/1 closed-loop transition matrix, invertible because \|\gamma P_\mu\|_\infty = \gamma < 1), and improvement, \mu_{k+1}(x) \in \arg\min_u \{ q(x, u) + \gamma J_{\mu_k}(f(x, u)) \}, keeping \mu_k(x) on ties. Since T_{\mu_{k+1}} J_{\mu_k} = T J_{\mu_k} \le J_{\mu_k}, applying the monotone T_{\mu_{k+1}} repeatedly gives J_{\mu_{k+1}} \le J_{\mu_k}, strictly at some state whenever an action changes. No policy can repeat and there are finitely many, so the algorithm stops; then TJ_{\mu_k} = J_{\mu_k}, that is, J_{\mu_k} = J^\star. The table above shows the notebook's run from "coast": four improvement steps changing 140, 120, 46 and 6 actions, and a fifth evaluation equal to J^\star to rounding error, in a policy space of 5^{215} \approx 10^{150}. Each iteration costs a linear solve instead of a sweep, but very few are needed.

Part 4

The constrained double integrator

Hard constraints without discount. MPC problems are undiscounted with hard constraints, so the contraction argument fails twice. Take the lattice with h = 0.25, no crash state (inputs that leave the box are inadmissible), \gamma = 1, and iterate J_{k+1} = TJ_k from J_0 = 0. Monotonicity replaces contraction: since q \ge 0, J_1 \ge J_0, and by induction J_{k+1} \ge J_k, so J_k is the optimal k-step cost, a lower bound on J^\star. Finite convergence: off the origin q \ge x^\top Q x \ge c_{\min} > 0 on the lattice, so a finite-cost trajectory must reach the origin, and a k-step path that has not reached it costs at least k c_{\min}; hence J_k(x) = J^\star(x) once k > J^\star(x) / c_{\min}.

The landscape. The fixed point is reached after 10 iterations, far below the bound J^\star / c_{\min} \approx 2031, and the finite-cost set shrinks from 813 to 703, 661 and 655 states. J^\star is largest at large |p| and along the slanted edges of the finite-cost set, where the state moves away from the origin almost as fast as can still be braked. The policy is bang-bang except near the origin. Everywhere J^\star \ge x^\top P x, the unconstrained optimum; inside \mathcal O_\infty (117 states), where LQR solves the continuous constrained problem, the lattice value exceeds it by a median of 0.6 percent (maximum 16.7 percent): the price of rounding the inputs. The demo at the top of the page is this computation.

The domain of J^\star and the sets of chapter 02. Whatever finite values q takes, the Bellman step acts on domains as \operatorname{dom}(TJ) = \mathcal X \cap \operatorname{Pre}(\operatorname{dom} J). From J_0 = 0 the domains are therefore the outer iterates \mathcal C_{k+1} = \mathcal X \cap \operatorname{Pre}(\mathcal C_k), \mathcal C_0 = \mathcal X, that chapter 02 uses to compute the maximal control-invariant set; from J_0 = \bar V_f they are the controllable sets \mathcal K_k(\mathcal X_f). The counts agree at every iteration, 813, 703, 661, 655, 655, and since lattice inputs form a subset of [-1, 1] each lattice domain lies inside the polytope, so the sets agree state for state: the finite-cost states are exactly the lattice points of \mathcal C_\infty, and the set recursions of chapter 02 are the \{0, \infty\} shadow of value iteration. The grey states of the demo satisfy the constraints now but are doomed to leave \mathcal X within three steps, which DP reports as infinite cost.

The curse of dimensionality. A table with n_g points per axis in n dimensions holds n_g^{\,n} values, and each sweep evaluates all of them against every input. At about 30 points per axis (25 to 33 here), n = 2 needs 900 values, n = 4 810 000, n = 6 7.29 \times 10^{8} (5.83 GB per table), and the 12 states of a rigid body in space 5.31 \times 10^{17}. Two escapes structure the rest of the chapter: do not tabulate, but optimise only at the current state over a finite horizon with an approximate tail (MPC); or do not grid, but fit a parametric approximator to samples (RL).

Part 5

MPC is truncated dynamic programming

The MPC problem at state x is the finite-horizon problem of Part 1, so

J_N(x) = \big( T^N \bar V_f \big)(x), \qquad \kappa_N(x) \in \arg\min_u \big\{ \bar q(x, u) + \big( T^{N-1} \bar V_f \big)\big( f(x, u) \big) \big\},

with T the undiscounted constrained operator of Part 4. MPC is the greedy policy with respect to T^{N-1}\bar V_f, evaluated only at the current state by solving an optimisation over N inputs; its feasible set is \mathcal K_N(\mathcal X_f).

What a good terminal cost buys

An exact tail gives exact MPC. If V_f = J^\star on \mathcal X_f, then \bar V_f \ge J^\star and monotonicity gives T^N\bar V_f \ge T^N J^\star = J^\star, with equality wherever an optimal trajectory enters \mathcal X_f within N steps: the horizon only has to reach \mathcal X_f.

Terminal decrease gives a monotone value and stability. The terminal assumptions of chapter 03 (for every x \in \mathcal X_f an admissible u with f(x, u) \in \mathcal X_f and V_f(f(x, u)) - V_f(x) \le -q(x, u)) say exactly \bar V_f \ge T\bar V_f. Applying T^N gives J_N \ge J_{N+1}, and with x^+ = f(x, \kappa_N(x)),

J_N(x) = q\big( x, \kappa_N(x) \big) + J_{N-1}(x^+) \;\ge\; q\big( x, \kappa_N(x) \big) + J_N(x^+).

So J_N is a Lyapunov function, x^+ \in \operatorname{dom} J_{N-1} \subseteq \operatorname{dom} J_N gives recursive feasibility, and the closed-loop cost is at most J_N(x_0). Without terminal ingredients the tail is underestimated: with V_f = 0 and \mathcal X_f = \mathcal X, T^N 0 \le J^\star, the feasible set \mathcal C_N contains doomed states for small N, and the decrease is unavailable. With discounting the effect is quantitative: the greedy policy of T^{N-1} V_f is within \frac{2\gamma^N}{1 - \gamma}\|V_f - J^\star\|_\infty of optimal.

Three designs on the lattice. On the lattice the MPC law is a table, so the receding-horizon closed loop can be run from every feasible state. Design A: V_f = 0, \mathcal X_f = \mathcal X. Design B: V_f = x^\top P x on \mathcal X_f = \mathcal O_\infty. Design C: V_f = J^\star on the same set. A closed loop fails if the MPC problem becomes infeasible or the origin is not reached within 80 steps.

Part 6

LMPC learns an upper bound of the value

Chapter 05 stores the realised cost-to-go J_t^i = \sum_{k \ge t} q(x^i[k], u^i[k]) of every state visited in a successful iteration i. The visited states form the sampled safe set SS^j, and V^j(x) = \min\{J_t^i : x^i[t] = x\} is the terminal cost on \mathcal X_f = SS^j. Two facts:

  1. Realised costs are upper bounds. J_t^i is the cost of an admissible trajectory to the target, so V^j \ge J^\star on SS^j. Data approach J^\star from above; value iteration from J_0 = 0 approaches it from below.
  2. The data satisfy the terminal decrease. If V^j(x) = J_t^i, the stored successor x^i[t+1] \in SS^j gives V^j(x) = q(x, u^i[t]) + J_{t+1}^i \ge q(x, u^i[t]) + V^j(x^i[t+1]) \ge (T\bar V^j)(x).

The decrease fact of Part 5 then gives recursive feasibility and a closed-loop cost at most J_N(x_S) \le V^j(x_S), the previous iteration's cost: iteration costs never increase, chapter 05's main result in operator form. On the lattice, LMPC is greedy with respect to T^{N-1}\bar V^j; a sluggish feasible trajectory from x_S = (-4, 0) (DP for an energy-dominated cost) seeds the iterations.

The notebook's seed costs 66.456, 92.6 percent above J^\star(x_S) = 34.500. With N = 1 nothing beats replaying the seed; with N = 2 the cost stalls at 38.369, 11.2 percent above; with N = 3 the first iteration is already optimal, and the smallest stored J_t^j - J^\star(x^j[t]) over all runs is exactly zero. On this lattice, with its sampled (not convexified) safe set, the horizon decides how much of the data is usable.

The Q-function.

Q^\star(x, u) = q(x, u) + \gamma J^\star\big( f(x, u) \big), \qquad J^\star(x) = \min_u Q^\star(x, u), \qquad \mu^\star(x) \in \arg\min_u Q^\star(x, u).

With Q^\star in hand, acting optimally needs no model, only a minimisation over inputs. Substituting J^\star = \min_{u'} Q^\star(\cdot, u') gives the Bellman equation for Q, Q^\star(x, u) = q(x, u) + \gamma \min_{u'} Q^\star(f(x, u), u') =: (FQ^\star)(x, u), and F is a \gamma-contraction by the proof of Part 3.

Tabular Q-learning. After observing a transition (x, u, c, x^+) of the real system,

\boxed{Q(x, u) \leftarrow Q(x, u) + \alpha \Big[ c + \gamma \min_{u'} Q(x^+, u') - Q(x, u) \Big].}

The bracket is the temporal-difference error; its target is a sample of (FQ)(x, u), exact for deterministic transitions, and just c after a crash. Convergence (Watkins and Dayan [4], Tsitsiklis [5]): with finite sets, bounded costs and \gamma < 1, if every pair is updated infinitely often with step sizes satisfying \sum \alpha_t = \infty, \sum \alpha_t^2 < \infty, then Q \to Q^\star with probability one, as a noisy asynchronous iteration of F. Exploration is thus an assumption, met by \varepsilon-greedy inputs or by optimistic initialisation (Q_0 = 0 lies below every Q^\star here, so untried inputs look attractive). Learning is off-policy: the limit is Q^\star whatever policy generated the data.

On the lattice. The learner faces the problem of Part 3 but only sees transitions. Episodes start at random states, a luxury that real systems rarely allow, and end after 30 steps or at a crash. The score is the fraction of the 171 viable states at which the greedy input is optimal. The page runs the five configurations with its own random stream; the notebook's runs are drawn dotted for comparison.

The notebook's runs. With \alpha = 1 and \varepsilon = 0.1 the greedy policy is optimal everywhere after 40 000 transitions and 972 crashes; value iteration needed 6 sweeps (6 450 backups) and no crash. The decaying \alpha = n^{-0.6} needs 68 000 transitions; the constant \alpha = 0.1 is still at 93 percent after 200 000. With \varepsilon = 0 the optimistic Q_0 = 0 succeeds after 52 000 transitions, while the pessimistic Q_0 = 200 fails with 70 217 crashes, a score stuck at 0.327 and a greedy input that crashes from 29 viable states. After the first run, Q = Q^\star at the optimal input of every viable state, while other pairs are off by up to 95.0: accurate only where it matters.

Part 8

Function approximation, DQN, and the common pitfalls

DQN (Mnih et al. [7]) replaces the table by a network Q_\theta over a finite input set and minimises the squared temporal-difference error

\mathcal L(\theta) = \mathbb E_{(x, u, c, x^+) \sim \mathcal D} \Big[ \big( c + \gamma \min_{u'} Q_{\theta^-}(x^+, u') - Q_\theta(x, u) \big)^2 \Big].

The replay buffer \mathcal D decorrelates the samples and reuses costly data; the target network \theta^-, refreshed only occasionally, makes each phase a regression towards a fixed target. Why the contraction argument no longer applies: with exact regressions DQN would iterate Q_{k+1} = \Pi F Q_k, where \Pi projects onto the network class in a norm weighted by the replay distribution. F contracts in the sup-norm, but \Pi is non-expansive only in its weighted L^2 norm, so \Pi F need not contract in any norm; with bootstrapping and off-policy data, divergence is possible (Tsitsiklis and Van Roy [6]). The minimum over noisy estimates is also biased low, which double Q-learning corrects. Part 3 says what is approximated, not that the approximation converges. Compared with MPC and LMPC, DQN learns a global Q and meets constraints only through penalties, by violating them; MPC keeps the model, enforces constraints online, and needs only a local tail approximation, which LMPC learns from its own data as a guaranteed upper bound.

Common pitfalls. The contraction proof covers MPC: no, it needs bounded costs and \gamma < 1; MPC is undiscounted with infinite constraint costs, and rests on monotonicity and the terminal decrease. A long horizon without terminal ingredients is a safe default: T^N 0 underestimates J^\star and its domain contains doomed states (48 at N = 1, 6 at N = 2); it works eventually, but nothing certifies when. A terminal cost valid for the model is valid for the implementation: x^\top P x on \mathcal O_\infty is exact for the continuous LQ problem, but restricting the inputs broke its decrease at 114 of 117 states. Finite cost and control invariance are the same: \operatorname{dom} J^\star \subseteq \mathcal C_\infty, but equality also needs every viable state to reach the target; for x^+ = 2x + u with integer |x| \le 10 and |u| \le 3, the state 3 can stay put but never goes lower, viable yet infinitely costly. LMPC's terminal cost is a Q-function: it is a state function, an upper bound of J^\star built from realised costs, not a state-input function learned by bootstrapping. A penalty is a constraint: it leaves the optimal policy unchanged only if large enough, and a learner respects it only after violating it, 972 crashes before the policy became optimal.

Part 9

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question before opening it.

1. State the principle of optimality. What does its proof use?

The tail of an optimal input sequence is optimal for the tail problem from the state it reaches. The proof splices a cheaper tail onto the optimal head; it needs an additive cost, a state that summarises the past, and constraints that act stage by stage.

2. Why is the controllable-set recursion of chapter 02 a special case of DP?

With constraints as infinite costs, the Bellman step maps a function with domain \mathcal S to one with domain \mathcal X \cap \operatorname{Pre}(\mathcal S). For functions with values in \{0, +\infty\}, the DP recursion is the set recursion.

3. In the LQ problem, why does a matrix recursion suffice, and what sets its convergence rate?

Minimising a strictly convex quadratic in u leaves a quadratic in x, so J_k = x^\top P_k x for all k. The sandwich inequality shows that the closed loop propagates the error, at the asymptotic rate \rho(A_{\mathrm{cl}})^2 per step.

4. Which assumptions make the Bellman operator a contraction, and where do they enter?

Bounded costs keep TJ bounded and \|J - J'\|_\infty finite. The factor \gamma < 1 enters through the constant shift T(J + c\mathbf 1) = TJ + \gamma c\mathbf 1, which together with monotonicity is the whole proof. With \gamma = 1, T is only non-expansive.

5. Why does policy iteration terminate on a finite problem?

Each improvement gives J_{\mu_{k+1}} \le J_{\mu_k}, strictly at some state whenever an action changes, so no policy repeats and there are finitely many. When nothing changes, TJ_{\mu_k} = J_{\mu_k}, hence J_{\mu_k} = J^\star.

6. Describe MPC in DP terms. Why does V_f = x^\top P x on \mathcal O_\infty allow a short horizon?

MPC is greedy with respect to T^{N-1}\bar V_f, evaluated at the current state. If V_f = J^\star on \mathcal X_f, as x^\top P x is on \mathcal O_\infty for the constrained LQ problem, then T^N\bar V_f = J^\star wherever an optimal trajectory enters \mathcal X_f within N steps. The decrease \bar V_f \ge T\bar V_f then gives recursive feasibility and a Lyapunov function.

7. Why are LMPC's stored costs-to-go upper bounds of J^\star, and why does that matter?

Each is the realised cost of an admissible trajectory to the target, so it is at least optimal. Each also comes with a stored feasible continuation, so \bar V^j \ge T\bar V^j, which yields recursive feasibility and non-increasing iteration costs. A lower bound such as T^N 0 gives neither.

8. What does a DQN target network do, and why does the tabular convergence proof not carry over?

It freezes the parameters used in the targets, so each phase is a regression towards a fixed target. With function approximation the update is \Pi F, and since \Pi is non-expansive only in a weighted L^2 norm, \Pi F need not contract and can diverge.

  2. LQ: the Riccati recursion, converging at the rate \rho(A_{\mathrm{cl}})^2; starting at P it never moves.
  3. Contraction: bounded costs and \gamma < 1 make T a sup-norm contraction; value iteration converges at the tight rate \gamma^k, policy iteration stops after a few improvements (4 here).
  4. Constrained: monotone, finitely convergent value iteration reproduces chapter 02's sets state for state; tabulation breaks down within a handful of dimensions.
  5. MPC: greedy with respect to T^{N-1}\bar V_f; \bar V_f \ge T\bar V_f is its stability proof. The set buys safety, the cost buys short-horizon performance.
  6. LMPC: realised costs-to-go are upper bounds of J^\star that satisfy the decrease automatically; iteration costs never increase.
  7. Q-learning: samples and exploration instead of a model, 40 000 transitions and 972 crashes against 6 450 backups and none.
  8. DQN: a projected contraction need not contract; no general guarantee.

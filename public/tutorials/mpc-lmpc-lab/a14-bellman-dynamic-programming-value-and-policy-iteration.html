Bellman: dynamic programming, value and policy iteration

Every controller in this course rests on one object: the optimal cost-to-go, the price of acting optimally from a state onward. Dynamic programming computes it for every state at once, by one backward recursion that Bellman wrote down in 1957. This chapter proves the principle behind that recursion, applies it to an integer chain small enough to check by brute force, shows it collapse to the Riccati equation in the linear-quadratic case, and turns it into an operator whose contraction gives value iteration and policy iteration their guarantees. Below, the course's double integrator is restricted to a lattice that its own dynamics map into itself, so that every cost-to-go on the page is exact.

Part 1

Bellman's principle of optimality

The finite-horizon problem. For x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k) with x_k \in \mathcal X, u_k \in \mathcal U and x_N \in \mathcal X_f, an input sequence costs J(x_0; \mathbf u) = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} q(x_k, u_k) + V_f(x_N), the problem of chapter A5. Fold the constraints into the costs: \bar q = q on \mathcal X \times \mathcal U and +\infty elsewhere, \bar V_f = V_f on \mathcal X_f and +\infty elsewhere. A sequence is admissible exactly when its extended cost is finite. The tail problem at stage k optimises the remaining inputs from x_k = x, with value J_k^\star(x) and J_N^\star = \bar V_f. Minima are assumed attained, which is automatic for the finite input sets used in every computation here.

The principle of optimality

In plain words: whatever the first moves of an optimal plan were, the rest of the plan is itself optimal from wherever it has got to. A route that is the fastest from A to C through B contains the fastest route from B to C.

More precisely: if \mathbf u^\star is optimal from x_0 with finite cost and trajectory x_0^\star, \ldots, x_N^\star, then for every k the tail (u_k^\star, \ldots, u_{N-1}^\star) is optimal for the tail problem from x_k^\star. Proof. If a tail from x_k^\star were strictly cheaper, splicing it onto the head (u_0^\star, \ldots, u_{k-1}^\star) would give an admissible sequence (the head still reaches x_k^\star, and each constraint involves a single stage) with strictly lower total cost, contradicting optimality.

Additive cost
The proof needs the total to be a sum of stage costs, so that a cheaper tail lowers the total.
A state that summarises the past
The head and the new tail meet at x_k^\star, and nothing before it matters to the tail.
Stage-wise constraints
A constraint coupling all stages, such as \sum_k \|u_k\|^2 \le E, breaks the splice until the state is augmented with the remaining budget.

The recursion. Split the minimisation into the first input and the tail. The first stage cost does not depend on the tail inputs, and the tail depends on u_k only through x_{k+1}, so

\boxed{J_N = \bar V_f, \qquad J_k(x) = \min_u \big\{ \bar q(x, u) + J_{k+1}\big( f(x, u) \big) \big\}, \quad k = N-1, \ldots, 0,}

and any minimiser \mu_k(x) is an optimal feedback law: by backward induction every sequence from x at stage k costs at least J_k(x), and \mu_k, \mu_{k+1}, \ldots attains it. The recursion uses no linearity and no convexity, only evaluations of f and q. Its price is a table over all states.

The domains are the controllable sets. If q is finite on \mathcal X \times \mathcal U, the states of finite cost obey \operatorname{dom} J_k = \mathcal X \cap \operatorname{Pre}(\operatorname{dom} J_{k+1}) with \operatorname{dom} J_N = \mathcal X_f: this is chapter A9's recursion for the controllable set \mathcal K_{N-k}(\mathcal X_f). Set computations are dynamic programming applied to functions with values in \{0, +\infty\}.

An integer chain, checked by brute force. Positions x \in \{-10, \ldots, 10\}, moves u \in \{-2, \ldots, 2\} with x^+ = x + u, cost x^2 + u^2, horizon N = 5 and the terminal constraint x_N = 0. Enumerating all 5^5 = 3125 input sequences from every initial state and comparing with the recursion, the two agree on every state, including the infeasible ones, with 525 stage-cost evaluations against 328 125: linear rather than exponential in N. The values at x = 0, \ldots, 10 are 0, 2, 7, 15, 27, 44, 67, 97, 135, 182, 240, and the domains shrink as the constraint propagates backwards, |x| \le 2(N - k). From x = 2 the optimal move is -1 at stages 0 to 3 and -2 at stage 4: the policy is time varying, saving the big move for the last step, because arriving early and waiting at the origin would pay u^2 later.

Part 2

The linear-quadratic case

A quadratic cost-to-go is preserved by the Bellman step. Let f = Ax + Bu, q = x^\top Q x + u^\top R u with Q \succeq 0, R \succ 0, V_f = x^\top P_f x and no constraints. If J_{k+1} = x^\top P_{k+1} x, completing the square in u gives

\varphi(u) = (u + K_k x)^\top H_k (u + K_k x) + x^\top P_k x, \qquad H_k = R + B^\top P_{k+1} B \succ 0, \quad K_k = H_k^{-1} B^\top P_{k+1} A,

with

\boxed{P_k = Q + A^\top P_{k+1} A - A^\top P_{k+1} B\, (R + B^\top P_{k+1} B)^{-1} B^\top P_{k+1} A.}

So \mu_k = -K_k x and J_k = x^\top P_k x: dynamic programming over functions collapses to the Riccati recursion over matrices, whose fixed point is the DARE of chapter A7. Evaluating \varphi at u = -Kx for any other gain gives the inequality (\star): P_k \preceq Q + K^\top R K + (A - BK)^\top P_{k+1}(A - BK), with equality at K = K_k. With constraints this structure is lost, J_k becomes piecewise quadratic, which is one reason MPC optimises online for the current state only (chapter A6).

Why the recursion converges. Write \operatorname{Ric}(X) for the boxed map, so that P_{N-j} = \operatorname{Ric}^j(P_f); let P be the stabilising DARE solution with gain K and A_{\mathrm{cl}} = A - BK, and K_X, A_X the gain and closed loop computed from X. Applying (\star) twice gives the sandwich

A_X^\top (X - P) A_X \;\preceq\; \operatorname{Ric}(X) - P \;\preceq\; A_{\mathrm{cl}}^\top (X - P) A_{\mathrm{cl}}.

If P_f \succeq P, every iterate stays above P and \|\operatorname{Ric}^j(P_f) - P\|_2 \le \|A_{\mathrm{cl}}^j\|_2^2\, \|P_f - P\|_2: the closed loop propagates the cost-to-go error, at the asymptotic rate \rho(A_{\mathrm{cl}})^2 per step. For the canonical system \rho(A_{\mathrm{cl}}) = 0.3308 and \rho^2 = 0.1094; from P_f \in \{0, Q, 10P\} the recursion reaches the DARE solution to about 10^{-15} in 25 steps, gains included, with mean rates per step of 0.114, 0.112 and 0.104 over j = 2, \ldots, 12. Starting at P_f = P it never moves, which is why an unconstrained MPC with the Riccati terminal weight is the LQR at every horizon.

Part 3

Infinite horizon: the Bellman operator

The Bellman equation. Let f and q be stationary, \mathcal U(x) \ne \emptyset the admissible inputs at x, and discount by \gamma \in [0, 1). A stationary policy \mu costs J_\mu(x) = \sum_{k \ge 0} \gamma^k q(x_k, \mu(x_k)), and J^\star is the infimum over input sequences. The Bellman operators are

(TJ)(x) = \min_{u \in \mathcal U(x)} \big\{ q(x, u) + \gamma J\big( f(x, u) \big) \big\}, \qquad (T_\mu J)(x) = q\big( x, \mu(x) \big) + \gamma J\big( f(x, \mu(x)) \big).

Part 1 is J_k = T J_{k+1} with \gamma = 1, so value iteration J_{k+1} = TJ_k is dynamic programming with an ever longer horizon, and J^\star satisfies J^\star = TJ^\star. Assumptions: (A1) bounded costs, |q| \le M; (A2) \gamma < 1; (A3) the minimum is attained. We work in the space \mathcal B of bounded functions with the sup-norm.

Why T is a γ-contraction

In plain words: applying one Bellman step to two guesses of the cost-to-go brings them closer by the factor \gamma, uniformly over all states. Everything else in the chapter follows from that one fact.

More precisely: two properties suffice. Monotonicity: J \le J' implies TJ \le TJ'. The constant shift: T(J + c\mathbf 1) = TJ + \gamma c\mathbf 1. Under (A1) to (A3), T maps \mathcal B into itself and \|TJ - TJ'\|_\infty \le \gamma \|J - J'\|_\infty; the same holds for T_\mu. Proof. |(TJ)(x)| \le M + \gamma\|J\|_\infty. With c = \|J - J'\|_\infty we have J' - c\mathbf 1 \le J \le J' + c\mathbf 1, and monotonicity and the shift give TJ' - \gamma c\mathbf 1 \le TJ \le TJ' + \gamma c\mathbf 1. Boundedness makes J - J' meaningful (with +\infty constraint costs it is not), and \gamma < 1 is the contraction factor: for \gamma = 1 the proof only gives non-expansiveness, and fixed points can be non-unique.

Unique fixed point
By Banach, J^\star is the unique fixed point of T in \mathcal B, bounded by M / (1 - \gamma).
Convergence and stopping rule
\|J_k - J^\star\| \le \gamma^k \|J_0 - J^\star\| from any bounded start, and \|J_k - J^\star\| \le \frac{\gamma}{1 - \gamma}\|J_k - J_{k-1}\|: a computable certificate.
Greedy policies
A greedy policy for J^\star is optimal; one greedy for an approximation \tilde J satisfies \|J_{\tilde\mu} - J^\star\| \le \frac{2\gamma}{1 - \gamma}\|\tilde J - J^\star\|. Good approximate costs-to-go, terminal costs or learned values, give good policies.
Part 4

A finite test problem: the lattice

A grid the dynamics map into itself. Choose h with 1/h an integer and keep only the states with v \in h\mathbb Z and p + v/2 \in h\mathbb Z, and the inputs u \in h\mathbb Z, within the canonical bounds. Since x^+ = (p + v + u/2,\ v + u), the successor has v^+ \in h\mathbb Z and p^+ + v^+/2 = (p + v/2) + v + u \in h\mathbb Z: the lattice is mapped into itself exactly, so every lattice trajectory is a true trajectory of the double integrator and every lattice result is an exact statement about a restricted problem. With h = 0.5 there are 215 states and 5 inputs; the numbers script checks that the successor table equals Ax + Bu to the last bit and is empty exactly where Ax + Bu leaves the box.

The discounted crash problem. To satisfy (A1), leaving the box costs c_{\mathrm{crash}} = 100 and ends in an absorbing, cost-free crash state, the usual reinforcement-learning encoding of a constraint, with \gamma = 0.95 and the canonical stage cost. Value iteration from J_0 = 0 certifies J^\star after 11 iterations by the stopping rule \frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\|J_{k+1} - J_k\| < 10^{-12}. Of the 215 states, 171 are viable, some input sequence keeps them in the box for ever; there J^\star \le 47.06, elsewhere J^\star \ge 101.85, and the optimal policy keeps every viable state viable, so the penalty does not alter the constrained behaviour on this problem. From three starts, J_0 = 0, J_0 = 2000 and a random J_0 in [0, 2000], the error stays within the bound \gamma^k \|J_0 - J^\star\| (largest ratio 0.895, 1.000 and 0.553), and the greedy policy is optimal from step 6, 6 and 8, long before the values are exact (step 10 from zero, never within 150 steps from the other two): a constant offset in J does not change the minimiser, which is why policies converge before values. The bound is tight from the constant 2000, because at the absorbing, cost-free origin T acts as J \mapsto \gamma J.

Part 5

Policy iteration

From an initial policy, alternate two steps. Evaluation: solve (I - \gamma P_{\mu_k}) J = q_{\mu_k} for J_{\mu_k}, where P_\mu is the 0/1 closed-loop transition matrix, invertible because \|\gamma P_\mu\|_\infty = \gamma < 1. Improvement: \mu_{k+1}(x) \in \arg\min_u \{ q(x, u) + \gamma J_{\mu_k}(f(x, u)) \}, keeping \mu_k(x) on ties.

Why it terminates. Since T_{\mu_{k+1}} J_{\mu_k} = T J_{\mu_k} \le J_{\mu_k}, applying the monotone T_{\mu_{k+1}} repeatedly gives J_{\mu_{k+1}} \le J_{\mu_k}, strictly at some state whenever an action changes. No policy can repeat and there are finitely many, so the algorithm stops; then TJ_{\mu_k} = J_{\mu_k}, that is, J_{\mu_k} = J^\star. The table in the widget above shows the run from "coast", u = 0 everywhere: four improvement steps changing 140, 120, 46 and 6 actions, and a fifth evaluation equal to J^\star to rounding error, 1.4 \times 10^{-14}, in a policy space of 5^{215} \approx 10^{150}. Each iteration costs a linear solve instead of a sweep, but very few are needed.

Part 6

The constrained double integrator, without discount

Hard constraints and no discount break the contraction twice. MPC problems are undiscounted with hard constraints, so (A1) and (A2) both fail. Take the lattice with h = 0.25, 813 states and 9 inputs, no crash state (inputs that leave the box are simply inadmissible), \gamma = 1, and iterate J_{k+1} = TJ_k from J_0 = 0. Monotonicity replaces contraction: since q \ge 0, J_1 \ge J_0, and by induction J_{k+1} \ge J_k, so J_k is the optimal k-step cost, a lower bound on J^\star. Finite convergence: off the origin q \ge x^\top Q x \ge c_{\min} = 0.0281 on the lattice, so a finite-cost trajectory must reach the origin, and a k-step path that has not reached it costs at least k c_{\min}; hence J_k(x) = J^\star(x) once k > J^\star(x) / c_{\min}, which is at most 2031 here.

The landscape. The exact fixed point is reached after 10 iterations, and the finite-cost set shrinks from 813 to 703, 661 and 655 states. J^\star is largest at large |p| and along the slanted edges of the finite-cost set, where the state moves away from the origin almost as fast as can still be braked; the largest finite value is 57.12. The policy is at the input bound on 476 of the 655 viable states, bang-bang except near the origin, and from (-4, 0) the optimal trajectory costs 34.500 and reaches the origin in 4 steps. Everywhere J^\star \ge x^\top P x, the unconstrained optimum of Part 2; inside \mathcal O_\infty (117 lattice states), where the LQR solves the continuous constrained problem, the lattice value exceeds it by a median of 0.6 percent (mean 1.9, maximum 16.7): the price of rounding the inputs to the lattice. The hero demo is this computation.

The domain of J^\star and the sets of chapters A9 and A10. Whatever finite values q takes, the Bellman step acts on domains as \operatorname{dom}(TJ) = \mathcal X \cap \operatorname{Pre}(\operatorname{dom} J). From J_0 = 0 the domains are therefore the outer iterates \mathcal C_{k+1} = \mathcal X \cap \operatorname{Pre}(\mathcal C_k), \mathcal C_0 = \mathcal X, that chapter A10 uses to compute the maximal control-invariant set; from J_0 = \bar V_f they are the controllable sets \mathcal K_k(\mathcal X_f). The counts agree at every iteration, 813, 703, 661, 655, 655, and since the lattice inputs form a subset of [-1, 1] each lattice domain lies inside the polytope, so the sets agree state for state: the finite-cost states are exactly the lattice points of \mathcal C_\infty, and the set recursions are the \{0, \infty\} shadow of value iteration. The grey states of the demo satisfy the constraints now but are doomed to leave \mathcal X: 110 at the next step, 42 after one more, 6 after two.

The curse of dimensionality. A table with n_g points per axis in n dimensions holds n_g^{\,n} values, and each sweep evaluates all of them against every input. At about 30 points per axis, the resolution of this chapter, a cart in a plane with four states already needs nearly a million, a six-state model a table of six gigabytes, and the twelve states of a rigid body in space more values than there are atoms in a grain of sand. Two escapes structure the rest of the course: do not tabulate, but optimise only at the current state over a finite horizon with an approximate tail, which is MPC, or do not grid, but fit a parametric approximator to samples, which is reinforcement learning. Lab B5 puts the two side by side on this very lattice.

Part 7

The code behind it

The course package contains no dynamic programming, since every controller in it optimises online; the lattice routines live in the course's browser toolkit, interactive/mpc-dp.js, and the numbers script repeats them in NumPy. They are short enough to read whole. The Bellman operator is one loop over states and one over inputs:

function bellman(Pb, J) {
  const TJ = new Array(Pb.n + 1), act = new Array(Pb.n + 1);
  for (let i = 0; i <= Pb.n; i++) {
    let best = Infinity, ba = 0;
    for (let a = 0; a < Pb.m; a++) {
      const v = Pb.cost[i][a] + Pb.gamma * J[Pb.succ[i][a]];
      if (v < best) { best = v; ba = a; }
    }
    TJ[i] = best; act[i] = ba;
  }
  return { TJ, act };
}

Pb.succ[i][a] is the successor index of state i under input a, and Pb.cost[i][a] the stage cost with the crash penalty folded in; the crash state is index n, with successors all equal to itself and zero cost, which is what "absorbing and cost-free" means in a table. The operator returns the greedy action with the value, so a policy is read off the same sweep. Value iteration applies it until the a-posteriori stopping rule of Part 3 holds:

function valueIteration(Pb, J0, tol = 1e-12, maxIter = 5000) {
  let J = J0.slice(), k;
  for (k = 1; k < maxIter; k++) {
    const { TJ } = bellman(Pb, J);
    let d = 0; for (let i = 0; i <= Pb.n; i++) d = Math.max(d, Math.abs(TJ[i] - J[i]));
    if (Pb.gamma / (1 - Pb.gamma) * d < tol) { J = TJ; break; }
    J = TJ;
  }
  return { J, iterations: k };
}

The test gamma / (1 - gamma) * d < tol is the certificate, not a heuristic: when it fires, the last iterate is within tol of J^\star in the sup-norm. Policy iteration alternates an exact evaluation, a linear solve of (I - \gamma P_\mu) J = q_\mu by the toolkit's Gaussian elimination, with a greedy improvement that keeps the old action on ties, the detail that makes the termination proof work:

function policyIteration(Pb, mu0, Jstar) {
  let mu = mu0.slice(); const trace = [];
  for (let k = 0; k < 50; k++) {
    const Jmu = evaluatePolicy(Pb, mu);
    const Qsa = Pb.cost.map((r, i) => r.map((c, a) => c + Pb.gamma * Jmu[Pb.succ[i][a]]));
    const muNew = mu.map((a, i) => { const best = Math.min(...Qsa[i]);
      return Qsa[i][a] <= best + 1e-10 ? a : Qsa[i].indexOf(best); });
    const changed = muNew.filter((a, i) => a !== mu[i]).length;
    trace.push({ k, changed, Jmu });
    if (!changed) break;
    mu = muNew;
  }
  return trace;
}

The linear-quadratic case needs no table at all. The package's dlqr is the Riccati fixed point of Part 2 computed directly:

advanced_mpc_lmpc/linear.py · dlqr

def dlqr(A: np.ndarray, B: np.ndarray, Q: np.ndarray, R: np.ndarray):
    """Discrete-time infinite-horizon LQR with convention u = -Kx."""
    P = solve_discrete_are(A, B, Q, R)
    K = np.linalg.solve(R + B.T @ P @ B, B.T @ P @ A)
    eig = np.linalg.eigvals(A - B @ K)
    return K, P, eig

SciPy's solve_discrete_are returns the stabilising solution P of the DARE, the limit of the Riccati recursion from any P_f \succeq 0 under stabilisability and detectability. The gain is then one linear solve, K = (R + B^\top P B)^{-1} B^\top P A, the sign convention being u = -Kx, and the eigenvalues of A - BK are returned so that the caller can check \rho < 1. The browser toolkit computes the same P by iterating the recursion until it stops changing, which Part 2 showed takes about 25 steps here.

Part 8

Common pitfalls

  1. "The contraction proof covers MPC." No. It needs bounded costs and \gamma < 1; MPC is undiscounted with infinite constraint costs, and rests on monotonicity and the terminal decrease of chapter A11 instead.
  2. "Value iteration has converged when the policy stops changing." The policy is optimal from step 6 on the lattice while the values are still off by tens of units. A policy certificate and a value certificate are different things; the stopping rule bounds the value.
  3. "A finite cost and control invariance are the same." \operatorname{dom} J^\star \subseteq \mathcal C_\infty always, but equality also needs every viable state to reach the target: for x^+ = 2x + u with integer |x| \le 10 and |u| \le 3, the state 3 can stay put for ever but never go lower, viable yet infinitely costly.
  4. "A penalty is a constraint." The crash cost leaves the optimal policy unchanged only if it is large enough, and a learner respects it only after violating it. Lab B5 counts the crashes.
  5. "Dynamic programming is impractical, so it does not matter." Every terminal cost in the course approximates a cost-to-go, every learned value in Learning MPC is a sampled cost-to-go, and every Q-function is one. The table is impractical; the object is everywhere.
Part 9

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question before opening it.

1. State the principle of optimality. What does its proof use?

The tail of an optimal input sequence is optimal for the tail problem from the state it reaches. The proof splices a cheaper tail onto the optimal head; it needs an additive cost, a state that summarises the past, and constraints that act stage by stage.

2. Why is the controllable-set recursion of chapter A9 a special case of dynamic programming?

With constraints as infinite costs, the Bellman step maps a function with domain \mathcal S to one with domain \mathcal X \cap \operatorname{Pre}(\mathcal S). For functions with values in \{0, +\infty\}, the recursion on values is the recursion on sets.

3. In the linear-quadratic problem, why does a matrix recursion suffice, and what sets its convergence rate?

Minimising a strictly convex quadratic in u leaves a quadratic in x, so J_k = x^\top P_k x for all k. The sandwich inequality shows that the closed loop propagates the error, at the asymptotic rate \rho(A_{\mathrm{cl}})^2 per step, 0.109 on the canonical system.

4. Which assumptions make the Bellman operator a contraction, and where do they enter?

Bounded costs keep TJ bounded and \|J - J'\|_\infty finite. The factor \gamma < 1 enters through the constant shift T(J + c\mathbf 1) = TJ + \gamma c\mathbf 1, which together with monotonicity is the whole proof. With \gamma = 1, T is only non-expansive.

5. Why does policy iteration terminate on a finite problem?

Each improvement gives J_{\mu_{k+1}} \le J_{\mu_k}, strictly at some state whenever an action changes, so no policy repeats and there are finitely many. When nothing changes, TJ_{\mu_k} = J_{\mu_k}, hence J_{\mu_k} = J^\star.

6. Why does undiscounted value iteration with hard constraints still converge, and in finitely many steps?

Monotonicity: from J_0 = 0 the iterates increase and each is the optimal k-step cost, a lower bound on J^\star. A positive stage cost off the origin forces finite-cost trajectories to reach the origin, so J_k = J^\star once k exceeds J^\star / c_{\min}; on the lattice the fixed point arrives at k = 10, far below the bound of 2031.

7. What does the lattice make exact that a grid would not?

The dynamics map the lattice into itself, so successors are lattice states and no interpolation is needed. Every lattice trajectory is a true trajectory of the double integrator, and every lattice value is the exact optimum of the restricted problem.

8. What is the curse of dimensionality, and what are the two ways out?

A table over n_g points per axis in n dimensions has n_g^{\,n} entries and each sweep visits them all. Either optimise online from the current state over a finite horizon with an approximate tail (MPC), or approximate the cost-to-go from samples with a parametric model (reinforcement learning).

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution; the code runs with the course package installed, after the numbers script's definitions. Try first, then peek.

  1. The chain by hand. For the integer chain with N = 2, compute J_1 and J_0 at x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 by hand and read off \mu_0 and \mu_1.

    Hint

    J_2 is 0 at the origin and +\infty elsewhere, so J_1(x) is the cost of the one move that reaches the origin, if it exists.

    Solution

    J_1(x) = x^2 + x^2 = 2x^2 for |x| \le 2 (the move u = -x), infinite beyond: 0, 2, 8, \infty, \infty. Then J_0(x) = \min_u \{x^2 + u^2 + J_1(x + u)\}: J_0(0) = 0 with u = 0; J_0(1) = 1 + \min(0 + 2, 1 + 0, 1 + 8, 4 + 2) = 2 with u = -1; J_0(2) = 4 + \min(1 + 2, 4 + 0, 0 + 8) = 7 with u = -1; J_0(3) = 9 + \min(4 + 2, 1 + 8) = 15 with u = -2; J_0(4) = 16 + (4 + 8) = 28 with u = -2. Set N = 2 in the chain widget to check.

  2. The sandwich inequality. Prove A_X^\top (X - P) A_X \preceq \operatorname{Ric}(X) - P \preceq A_{\mathrm{cl}}^\top (X - P) A_{\mathrm{cl}} from (\star), and deduce the bound on \|\operatorname{Ric}^j(P_f) - P\|_2 for P_f \succeq P.

    Hint

    Write \operatorname{Ric}(X) = Q + K_X^\top R K_X + A_X^\top X A_X and P = Q + K^\top R K + A_{\mathrm{cl}}^\top P A_{\mathrm{cl}}, then apply (\star) once with each gain in the other's recursion.

    Solution

    (\star) at the matrix X with the gain K gives \operatorname{Ric}(X) \preceq Q + K^\top R K + A_{\mathrm{cl}}^\top X A_{\mathrm{cl}}; subtracting the DARE identity for P gives the right inequality. (\star) at the matrix P with the gain K_X gives P = \operatorname{Ric}(P) \preceq Q + K_X^\top R K_X + A_X^\top P A_X; subtracting it from the exact expression of \operatorname{Ric}(X) gives the left one. If X \succeq P the left side is \succeq 0, so the iterates stay above P, and the right side bounds the 2-norm by \|A_{\mathrm{cl}}\|_2^2 \|X - P\|_2; iterating j times and keeping the product of the closed loops gives \|A_{\mathrm{cl}}^j\|_2^2 \|P_f - P\|_2. The numbers script checks both inequalities from P_f = 10P for j = 1, \ldots, 10: the smallest eigenvalue of each difference is non-negative to rounding, 2.4 \times 10^{-3} and 6 \times 10^{-17} relative to the error's norm.

  3. Build value iteration yourself. Write the discounted Bellman operator for the crash problem on the h = 0.5 lattice in NumPy, run value iteration with the stopping rule, and compare with the numbers script: 11 iterations, 171 viable states, largest viable value 47.06.

    Hint

    With succ_c the successor table (crash state last) and cost_c the costs, one sweep is (cost_c + gamma * J[succ_c]).min(axis=1).

    Solution 
    def bellman(J):
    Qsa = cost_c + gamma * J[succ_c]          # q(x,u) + gamma J(f(x,u)) for every (state, input)
    return Qsa.min(axis=1), Qsa.argmin(axis=1)

J = np.zeros(n3 + 1)
for k in range(1, 5000):
    J_new, _ = bellman(J)
    if gamma / (1 - gamma) * np.max(np.abs(J_new - J)) < 1e-12:
        break
    J = J_new
print(k, J_new[:n3][viable3].max())           # 11, 47.06

    The fancy index J[succ_c] gathers the successor values for all 216 by 5 pairs at once, so a sweep is three vectorised lines. The crash row of succ_c points to itself and its cost row is zero, which makes the crash state absorbing and free.

  4. Build policy iteration yourself. Implement the evaluation step as a linear solve and the improvement step with ties kept, start from the coasting policy, and reproduce the action-change counts 140, 120, 46, 6, 0.

    Hint

    P_\mu has a single 1 per row, at the successor under \mu; evaluation is np.linalg.solve(np.eye(n) - gamma * P_mu, q_mu).

    Solution 
    def evaluate(mu):
    rows = np.arange(n3 + 1)
    P_mu = np.zeros((n3 + 1, n3 + 1)); P_mu[rows, succ_c[rows, mu]] = 1.0
    return np.linalg.solve(np.eye(n3 + 1) - gamma * P_mu, cost_c[rows, mu])

mu = np.full(n3 + 1, m3 // 2)                 # u = 0 everywhere
for k in range(50):
    J_mu = evaluate(mu)
    Qsa = cost_c + gamma * J_mu[succ_c]
    keep = Qsa[np.arange(n3 + 1), mu] <= Qsa.min(axis=1) + 1e-10
    mu_new = np.where(keep, mu, Qsa.argmin(axis=1))
    print(k, int(np.sum(mu_new != mu)))       # 140, 120, 46, 6, 0
    if not np.any(mu_new != mu): break
    mu = mu_new

    Without keep, an action tied with the best could flip back and forth and the termination argument would not apply; with it, the evaluated cost strictly decreases whenever anything changes.

  5. The domains against the polytopes. Count the lattice states inside each \mathcal C_k of chapter A10 and compare with the finite-cost states of each value-iteration step. Why do they agree exactly, and why is that not a coincidence of this grid?

    Hint

    maximal_control_invariant returns the history of iterates; inside(poly, S4) tests all lattice states at once.

    Solution

    Both sequences are 813, 703, 661, 655, 655. The Bellman step acts on domains as \mathcal X \cap \operatorname{Pre}(\cdot) whatever the finite values, so the k-th domain is the k-th iterate of the set recursion, restricted to the lattice. Because the lattice inputs are a subset of the interval [-1, 1], a lattice state with a finite value has a lattice input into the previous domain, which is in particular an admissible input into the previous polytope, so the lattice domain lies inside the polytope; and here the converse holds too, since a lattice state inside a polytope iterate has a lattice input that stays inside (checked: no lattice state of \mathcal C_\infty has infinite cost). The second half is the part that could fail on a coarser lattice, where the admissible interval of inputs might contain no lattice point.

  6. A viable state of infinite cost. For x^+ = 2x + u with integer |x| \le 10, |u| \le 3, cost x^2 + u^2 and no discount, show that x = 3 is viable but J^\star(3) = \infty, and find the largest state from which the origin is reachable.

    Hint

    From 3 the move u = -3 stays at 3; which moves go lower?

    Solution

    From x = 3 the successors are 6 + u \in \{3, \ldots, 9\}: the state can stay at 3 for ever with u = -3 (viable), but never go below 3, so the origin is unreachable and every trajectory pays at least 9 per step for ever: J^\star(3) = \infty. The origin is reachable only from |x| \le 1: from x = 1 the move u = -2 reaches 0, from x = 2 the best successor is 4 - 3 = 1, and from x = 2 one reaches 1 and then 0, so the reachable set is |x| \le 2 (J^\star(2) = 4 + 9 + 1 + 4 = 18); from |x| \ge 3 the dynamics outrun the input. Viability and finite cost part ways exactly where the input can hold but not win.

Part 11

The chapter on one page

  1. The principle: the tail of an optimal plan is optimal; the proof splices a cheaper tail onto the head and needs an additive cost, a summarising state and stage-wise constraints.
  2. The recursion: J_k = \min_u\{\bar q + J_{k+1}(f)\}, J_N = \bar V_f, for any model and cost; its domains are chapter A9's controllable sets. The chain: 525 evaluations against 328 125, same answer.
  3. Linear-quadratic: the Riccati recursion, converging at the rate \rho(A_{\mathrm{cl}})^2 = 0.109; starting at P it never moves.
  4. Contraction: bounded costs and \gamma < 1 make T a sup-norm contraction; value iteration converges at the rate \gamma^k with a computable stopping rule, and the greedy policy is optimal long before the values are exact.
  5. Policy iteration: evaluate, improve keeping ties, stop; 4 improvement steps on the lattice against 10^{150} policies.
  6. Constrained, undiscounted: monotone, finitely convergent value iteration, fixed point at k = 10; 655 viable states, exactly the lattice points of \mathcal C_\infty; the LQR cost is a lower bound, exceeded by 0.6 percent in the median inside \mathcal O_\infty.
  7. The curse: n_g^{\,n} table entries; the ways out are MPC and learning, side by side in lab B5.

The vocabulary, in one table

Term Meaning here
Cost-to-go J^\star Cost of acting optimally from a state onward.
Tail problem The remaining optimisation from stage k.
Extended cost \bar q, \bar V_f The stage and terminal costs with +\infty outside the constraints.
Bellman operator T One backward step: minimise stage cost plus discounted next value.
Contraction |TJ - TJ'| \le \gamma|J - J'|; needs bounded costs and \gamma < 1.
Value iteration Repeated application of T; dynamic programming with an ever longer horizon.
Policy iteration Alternate exact evaluation and greedy improvement.
Lattice A grid the dynamics map into itself exactly; results are exact for the restricted problem.
Crash state An absorbing, cost-free state that a penalty sends constraint violations to.
Viable, doomed Can stay in the box for ever; must leave it within a few steps.

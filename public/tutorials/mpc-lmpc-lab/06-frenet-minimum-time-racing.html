Frenet coordinates and minimum-time racing

A raceline is not the centre of the road driven as fast as possible. It is the solution of an optimal-control problem: stay inside the track, obey the vehicle and its tires, and minimise lap time. That problem is written most naturally in Frenet coordinates, with progress along the track as the independent variable instead of time. This chapter derives the geometry carefully, the progress rate, the spatial dynamics and the full nonlinear raceline problem for a small RC car, and then solves a reduced piece of it exactly: the minimum-time speed profile along a fixed centreline, which needs nothing but a forward pass for acceleration and a backward pass for braking. The planner runs live below; change the grip, the brakes, the engine and the grid.

Part 1

The racing problem and the geometry of the centreline

Raceline against tracking. Racing control is usually split in two. Offline: find a minimum-time trajectory that satisfies the vehicle, tire and track constraints. Online: track it with MPC or Learning MPC, reacting to deviations. This chapter is about the offline viewpoint and its geometry; chapters 07 and later use the same Frenet concepts inside data-driven prediction and LMPC.

Why Cartesian coordinates are inconvenient. With the position p = (X, Y), "remain inside the road" is a geometric relation between the vehicle and two curved boundaries. If the road is parameterised by arc length s instead, the same constraint is a box on one number:

\boxed{-W(s) \le e_y(s) \le W(s).}

That is the main reason Frenet coordinates are so useful for racing and path following.

What is the Frenet frame of a centreline?

In plain words: at every point of the road's centre curve, a forward direction and a sideways direction, plus a number saying how fast the forward direction is turning.

More precisely: let \gamma(s) = (X_c(s), Y_c(s)) be the centreline with s its arc length, so \|\gamma'(s)\| = 1. The unit tangent is t(s) = \gamma'(s); for a parameter that is only approximately arc length, t = \gamma' / \|\gamma'\|. The left normal is

\boxed{n = \begin{pmatrix} -t_y \\ t_x \end{pmatrix}}, \qquad t^\top n = 0, \quad \|n\| = 1,

and the signed curvature of a planar curve with a generic parameter is

\boxed{K(s) = \frac{X'(s) Y''(s) - Y'(s) X''(s)}{\big( X'(s)^2 + Y'(s)^2 \big)^{3/2}}.}

With exact arc length the Frenet–Serret relations are t' = K n and n' = -K t. The sign of K is the turning direction: positive for a left turn, negative for a right turn (this track is driven counter-clockwise, so its corners are left turns and its two kinks right turns).

Radius of curvature
|R| = 1 / |K| where K \ne 0; large |K| is a tight corner.
Normal convention
Some codes use the right normal (t_y, -t_x) and subtract e_y times it. The two sign changes cancel; in both conventions e_y > 0 is to the left of travel. The course code returns the left normal.
Part 2

The synthetic track and the Frenet position map

Why a synthetic track. Real track maps are measured centreline points fitted by a periodic spline. Here a smooth closed curve with a closed-form expression is used instead,

X_c(\theta) = 45\cos\theta + 5\cos 2\theta, \qquad Y_c(\theta) = 30\sin\theta + 6\sin 3\theta, \qquad \theta \in [0, 2\pi),

driven counter-clockwise from (50, 0). Heading and curvature come from the exact derivatives of the curve; the arc length is integrated numerically on a dense parameter grid, and the centreline is then sampled uniformly in arc length. The track has two tight corners, two sweeping bends and two slight reverse kinks on the long sides; the half-width is W = 3 m.

The position map. A point at arc length s and lateral offset e_y is

\boxed{p = \gamma(s) + e_y\, n(s)}:

the centreline is e_y = 0, the road edges are e_y = \pm W(s), and the two outer curves of the track figure are \gamma \pm W n. A vehicle at (s, e_y) lies on the normal line through \gamma(s).

Differentiating the map. With p(t) = \gamma(s(t)) + e_y(t)\, n(s(t)),

\dot p = \gamma'(s)\dot s + \dot e_y\, n + e_y\, n'(s)\dot s = t\dot s + n\dot e_y - K e_y\, t\dot s,

using \gamma' = t and n' = -Kt. Hence

\boxed{\dot p = (1 - K e_y)\,\dot s\, t + \dot e_y\, n.}

This is where the geometric factor 1 - K e_y comes from: a point on the inside of a bend travels less than the centreline for the same progress ds, a point on the outside travels more.

The singularity. The map is locally singular where 1 - K(s) e_y = 0, that is, when the lateral offset reaches the local centre of curvature: beyond it, different (s, e_y) pairs give the same point. A Frenet corridor must stay away from that condition; a simple conservative check is 1 - |K(s)| W(s) > 0 everywhere, and on this track its minimum is 0.612. That is a geometric check, not a dynamic safety guarantee.

Part 3

Velocity in the Frenet frame, and from time to space

Heading error. With the vehicle yaw \psi and the centreline tangent angle \psi_c(s), define e_\psi = \psi - \psi_c(s); zero means aligned with the local tangent. In the body frame the vehicle has longitudinal velocity v_x and lateral velocity v_y. Relative to the track's tangent and normal,

\boxed{v_t = v_x\cos e_\psi - v_y\sin e_\psi}, \qquad \boxed{v_n = v_x\sin e_\psi + v_y\cos e_\psi.}

The progress rate. Equating the tangent component of \dot p with v_t and the normal component with v_n:

\boxed{\dot s = \frac{v_x\cos e_\psi - v_y\sin e_\psi}{1 - K(s)e_y}}, \qquad \boxed{\dot e_y = v_x\sin e_\psi + v_y\cos e_\psi.}

The first is the central equation of Frenet-frame racing models: it converts body-frame velocity into progress along the track. With e_\psi = 0 and v_y = 0 the lateral motion stops, as it should for perfect alignment. The centreline tangent rotates with curvature, d\psi_c/ds = K, so \dot\psi_c = K\dot s, and with the yaw rate r = \dot\psi,

\boxed{\dot e_\psi = r - K(s)\,\dot s.}

From time to space

If a state component obeys \dot\xi = f_\xi(x, u) and \dot s > 0, then s can serve as the independent variable:

\boxed{\frac{d\xi}{ds} = \frac{\dot\xi}{\dot s}.}

This chain rule is the whole transformation behind spatial raceline formulations. It needs \dot s > 0: at \dot s = 0 the quotient is undefined, and \dot s < 0 means driving backwards relative to the track's orientation. Dividing the three rates above by \dot s gives the spatial Frenet kinematics,

\frac{de_y}{ds} = \frac{(1 - Ke_y)(v_x\sin e_\psi + v_y\cos e_\psi)}{v_x\cos e_\psi - v_y\sin e_\psi}, \qquad \frac{de_\psi}{ds} = \frac{r}{\dot s} - K(s), \qquad \frac{dt}{ds} = \frac{1}{\dot s}.

Time as a state. Once s is the independent variable, elapsed time becomes a state with t'(s) = 1/\dot s, and the lap time is simply T_{\mathrm{lap}} = t(s_f) - t(0). Minimising lap time is minimising a terminal state.

Part 4

The dynamic bicycle model and the full raceline problem

The six-state spatial vector. The full spatial raceline problem uses x(s) = [v_x, v_y, r, e_\psi, t, e_y]^\top with the inputs a_x (longitudinal acceleration) and \delta (steering angle). The order of the two inputs is only a convention: raceline formulations often write [a_x, \delta], the course code stores u = [\delta, a]. Use the names, not the indices.

The vehicle. Chapter 07 simulates a 1:10-scale RC car with the parameters of the BARC platform: mass m = 1.98 kg, l_f = l_r = 0.125 m from the centre of gravity to each axle, yaw inertia I_z = 0.024 kg m². Its slip angles are

\boxed{\alpha_f = \delta - \operatorname{atan2}(v_y + l_f r,\ v_x)}, \qquad \boxed{\alpha_r = -\operatorname{atan2}(v_y - l_r r,\ v_x)},

and each axle's lateral force follows the simplified Pacejka "magic formula" F_y = D\sin(C\arctan(B\alpha)) with stiffness factor B = 1, shape factor C = 1.25 and peak force D = 0.8\, mg/2 = 7.77 N: each axle carries half the weight (because l_f = l_r) and can deliver 0.8 times its normal load. With these forces,

\boxed{\dot v_x = a_x - \frac{F_{y,f}}{m}\sin\delta + r v_y}, \qquad \boxed{\dot v_y = \frac{F_{y,f}\cos\delta + F_{y,r}}{m} - r v_x}, \qquad \boxed{\dot r = \frac{l_f F_{y,f}\cos\delta - l_r F_{y,r}}{I_z},}

and the spatial derivatives are v_x' = \dot v_x / \dot s, v_y' = \dot v_y / \dot s, r' = \dot r / \dot s, together with the three kinematic rates of Part 3. The course's spatial right-hand side evaluates all six.

The full raceline CFTOC. Minimise t(s_f) subject to the spatial vehicle dynamics, tire-force constraints, the track boundaries -W(s) \le e_y(s) \le W(s), initial conditions and terminal or periodic conditions. It is a nonlinear optimal-control problem: trigonometric Frenet geometry, the denominator 1 - K e_y, nonlinear tire laws, steering-dependent force projections, the minimum-time term t' = 1/\dot s, possibly a combined tire-force constraint. It is usually solved by direct transcription: discretise the spatial dynamics on a grid in s (with Pyomo, say) and hand the nonlinear program to a solver such as IPOPT. Perantoni and Limebeer [1] solve a problem of this type for a Formula One car. This chapter does not solve it; it derives it, and solves the reduced problem below instead.

Part 5

The reduced problem: speed planning in squared speed

Assumptions. To isolate the minimum-time spatial reasoning in an executable problem, assume e_y = 0, e_\psi = 0, v_y = 0, and write v_x = v. Then \dot s = v and only the speed along the fixed centreline is optimised. Gone from the decision problem: lateral offset, steering, yaw dynamics, tire slip, combined force allocation. Left: curvature, the lateral speed limit, acceleration and braking, total time.

Squared speed. In time, \dot v = a and \dot s = v, so dv/ds = a/v, with an inconvenient division by v. Define z = v^2. Then dz/ds = 2v\, dv/ds and

\boxed{\frac{dz}{ds} = 2a}:

nonlinear speed dynamics in v become affine dynamics in z. For a segment of length \Delta s_i with piecewise-constant acceleration a_i,

\boxed{z_{i+1} = z_i + 2\Delta s_i a_i}, \qquad \boxed{a_i = \frac{z_{i+1} - z_i}{2\Delta s_i}},

so acceleration constraints become linear difference inequalities in z.

The curvature cap. Along a path of curvature K the lateral acceleration is a_{\mathrm{lat}} = v^2 K. Imposing the reduced friction condition |a_{\mathrm{lat}}| \le \mu g gives v^2|K| \le \mu g, and with an independent top speed,

\boxed{z_{\max}(s) = \min\Big( v_{\max}^2,\ \frac{\mu g}{|K(s)|} \Big).}

On a straight only the top-speed limit remains. With \mu = 1 the tightest corner caps the speed at \sqrt{9.81 / 0.12943} = 8.706 m/s. The local cap answers only "how fast could the vehicle go here if its acceleration history were ignored?"; it does not say whether it can accelerate or brake quickly enough to move between neighbouring caps. On this track the chosen v_{\max} = 35 m/s is never reached: the planned top speed is 20.9 m/s, set by the corners and the acceleration limit, so the top-speed constraint is never active (the demo confirms this for any setting you choose).

Part 6

Segment time, the two envelopes, and why the naive profile fails

Exact segment time. Since dt = ds/v, the time across segment i is \int_{s_i}^{s_{i+1}} ds/v(s). Under constant acceleration z is linear in s and the integral is exact: from v_{i+1} - v_i = a_i\Delta t_i and v_{i+1}^2 - v_i^2 = (v_{i+1} - v_i)(v_{i+1} + v_i) = 2a_i\Delta s_i,

\boxed{\Delta t_i = \frac{2\Delta s_i}{v_i + v_{i+1}}.}

Check: from 10 to 14 m/s over 30 m the acceleration is (196 - 100)/60 = 1.6 m/s², the time from v_1 = v_0 + a\Delta t is 2.5 s, and the formula gives 60/24 = 2.5 s. It is not an arbitrary trapezoidal rule: for the piecewise-constant-acceleration model it is exact. The lap time on a periodic grid is

\boxed{T = \sum_i \frac{2\Delta s_i}{\sqrt{z_i} + \sqrt{z_{i+1}}}},

with the index wrapping at the finish line. Every term decreases when a feasible z increases. That monotonicity is why the reduced optimum is the largest feasible speed profile.

Forward envelope. From a_i \le a_{\max}:

\boxed{z_{i+1} \le z_i + 2\Delta s_i a_{\max}},

an upper bound that a forward pass propagates around the track. Backward envelope. From a_i \ge a_{\min} with a_{\min} < 0:

\boxed{z_i \le z_{i+1} - 2\Delta s_i a_{\min}},

the largest speed one may have before the next point and still brake down to it. A tight corner requires a small z_{i+1}; this inequality limits z_i, which limits z_{i-1}, and so on upstream. That is the mathematics behind "brake before the corner, not after entering it".

The naive profile. Setting z_i = z_{\max, i} respects the lateral limit at every point but implies absurd longitudinal accelerations between neighbouring samples: on the 240-sample track the implied a ranges from -204.8 to +204.8 m/s², and 142 of 240 segments violate the bounds [-6, 3]. (The extreme values come from the straights, where the cap jumps to v_{\max}^2 within one sample.) The longitudinal dynamics must smooth the cap through forward and backward reachability.

Part 7

The closed-lap forward/backward planner

  1. Initialise with the local cap, z_i^{(0)} = z_{\max, i}.
  2. Forward pass: z_{i+1} \leftarrow \min(z_{i+1},\ z_i + 2\Delta s_i a_{\max}) around the loop.
  3. Backward pass: z_i \leftarrow \min(z_i,\ z_{i+1} - 2\Delta s_i a_{\min}) around the loop.
  4. Repeat until a sweep changes nothing (to 10^{-9}). Indices are circular; the code reports the number of sweeps.

The algorithm only ever decreases values, so it converges to the greatest fixed point satisfying all the upper-envelope constraints. Why that is minimum-time: every segment time 2\Delta s_i / (\sqrt{z_i} + \sqrt{z_{i+1}}) is strictly decreasing in each endpoint, so a componentwise larger feasible profile cannot be slower, and the envelope algorithm returns the largest profile compatible with the caps, the forward and the backward constraints. Hence it is the time-minimising profile for this reduced decoupled model.

Reading the speed figure. The upper dashed curve of the demo is the speed curvature alone would permit; the planned curve lies below it for two different reasons: before a tight corner braking must start early, after it the finite acceleration lets the speed recover only gradually. Where the two curves coincide the local cap is active. Around the tightest corner the optimiser is not reacting to the curvature at the current position: the backward envelope has propagated the corner's requirement upstream, a deterministic form of preview. Part 8

Friction, authority, and the spatial grid

Friction. The cap is z \le \mu g / |K|, so less grip lowers the speed in every curved region, changes how much speed must be shed before corners and how much can be carried through them. The notebook's lap times are 20.1416, 18.3589, 17.0939 and 16.1251 s for \mu = 0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.2. Under this reduced model more grip cannot make the optimum worse: a larger \mu only enlarges the feasible set, the same inclusion argument as chapter 04's friction maps.

Acceleration against braking authority. Braking authority decides how late braking may begin before a slow corner; acceleration authority decides how quickly speed is regained after it. They shape different parts of the lap: weaker braking (a_{\min} = -3.5) costs 0.64 s and pushes the braking zones upstream; weaker acceleration (a_{\max} = 1.5) costs 1.65 s and slows every corner exit. In a full vehicle model both are further coupled to tire loading, steering, power and aerodynamics.

Grid convergence. The continuous problem lives on s \in [0, s_f]; a planner uses finitely many nodes. A grid that is too coarse misses curvature peaks, approximates braking zones poorly and biases the lap time. With 60, 120, 240 and 480 samples the notebook obtains 16.9383, 17.0721, 17.0939 and 17.1062 s: the lap time increases with the grid because a coarse grid steps over the apex, underestimates the peak curvature, allows a higher corner speed and so returns an optimistic time. The increments shrink; convergence need not be monotone in general, since both the curvature representation and the piecewise dynamics change with the grid. The demo also computes 960 samples, where the increment has fallen below 0.002 s; extrapolating the tail (exercise 11) puts the limit near 17.110 s, so the 240-sample lap time is about 0.016 s optimistic.

Part 9

Tire-force coupling, and why a full raceline is faster

Separate limits are optimistic. The planner imposes |a_x| \le a_{x,\max} and, separately, |a_y| = v^2|K| \le \mu g. A physical tire has a limited combined force. The simplest approximation is the friction circle,

\boxed{a_x^2 + a_y^2 \le (\mu g)^2}, \qquad \text{with } a_y = zK:\quad \boxed{a_x^2 + (zK)^2 \le (\mu g)^2.}

The utilisation \sqrt{a_x^2 + a_y^2} / (\mu g) of the planner's solution is a diagnostic: a value of one is the boundary of the circle, a value above one means the reduced model allowed a combination that the friction circle forbids. For the notebook's solution the maximum is 1.1708 and 66 of the 240 segments exceed one. This is not a numerical error in the planner; it is why full raceline formulations impose tire-force constraints rather than an independent curvature cap.

The centreline is not the minimum-time path. The planner fixes e_y = 0. The full problem optimises e_y(s) inside [-W, W]: approach a corner on the outside, cut to the inside, exit to the outside. That lowers the curvature the vehicle actually experiences and lets it carry more speed, so

\text{centreline minimum-time speed planning} \;\text{is not}\; \text{full minimum-time raceline optimisation}.

Everything that makes the full problem nonlinear was listed in Part 4; that is why it is solved as a nonlinear program rather than a QP.

Part 10

From the offline raceline to MPC and LMPC; conventions; pitfalls

The standard pipeline. Once the raceline is computed offline, store reference profiles e_y^\star(s), v_x^\star(s), e_\psi^\star(s), u^\star(s); an online MPC tracks them while reacting to deviations. Learning MPC adds a layer: successful laps become safe-set data, lap performance provides cost-to-go data, and local dynamics may be learned from stored transitions. That is the bridge to chapter 07 and the later racing chapters. An offline raceline is one nonlinear trajectory optimisation for the whole lap; LMPC racing (Rosolia and Borrelli [2]) solves short-horizon problems online and learns from previous laps. Related, not interchangeable.

Conventions worth keeping straight. This chapter's normal is the left normal n = (-t_y, t_x) with p = \gamma + e_y n; a code using n_{\mathrm{code}} = -n and p = \gamma - e_y n_{\mathrm{code}} produces the same mapping. The course code's input vector is u = [\delta, a], steering first; raceline papers often write [a_x, \delta].

Common pitfalls. Frenet coordinates constrain the vehicle to the centreline: no, they keep e_y; only the reduced planner sets it to zero. Curvature alone determines the optimal speed: no, it gives a local lateral limit; acceleration and braking couple different locations. Brake where the cap becomes small: too late; the braking constraint propagates the corner backwards. 2\Delta s/(v_i + v_{i+1}) is an arbitrary trapezoidal rule: for the constant-acceleration segment model it is exact. 1 - K e_y is just a denominator: it comes from differentiating \gamma + e_y n and has a genuine singularity. Independent longitudinal and lateral limits equal tire-force constraints: no, a combined limit couples them, as the friction-circle diagnostic shows. The reduced speed planner is the raceline optimiser: no, it fixes the path and optimises only speed.

Part 11

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question before opening it.

1. Why use Frenet coordinates for racing?

Progress along the track becomes s, lateral road constraints become direct bounds on e_y, and the track geometry is summarised by the curvature K(s).

2. Derive the factor 1 - K e_y.

Differentiate p = \gamma(s) + e_y n(s) with \gamma' = t and n' = -Kt: \dot p = (1 - K e_y)\dot s\, t + \dot e_y\, n.

3. Derive \dot s.

The tangential velocity relative to the track is v_x\cos e_\psi - v_y\sin e_\psi; equating tangent components gives \dot s = (v_x\cos e_\psi - v_y\sin e_\psi)/(1 - K e_y).

4. Why must \dot s > 0?

The spatial transformation divides every time derivative by \dot s. Zero progress makes it singular; negative progress reverses the assumed direction of the parameterisation.

5. Why is time a state?

Because s is the independent variable. The state t'(s) = 1/\dot s accumulates elapsed time, and minimising t(s_f) minimises lap time.

6. Why introduce z = v^2?

It turns dv/ds = a/v into dz/ds = 2a, affine in the longitudinal acceleration.

7. Where does the curvature speed limit come from?

Lateral acceleration is |a_y| = v^2|K|; imposing |a_y| \le \mu g gives v^2 \le \mu g / |K|.

8. Why is a forward pass needed?

To enforce that speed cannot increase faster than maximum longitudinal acceleration permits.

9. Why is a backward pass needed?

To propagate future low-speed corner requirements upstream according to the finite braking authority.

10. Why is the largest feasible z profile minimum-time?

Every segment-time term decreases as either endpoint speed increases, so a componentwise larger feasible profile cannot be slower.

11. What is missing from the executable planner?

Optimised lateral offset, steering, lateral and yaw dynamics, nonlinear tire forces, combined tire-force constraints.

12. Why can a full raceline be faster than the centreline solution?

It can use the track width to reduce the effective path curvature through corners and carry more speed while staying inside the corridor.

13. What does a friction-circle utilisation above one mean here?

The decoupled reduced model allowed simultaneous longitudinal and lateral accelerations that a simple combined-friction model would forbid.

14. How does this chapter connect to Learning MPC?

The offline raceline provides reference geometry and performance; repeated online laps can later supply safe-set, terminal-value and local-model data for LMPC.

Part 12

Exercises

  1. Frenet-map derivative. From p = \gamma(s) + e_y n(s), derive \dot p = (1 - Ke_y)\dot s\, t + \dot e_y\, n.
    Hint

    Chain rule in s(t), then Frenet–Serret for n'.

    Solution

    \dot p = \gamma'(s)\dot s + \dot e_y n + e_y n'(s)\dot s. With \gamma' = t and n' = -Kt: \dot p = t\dot s + n\dot e_y - Ke_y t\dot s = (1 - Ke_y)\dot s\, t + \dot e_y\, n. The coefficient of t is the tangential speed of the offset point, the coefficient of n its normal speed.

  2. Progress rate. Resolve the body-frame velocity into tangent and normal components and derive \dot s and \dot e_y.
    Hint

    Rotate (v_x, v_y) by the heading error.

    Solution

    The body axes are rotated by e_\psi relative to (t, n), so the velocity in the track frame is v_t = v_x\cos e_\psi - v_y\sin e_\psi and v_n = v_x\sin e_\psi + v_y\cos e_\psi. Matching with exercise 1: (1 - Ke_y)\dot s = v_t, hence \dot s = v_t/(1 - Ke_y), and \dot e_y = v_n. The notebook's example, v_x = 12, v_y = 0.4, e_\psi = 0.05, K = 0.02, e_y = 0.5, gives v_t = 11.965, \dot s = 11.965/0.99 = 12.086 and \dot e_y = 0.999.

  3. Heading-error dynamics. From e_\psi = \psi - \psi_c(s) and d\psi_c/ds = K, derive \dot e_\psi = r - K\dot s.
    Hint

    Differentiate in time and use the chain rule on \psi_c(s(t)).

    Solution

    \dot e_\psi = \dot\psi - \frac{d\psi_c}{ds}\dot s = r - K(s)\dot s. In the example, 0.15 - 0.02 \cdot 12.086 = -0.0917 rad/s: the car yaws more slowly than the track turns, so its heading error decreases.

  4. Spatial derivatives. Derive explicit formulas for de_y/ds, de_\psi/ds and dt/ds.
    Hint

    Divide by \dot s.

    Solution

    de_y/ds = \dot e_y/\dot s = (1 - Ke_y)(v_x\sin e_\psi + v_y\cos e_\psi)/(v_x\cos e_\psi - v_y\sin e_\psi); de_\psi/ds = r/\dot s - K; dt/ds = 1/\dot s = (1 - Ke_y)/(v_x\cos e_\psi - v_y\sin e_\psi). Example: 0.0827, -0.0076 rad/m and 0.0827 s/m.

  5. Coordinate regularity. For a constant-curvature turn of radius R, find the lateral offset at which the Frenet map becomes singular and interpret it.
    Hint

    Set 1 - Ke_y = 0 with K = 1/R.

    Solution

    e_y = 1/K = R, on the inside of the turn: the offset point sits at the centre of the circle, where all normal lines meet, so s is no longer determined by the position. Beyond R the map folds over. For the tightest corner of this track, R = 7.73 m against a half-width of 3 m, which is why the margin 1 - |K|W stays at 0.61.

  6. Squared-speed dynamics. Show that z = v^2 leads to z_{i+1} = z_i + 2\Delta s_i a_i.
    Hint

    Integrate dz/ds = 2a over one segment.

    Solution

    dz/ds = 2v\,dv/ds = 2v\cdot a/v = 2a. With a = a_i constant on [s_i, s_{i+1}], integrating gives z_{i+1} - z_i = 2a_i\Delta s_i.

  7. Exact segment time. Derive \Delta t_i = 2\Delta s_i/(v_i + v_{i+1}) for constant acceleration.
    Hint

    Divide v_{i+1}^2 - v_i^2 = 2a_i\Delta s_i by v_{i+1} - v_i = a_i\Delta t_i.

    Solution

    (v_{i+1} - v_i)(v_{i+1} + v_i) = 2a_i\Delta s_i and v_{i+1} - v_i = a_i\Delta t_i, so a_i\Delta t_i(v_{i+1} + v_i) = 2a_i\Delta s_i, hence the formula for a_i \ne 0; for a_i = 0 it reduces to \Delta s_i/v_i, also correct. It is the distance divided by the mean speed, which is exact because speed is linear in time under constant acceleration.

  8. Braking distance in spatial form. From z_0 with constant braking a_{\min} < 0, derive the distance to reach z_f.
    Hint

    z_f = z_0 + 2a_{\min}\Delta s.

    Solution

    \Delta s = (z_f - z_0)/(2a_{\min}) = (z_0 - z_f)/(2|a_{\min}|). From 20.91 to 8.71 m/s at -6 m/s²: (437.4 - 75.8)/12 = 30.1 m, which is why the planner's braking zone before the tightest corner starts 31.5 m upstream (the last metre follows the falling cap rather than full braking).

  9. Friction sweep. Repeat the lap for several \mu and explain which portions of the track change most.
    Hint

    Compare the \mu = 1.2 and \mu = 0.6 profiles of the Part 8 widget.

    Solution

    Lap times 20.14, 18.36, 17.09 and 16.13 s for \mu = 0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.2. The difference between the two extreme profiles is largest in the corners and on the exits (5.8 m/s at s = 5.3, the exit of the final bend), because the cap \sqrt{\mu g/|K|} scales with \sqrt\mu and the exit speed starts from the cap. It is smallest in the middle of the long sides (1.3 m/s at s = 203), where the speed is limited by how much the car could accelerate since the last corner, not by grip. With \mu = 0.6 the cap is active on 31 percent of the samples.

  10. Braking sweep. Change a_{\min} at fixed a_{\max} and identify how far upstream the braking zone moves.
    Hint

    The Part 7 widget reports the start of full braking for any a_{\min}.

    Solution

    Before the tightest corner, full braking starts 46.3, 37.9, 31.5 and 24.2 m upstream for a_{\min} = -3, -4.5, -6, -9, from entry speeds of 18.6, 20.1, 20.9 and 21.8 m/s, with lap times 17.97, 17.40, 17.09 and 16.80 s. The apex speed is 8.706 m/s in every case: braking authority changes where braking starts and what speed can be reached before it, never the corner speed, which belongs to \mu and K.

  11. Grid convergence. Use at least four resolutions and estimate the discretisation error of the lap time.
    Hint

    Look at how the increments between successive doublings shrink.

    Solution

    Lap times 16.9383, 17.0721, 17.0939, 17.1062, 17.1081 and 17.1090 s for 60 to 1920 samples; the increments 0.134, 0.022, 0.012, 0.0019 and 0.0009 s shrink roughly by a factor two per doubling once the apex is resolved (from 480 on), which is first-order behaviour. Summing the geometric tail, the limit is near 17.110 s, so the 240-sample value is about 0.016 s (0.1 percent) optimistic and the 60-sample value 0.17 s (1 percent). The coarse grids are optimistic because they miss the apex: the maximum sampled curvature is 0.1205 at 60 samples against 0.1304 at 960.

  12. Combined tire-force constraint. Replace the decoupled condition with a_x^2 + (zK)^2 \le (\mu g)^2 and explain why the simple envelopes no longer have constant bounds.
    Hint

    Solve for the admissible a_x.

    Solution

    The admissible longitudinal acceleration becomes |a_x| \le \sqrt{(\mu g)^2 - (zK)^2}, which depends on the local speed and curvature: the faster the car corners, the less it may accelerate or brake. The forward bound z_{i+1} \le z_i + 2\Delta s_i a_{\max}(z_i, K_i) is now a nonlinear function of z_i, and the same for the backward bound, so the passes are no longer simple minimum operations with constant increments; at the apex, where zK = \mu g, there is no longitudinal authority at all, whereas the decoupled planner there still accelerates at 2.66 m/s². The fixed-point structure survives (the bounds are still monotone in z), but each pass must evaluate the circle, and the result is slower: the diagnostic of Part 9 shows the decoupled solution exceeding the circle on 66 segments.

  13. Full raceline variables. From the six-state spatial model, list all states, inputs and constraints of the full nonlinear raceline CFTOC.
    Hint

    Part 4's boxed problem.

    Solution

    States v_x, v_y, r, e_\psi, t, e_y as functions of s; inputs a_x(s) and \delta(s). Constraints: the six spatial differential equations (bicycle dynamics divided by \dot s, the kinematic rates, t' = 1/\dot s); the progress condition \dot s > 0; the track corridor |e_y| \le W(s); input bounds on a_x and \delta (and possibly on their rates); tire-force limits, either through the saturating tire law or an explicit friction circle; initial conditions at s = 0 and periodicity of the velocity states for a flying lap. Objective: t(s_f).

  14. Offline/online architecture. How could the offline raceline serve as a reference for an online MPC, and which deviations would it regulate?
    Hint

    Think in Frenet coordinates.

    Solution

    Store e_y^\star(s), v_x^\star(s), e_\psi^\star(s) and u^\star(s) on the spatial grid. Online, at the measured s, the MPC penalises the deviations e_y - e_y^\star, v_x - v_x^\star and e_\psi - e_\psi^\star over a short horizon, with the feedforward u^\star and the track corridor and input bounds as constraints, using a model linearised about the raceline, which keeps the online problem a QP. It must regulate what the offline solution could not foresee: initial errors, disturbances, model mismatch, grip changes. LMPC replaces the fixed reference by data from the previous laps: safe set, cost-to-go and, in chapter 07, locally learned dynamics.

Part 13

The chapter on one page

  1. Geometry: tangent t = \gamma', left normal n, signed curvature K; t' = Kn, n' = -Kt.
  2. Track: closed-form curve, 252.3 m, two corners of radius 7.7 m, W = 3; Frenet margin 0.61.
  3. Position map: p = \gamma + e_y n, \dot p = (1 - Ke_y)\dot s\, t + \dot e_y\, n, singular at e_y = 1/K.
  4. Rates: \dot s = (v_x\cos e_\psi - v_y\sin e_\psi)/(1 - Ke_y), \dot e_y = v_x\sin e_\psi + v_y\cos e_\psi, \dot e_\psi = r - K\dot s.
  5. Space: d\xi/ds = \dot\xi/\dot s needs \dot s > 0; time is a state, t' = 1/\dot s; minimise t(s_f).
  6. Vehicle: BARC bicycle model with Pacejka axles, D = 7.77 N; the full raceline CFTOC is a nonlinear program.
  7. Reduced: z = v^2, dz/ds = 2a, cap \min(v_{\max}^2, \mu g/|K|), exact \Delta t = 2\Delta s/(v_i + v_{i+1}).
  8. Planner: forward and backward passes from the cap to the greatest fixed point; 3 sweeps, 17.0939 s, 8.7 to 20.9 m/s.
  9. Sweeps: friction 20.14 to 16.13 s; weak brakes +0.64 s, weak engine +1.65 s; grid converges from below, about 0.016 s off at 240 samples.
  10. Coupling: friction-circle use up to 1.17 on 66 segments; the full raceline uses the width and tire constraints, and is faster.

Scope. The standard spatial formulation of minimum-lap-time problems, derived in full and executed only for the centreline speed-planning reduction, whose solution is exact for the piecewise-constant-acceleration model and computed here without any optimisation solver. The full raceline needs a nonlinear program; the chapter says what it contains and why.

Vehicle–bike collision avoidance as a constrained finite-time optimal control problem

A vehicle approaches a crossing that a bicycle will occupy at a known time. Should it stay on the near side, or accelerate and clear the crossing first? The vehicle model is a point mass; the difficulty is the word "or": during the bicycle's presence the vehicle's centre must be either short of the region or beyond it, and the union of those two half-lines is not convex. Below, both strategies are solved as convex quadratic programs for every one of 660 initial states, live, and you can change the friction, the horizon, the cost, the discretisation and the meaning of "yield".

Deriving the collision-free bounds. The vehicle centre is d, so the vehicle occupies [d - l_{\mathrm{car}}/2,\ d + l_{\mathrm{car}}/2]; the conflict region is [d_{\mathrm{bike}} - w_{\mathrm{bike}}/2,\ d_{\mathrm{bike}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}/2]. Two ways not to overlap. Yield side: the front stays short of the near edge, d + l_{\mathrm{car}}/2 \le d_{\mathrm{bike}} - w_{\mathrm{bike}}/2. Pass side: the rear has cleared the far edge, d - l_{\mathrm{car}}/2 \ge d_{\mathrm{bike}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}/2. Hence

\boxed{d \le d_{\max} = d_{\mathrm{bike}} - \tfrac{l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}}{2} = 93.75 \text{ m}} \quad\text{or}\quad \boxed{d \ge d_{\min} = d_{\mathrm{bike}} + \tfrac{l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}}{2} = 106.25 \text{ m}}.

The forbidden centre positions form a gap of d_{\min} - d_{\max} = l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}} = 12.5 m.

Why the safe set is nonconvex. During the bicycle's presence the collision-free set is \mathcal S_{\mathrm{safe}} = (-\infty, d_{\max}] \cup [d_{\min}, \infty), the union of two half-lines. Take d^{(1)} = d_{\max} - 1 and d^{(2)} = d_{\min} + 1, both safe; their midpoint is d_{\mathrm{bike}}, the middle of the crossing. A convex combination of safe points need not be safe, and that is the whole reason a logical decision appears.

What is a disjunctive constraint?

In plain words: a requirement of the form "this or that", where each alternative is easy but the choice between them is not, because the set of allowed states is two separate pieces.

More precisely: a constraint x \in \mathcal A \cup \mathcal B with \mathcal A, \mathcal B convex and disjoint. With a binary variable s \in \{0, 1\} and a bound M large enough, it becomes two linear inequalities, d \le d_{\max} + M(1 - s) and d \ge d_{\min} - Ms, and the problem a mixed-integer quadratic program. With one binary variable it is solved exactly by solving the two convex alternatives and taking the cheaper feasible one.

Part 2

Vehicle model, constraints and cost

Longitudinal point mass. \dot d = v, \dot v = a, state x = (d, v), input u = a. Discretised with explicit Euler and T_s = 1 s,

\boxed{d_{k+1} = d_k + T_s v_k}, \qquad \boxed{v_{k+1} = v_k + T_s a_k}, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & T_s \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ T_s \end{pmatrix}.

A subtle consequence: a_k changes the next velocity but does not enter d_{k+1} directly. The exact zero-order-hold discretisation of the same continuous model has d_{k+1} = d_k + T_s v_k + \tfrac12 T_s^2 a_k, so B_{\mathrm{ZOH}} = (\tfrac12 T_s^2, T_s). The chapter keeps Euler, for which Part 5 has closed-form reachability; with a one-second sample the choice visibly moves feasibility boundaries, which the demo's last toggle and exercise 8 show.

Constraints. Speed 0 \le v_k \le 32 m/s, the lower bound forbidding reversing in this one-dimensional scene. Acceleration is friction-limited,

\boxed{-\mu g \le a_k \le \mu g}: \qquad \mu = 0.10 \Rightarrow |a_k| \le 0.981 \text{ m/s}^2, \qquad \mu = 0.05 \Rightarrow |a_k| \le 0.4905 \text{ m/s}^2,

an intentionally simplified model: symmetric limits, a known constant \mu, no drag or powertrain limits, no combined longitudinal and lateral tyre usage.

Cost. With R = 1, Q = 3, P = 3 and reference speed v_{\mathrm{ref}} = 20 m/s,

\boxed{J = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} R a_k^2 + \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} Q (v_k - v_{\mathrm{ref}})^2 + P (v_N - v_{\mathrm{ref}})^2}.

Effort, speed tracking during the manoeuvre, terminal speed error. The position does not appear in the cost at all; it enters only through the collision-avoidance constraints.

Part 3

The logical manoeuvre choice

Two convex strategy problems. Fix the strategy in advance and each problem is a convex QP: linear dynamics, linear state and input constraints, a convex quadratic objective, plus d_k \le d_{\max} during the bicycle's presence for yield, or d_k \ge d_{\min} for pass. The nonconvexity appears only when the optimiser is asked to choose between them.

One binary variable. Let s = 1 mean yield and s = 0 pass. A compact Big-M encoding is (1 - s)\, d_{\min} \le d_k \le d_{\max} + (1 - s) D with a large D: for s = 1 it reads 0 \le d_k \le d_{\max}, for s = 0 it reads d_{\min} \le d_k \le d_{\max} + D. The more general symmetric form is

d_k \le d_{\max} + M(1 - s), \qquad d_k \ge d_{\min} - Ms,

which relaxes the branch not chosen by M. With a quadratic objective and a binary s, this is a mixed-integer quadratic program (MIQP) [2].

Why a very large Big-M is not free. M must be large enough to deactivate the inactive branch, but an unnecessarily huge value weakens the continuous relaxation used by branch-and-bound, worsens conditioning, makes tolerances troublesome and slows the search. M = 5000 is valid here, since every reachable position is within a few hundred metres of the crossing; production hybrid MPC derives the smallest valid bounds from the known state envelope (exercise 4).

Why two QPs instead. There is exactly one binary decision, so the mixed problem is \min\{J^\star_{\mathrm{yield}}, J^\star_{\mathrm{pass}}\} with infeasible branches discarded: solve the yield QP, solve the pass QP, keep the cheaper feasible one. Transparent, solver-independent, and exactly equivalent to enumerating the binary variable. It would not scale to dozens of decisions, where enumeration needs up to 2^m combinations and a mixed-integer solver's pruning is what makes the problem tractable.

Part 4

The nominal scenario

Which yield condition? The geometric condition d_k \le d_{\max} includes the vehicle and crossing geometry. A looser centre-line variant asks only that the vehicle centre has not passed the centre of the crossing, d_N \le d_{\mathrm{bike}}, which admits positions where the front already overlaps the region. Neither imposes a terminal speed, so yielding does not mean stopping. The course uses the geometric bound by default and labels the centre-line one explicitly when used.

A horizon that ends when the bicycle arrives. The nominal scenario sets T = t_{\min} = 4 s, so the strategy constraint is imposed only at k = N; Part 9 extends it to a whole occupancy window. From x_0 = (60, 5):

\text{yield: feasible, } J = 2582.56,\ x_4 = (85.886,\ 8.924); \qquad \text{pass: infeasible.}

Yield is selected. One thing the notebook's plots do not say out loud: the selected yield plan accelerates at the full +0.981 m/s² for all four steps and ends at d_4 = 85.9 m, short of d_{\max} = 93.75 with room to spare. The yield constraint is not active. From this state the vehicle cannot reach the region in four seconds whatever it does, so "yield" here is simply speed tracking towards 20 m/s, and the only thing the collision logic did was rule passing out.

Why passing is impossible from here. Even maximal acceleration cannot move the centre beyond d_{\min} = 106.25 m by t = 4 s under the Euler model. Part 5 shows this from the dynamics rather than from the solver.

Part 5

Analytic reachability under the Euler model

Closed-form velocity and terminal position. From v_{k+1} = v_k + T_s a_k, v_k = v_0 + T_s \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} a_j; from d_{k+1} = d_k + T_s v_k, d_N = d_0 + T_s \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} v_k, and substituting,

\boxed{d_N = d_0 + N T_s v_0 + T_s^2 \sum_{j=0}^{N-2} (N - 1 - j)\, a_j}.

Notice that a_{N-1} does not affect d_N: it changes v_N, but the final position was already computed from v_{N-1}. A discretisation-specific property worth knowing before reading the optimiser's output.

Extrema when the speed bounds are inactive. Setting every position-relevant acceleration to \pm\mu g and using \sum_{r=1}^{N-1} r = N(N-1)/2,

\boxed{d_N^{\max / \min} = d_0 + N T_s v_0 \pm \mu g T_s^2 \frac{N(N-1)}{2}}.

For x_0 = (60, 5), N = 4, \mu = 0.10: d_4 \in [74.114, 85.886]. The maximum is below d_{\min} = 106.25, so passing is dynamically impossible; the yield bound 93.75 is above the maximum, so yielding costs nothing. That is the analytic explanation of the solver's verdict, and of the inactive constraint.

Width of the controllable interval and the critical friction. The reachable terminal positions span d_N^{\max} - d_N^{\min} = \mu g T_s^2 N(N-1), while the forbidden gap is l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}. A necessary condition for some initial state to have both strategies reachable is therefore \mu g T_s^2 N(N-1) \ge l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}, with the critical value

\boxed{\mu_{\mathrm{crit}} = \frac{l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}}{g T_s^2 N(N-1)}} = \frac{12.5}{9.81 \cdot 12} = 0.1062 .

At \mu = 0.10 the width is 11.77 m, just short of the 12.5 m gap, so no initial state has both strategies feasible, which the map at the top confirms with zero "both" cells; at \mu = 0.12 the width is 14.13 m and overlap becomes possible.

Part 6

Friction and feasibility

A set-inclusion argument. Let \mathcal U(\mu) = [-\mu g, \mu g]. If 0 < \mu_1 < \mu_2 then \mathcal U(\mu_1) \subset \mathcal U(\mu_2): every input sequence admissible at low friction is admissible at high friction. With everything else unchanged,

\boxed{\mathcal F(\mu_1) \subseteq \mathcal F(\mu_2)},

where \mathcal F(\mu) is the set of initial states from which at least one modelled strategy is feasible. "Lower friction enlarges the infeasible region" is a theorem about sets, not an observation from a plot.

Strategy maps. The notebook solves both QPs on a grid of initial states, d_0 = 0, 5, \dots, 95 and v_0 = 0, 1, \dots, 32, 660 states, and keeps two maps: how many strategies are feasible (0, 1 or 2) and which feasible one is cheaper. That separates feasibility from preference. At \mu = 0.10 the notebook reports 8 of 660 states with no strategy; at \mu = 0.05, 36, an increase of 28 that the inclusion argument guarantees for every state, not only grid points. The demo recomputes both maps on every slider change.

One honest detail from reproducing the count: at the state (85, 3) the course's SLSQP solver reports the yield problem infeasible, and the notebook counts 8. It is feasible: full braking (with v \ge 0) lands the centre at d_4 = 91.1 < 93.75, and the optimal yield plan ends exactly on the bound, which is where that solver stumbles. The demo's interior-point solver finds the plan and reports 7. The infeasible states lie along the line d_0 + 4 v_0 \approx 100: too fast to stop short, too slow to clear.

Part 7

What do Q, R and P actually change?

Objective weights do not change feasibility. For a fixed model, horizon, state, input and collision constraints, changing Q, R, P changes only the objective; it cannot create or destroy feasible trajectories. In exact optimisation,

\boxed{\text{the feasible set is independent of } Q, R, P}.

What the weights can change is which feasible trajectory is optimal and, when both branches are feasible, which is cheaper.

A state where both are feasible. With \mu = 0.15 and x_0 = (30, 17), both strategies are feasible, and only R is varied:

With a small effort penalty, speed tracking dominates and passing, which keeps the speed near 20, is preferred. The correct separation: \mu changes control authority and feasibility; Q, R, P change preference among feasible solutions. Part 8

"Yield" is not automatically "stop"

Three distinct statements that casual language conflates. A, geometric yield: d_N \le d_{\max}, the vehicle is on the safe near side at the conflict time. B, full stop: d_N \le d_{\max} and v_N = 0, strictly more restrictive. C, centre-line condition: d_N \le d_{\mathrm{bike}}, looser than A because d_{\mathrm{bike}} > d_{\max}. From x_0 = (60, 5): A feasible, C feasible, B infeasible: the vehicle can stay short of the region but cannot brake from 5 m/s to zero in four seconds at 0.981 m/s² (it needs 5.1 s). Hence

\boxed{\text{yield feasible} \ \not\Rightarrow\ \text{full stop feasible}}.

Toggle "yield must end at a full stop" in the demo: the yield-feasible region collapses from 267 grid states to 75, roughly those with v_0 \le N \mu g = 3.9 m/s and enough room (exercise 6).

Part 9

The general bicycle-occupancy window

From a terminal-time condition to a time-indexed one. Suppose the bicycle occupies the region over t \in [t_{\min}, t_{\max}] = [4, 6] and the horizon continues beyond, N = 8. Yield must then impose d_k \le d_{\max} for k = 4, 5, 6, not merely at the first instant; pass must impose d_k \ge d_{\min} over the same steps, although with v_k \ge 0 the later pass inequalities are redundant, since a vehicle that has cleared the region cannot come back. For yield, enforcing the whole window is essential. From (60, 5), the window problem selects yield with cost 4730.0: the vehicle brakes briefly, holds its centre at exactly d_6 = 93.75 as the bicycle leaves, then accelerates to reach 112 m at k = 8.

Why one binary variable is meaningful. One strategy variable for the whole window commits to one topological manoeuvre: near side throughout, or far side throughout. Independent binaries s_k at every step would let the optimiser be yield-side at one conflict instant and pass-side at a later one, which physically means driving through the forbidden interval while the bicycle is there. Logical variables must encode the manoeuvre-level logic, not individual inequalities.

Part 10

Interpreting infeasibility, model limitations, pitfalls

"Neither strategy is feasible" has a precise but limited meaning. It says that under this model no admissible acceleration sequence satisfies either manoeuvre. It does not prove a collision is physically unavoidable: the model excludes steering around the bicycle, the bicycle reacting, leaving the lane, and emergency manoeuvres outside the acceleration envelope. The correct statement is that neither modelled longitudinal strategy is feasible under the stated assumptions, and that qualification matters in safety analysis.

Limitations. The bicycle's timing is exactly known, where real systems have uncertainty in detection, speed, intent and arrival time; a robust formulation would require safety for every arrival time in a set, a chance-constrained one a probabilistic risk bound. The friction is a known scalar, where a conservative robust option replaces \mu by a guaranteed lower bound \underline\mu (exercise 9); richer models use friction circles and combined-slip tyres [4]. The vehicle is a one-dimensional point mass without steering, lateral position, yaw, tyre slip, actuator delay or estimation error. This is deliberate: it isolates one idea:

\text{simple dynamics} + \text{logical environment constraint} \Rightarrow \text{hybrid, nonconvex optimal control}.

Pitfalls.

  1. "The double-integrator dynamics make the problem convex." No. Each fixed strategy is a convex QP; the union "d \le d_{\max} or d \ge d_{\min}" is not. The logic, not the plant, creates the nonconvexity.
  2. "Lower \mu only changes comfort." No. It bounds the input, so it changes the reachable set and feasibility.
  3. "Changing Q or R changes the feasible region." Not with the constraints and model unchanged. Weights change optimality.
  4. "Yield means v_N = 0." Only if imposed. The nominal formulation imposes no terminal speed.
  5. "d_N \le d_{\mathrm{bike}} is geometric collision avoidance." No. It admits d_N \in (d_{\max}, d_{\mathrm{bike}}], where the front already overlaps the region.
  6. "An enormous Big-M can only help." Too small invalidates the formulation; needlessly large weakens the relaxation and degrades the numerics.
  7. "Solver infeasibility means a collision is unavoidable." It means no solution exists within the modelled dynamics, bounds, strategies and prediction assumptions.
Part 11

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question before opening it.

1. How are dmax and dmin derived?

By preventing overlap between the vehicle interval [d - l_{\mathrm{car}}/2, d + l_{\mathrm{car}}/2] and the conflict interval [d_{\mathrm{bike}} - w_{\mathrm{bike}}/2, d_{\mathrm{bike}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}/2]. The two no-overlap cases give d \le d_{\mathrm{bike}} - (l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}})/2 or d \ge d_{\mathrm{bike}} + (l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}})/2.

2. Why is the collision-free set nonconvex?

It is a union of two separated half-lines. Two safe points on opposite sides have an unsafe midpoint inside the forbidden interval.

3. Why is each fixed-strategy problem convex?

Linear dynamics, linear state, input and strategy constraints once the branch is fixed, and a convex quadratic objective.

4. What optimisation class results when the binary variable is included?

An MIQP: quadratic objective, linear dynamics and constraints, one binary variable; a particular mixed-integer problem.

5. Why can we solve two QPs instead of an MIQP here?

There is one binary decision. Enumerating s = 0 and s = 1 gives exactly the minimum of the two branch-optimal costs.

6. What does μ do mathematically?

It scales the admissible input interval a \in [-\mu g, \mu g]. Reducing it gives a subset of the former control set, so the set of feasible initial states cannot grow.

7. Do Q, R and P change feasibility?

No, not when only the weights change. They change which feasible trajectory or strategy is optimal; feasibility is set by the dynamics and constraints.

8. What is unusual about the last acceleration under explicit Euler?

a_{N-1} changes v_N but not d_N, because the position update uses v_{N-1} and has no direct acceleration term.

9. Why is "yield" different from "stop"?

Yield can mean only that the vehicle remains before the region. A full stop additionally requires v_N = 0 or an equivalent condition.

10. Why must the yield constraint be enforced for the whole occupancy window?

Being safe at the instant the bicycle enters does not stop the vehicle from entering the region a step later while the bicycle is still there.

11. What is the main danger of a large Big-M?

A sufficiently large value deactivates the intended inequality, but an excessive one weakens the continuous relaxation and brings poor numerics and a slow mixed-integer search.

12. What does "both strategies infeasible" actually prove?

Only that neither modelled longitudinal manoeuvre is possible within the chosen horizon and constraints; not that every physically possible avoidance has failed.

Part 12

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution; the code runs after the notebook's cells. Try first, then peek.

  1. Conflict geometry. Derive d_{\max} and d_{\min} from interval non-overlap, then show d_{\min} - d_{\max} = l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}.
    Hint

    Two intervals do not overlap when one ends before the other begins.

    Solution

    No overlap means d + l_{\mathrm{car}}/2 \le d_{\mathrm{bike}} - w_{\mathrm{bike}}/2 or d - l_{\mathrm{car}}/2 \ge d_{\mathrm{bike}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}/2, which rearrange to d \le d_{\max} and d \ge d_{\min}. Subtracting, d_{\min} - d_{\max} = 2 \cdot (l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}})/2 = l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}} = 12.5 m.

  2. Euler prediction. From d_{k+1} = d_k + T_s v_k and v_{k+1} = v_k + T_s a_k, derive d_N = d_0 + N T_s v_0 + T_s^2 \sum_{j=0}^{N-2} (N - 1 - j) a_j.
    Hint

    Write v_k as a sum of accelerations, then sum the velocities and count how many times each a_j appears.

    Solution

    v_k = v_0 + T_s \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} a_j, and d_N = d_0 + T_s \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} v_k = d_0 + N T_s v_0 + T_s^2 \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} a_j. The acceleration a_j appears in v_k for every k \ge j + 1, that is N - 1 - j times for j \le N - 2 and zero times for j = N - 1. Swapping the sums gives the formula.

  3. Critical friction. Ignoring active speed constraints, derive \mu_{\mathrm{crit}} = (l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}) / (g T_s^2 N(N-1)). What does it mean, and what does it not guarantee?
    Hint

    Compare the width of the reachable terminal interval with the forbidden gap.

    Solution

    The reachable terminal positions span \mu g T_s^2 N(N-1); both strategies can only be reachable from one state if that span is at least the gap l_{\mathrm{car}} + w_{\mathrm{bike}}, which gives \mu_{\mathrm{crit}} = 0.1062 for N = 4. It is a necessary condition for the two feasible regions to overlap anywhere, and only that: it does not say overlap occurs, since the speed bounds may bind and the interval must also be placed so that both bounds fall inside it, and it says nothing about which strategy is preferred.

  4. Big-M. Starting from s = 1 for yield and s = 0 for pass, derive the symmetric Big-M constraints, and for a known envelope d \in [d_{\mathrm{low}}, d_{\mathrm{high}}] derive valid lower bounds on M.
    Hint

    The relaxed inequality must be satisfied by every position in the envelope.

    Solution

    Yield must hold when s = 1 and be void when s = 0: d_k \le d_{\max} + M(1 - s). Pass must hold when s = 0 and be void when s = 1: d_k \ge d_{\min} - Ms. "Void" means satisfied by every d \in [d_{\mathrm{low}}, d_{\mathrm{high}}]: d_{\mathrm{high}} \le d_{\max} + M and d_{\mathrm{low}} \ge d_{\min} - M, so M \ge \max(d_{\mathrm{high}} - d_{\max},\ d_{\min} - d_{\mathrm{low}}). For positions in [0, 300] m that is M \ge \max(206.25, 106.25) = 206.25, far below the round 5000.

  5. Objective weights. Choose an initial state where both strategies are feasible, vary only R, and verify that feasibility does not change while the selected strategy may.
    Hint

    Part 7's state and friction.

    Solution

    At \mu = 0.15 and (30, 17), both branches are feasible for R = 0.1, 1, 1000 (the solver succeeds every time, with unchanged constraints), and the choice flips from pass to yield at large R. The demo reproduces the table of Part 7.

  8. Discretisation. Repeat the feasibility map with the exact ZOH discretisation and explain why the boundary moves.
    Hint

    Under ZOH the current acceleration already moves the next position by \tfrac12 T_s^2 a_k.

    Solution

    Under ZOH every acceleration acts on the position half a step earlier, so the reachable terminal interval widens: from (60, 5) full throttle reaches d_4 = 87.85 instead of 85.89. On the grid, the states with no strategy fall from 8 to 2, and 14 states gain both strategies where Euler had none, because the wider interval can straddle the gap. The physics did not change; the discretisation's one-step lag in the position update did, which is why a one-second sample is coarse for this problem.

  9. Friction uncertainty. With \mu \in [0.05, 0.10], formulate a robust acceleration bound that guarantees admissibility for every value in the interval, and discuss its conservatism.
    Hint

    Plan for the worst friction.

    Solution

    Impose |a_k| \le \underline\mu g = 0.4905 m/s². Every such sequence is admissible for every \mu \ge 0.05, so the plan is safe whatever the road turns out to be. The price is the \mu = 0.05 map: 36 instead of 8 grid states with no strategy, 28 states given up although most of them would be fine on the actual road. A friction estimator that narrows the interval buys those states back.

  10. Beyond one dimension. List the states, inputs and constraints needed to let the vehicle steer around the region rather than only brake or accelerate.
    Hint

    What does a lane change need that a longitudinal model lacks?

    Solution

    States: lateral position and heading (and yaw rate for a dynamic model), so at least (d, y, \psi, v). Inputs: steering angle or lateral acceleration alongside a. Constraints: lane and road boundaries on y, a steering-rate limit, a combined tyre limit replacing the scalar bound, for instance a_x^2 + a_y^2 \le (\mu g)^2, and a two-dimensional collision constraint, the car's rectangle against the bicycle's region, which is again disjunctive: pass in front, behind, left or right, four binaries or a convex outer approximation. The nonconvex logic multiplies; chapter 06 moves to the Frenet coordinates that make such constraints tractable along a track.

Part 13

The chapter on one page

  1. Geometry: safe centre positions are d \le 93.75 or d \ge 106.25, a nonconvex union with a 12.5 m gap.
  2. Model: Euler point mass, 0 \le v \le 32, |a| \le \mu g, speed-tracking cost with no position term.
  3. Logic: each strategy is a convex QP; the choice is one binary variable; two QPs solve the MIQP exactly.
  4. Nominal: from (60, 5), yield at cost 2582.6 with its bound inactive; pass impossible because d_4 \le 85.9 < 106.25.
  5. Reachability: d_N = d_0 + N v_0 \pm \mu g N(N-1)/2; \mu_{\mathrm{crit}} = 0.106, just above the nominal 0.10.
  6. Friction: \mathcal F(\mu_1) \subseteq \mathcal F(\mu_2); 8 and 36 of 660 states without a strategy at \mu = 0.10 and 0.05.
  7. Weights: change preference only; at (30, 17), \mu = 0.15, the choice flips from pass to yield as R grows.
  8. Yield is not stop: a full stop is infeasible from the nominal state; the window must be enforced at every conflict step.
  9. Meaning: infeasibility is a statement about the model, not about the world.

Scope. A deterministic one-dimensional CFTOC: longitudinal point mass under explicit Euler, symmetric friction-limited acceleration, bounded speed, a speed-tracking cost, and a bicycle whose occupancy of the crossing is known in advance (one instant in Parts 4 to 8, a window in Part 9). The disjunction is solved exactly by enumerating two convex QPs; the Big-M formulation, closed-form Euler reachability, the critical friction coefficient and the model's limitations are covered. The geometric yield bound is the default; the centre-line condition, a terminal full stop and the exact ZOH discretisation are explicit options.

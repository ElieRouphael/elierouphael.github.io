Advanced MPC and Learning MPC · Chapter 07 of 09

Data-driven local models for autonomous racing

A Learning MPC racing controller keeps two systems apart: the real car, here a nonlinear bicycle model of a 1:10-scale RC vehicle, and the prediction model inside the controller, which must be affine so that the online problem stays a quadratic program. The trick is not to fit one linear model to the whole track and hope. At every operating point the stored laps supply the transitions that resemble the current state and input, a weighted regression turns them into a local affine model of the velocities and the yaw rate, and the Frenet kinematics are linearised exactly by hand. This chapter builds that hybrid model from first principles and tests it where it counts: on a lap it has never seen, one step and fourteen steps ahead. Every fit below is recomputed from the recorded laps as you move the query.

Part 1

Plant model against prediction model

The nonlinear "real" system. The vehicle is chapter 06's dynamic bicycle model of a 1:10-scale RC car (BARC parameters) in curvilinear coordinates, with state x = [v_x, v_y, r, e_\psi, s, e_y]^\top: longitudinal and lateral velocity, yaw rate, heading error, progress, lateral deviation. The simulator input is u = [\delta, a_x]^\top, steering first. The tire forces are the simplified Pacejka law of chapter 06, so the state transition is nonlinear in both state and steering.

Why not put the full model in every QP? One could, and the online problem would then be a nonlinear program. Learning MPC racing (Rosolia and Borrelli [1]) instead builds a short-horizon affine approximation

\boxed{x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + B_k u_k + c_k,}

which keeps the quadratic-program structure while letting the matrices change with the operating point. That is the role of local identification: a nonlinear plant becomes a sequence of local affine models.

What is a local affine time-varying model?

In plain words: near the place where the car currently is, in terms of its speeds and inputs, the next state is well described by a linear map plus a constant. Move somewhere else and the map changes. The controller refits it from stored data at every step of its prediction.

More precisely: for a query (x_q, u_q), a first-order expansion of the plant gives x^+ \approx f(x_q, u_q) + A_q(x - x_q) + B_q(u - u_q) = A_q x + B_q u + c_q with c_q = f(x_q, u_q) - A_q x_q - B_q u_q. The model here is hybrid: the rows for v_x, v_y, r are estimated from data near the query, the rows for e_\psi, s, e_y are the exact Jacobian of the known Frenet kinematics. Along a prediction horizon the query moves and so do the matrices, hence "time varying".

Part 2

The racing data

An RC-car-scale track. Chapter 06's closed track is scaled geometrically by \alpha = 0.08 and given a half-width of 0.4 m. Lengths scale as s_{\mathrm{new}} = \alpha s, curvature inversely, K_{\mathrm{new}} = K / \alpha, which preserves the shape: 20.187 m long, curvature from -0.157 to 1.626 per metre (a tightest radius of 0.61 m), Frenet margin \min(1 - |K| W) = 0.349.

The exploratory seed controller. Learning starts from suboptimal but feasible laps driven by a noisy path-following controller,

\boxed{\delta = -0.6 e_y - 0.9 e_\psi + \text{steering dither}}, \qquad \boxed{a_x = 1.5 (v_t - v_x) + \text{acceleration dither}},

with target speed v_t = 0.8 m/s for the training laps and 0.85 m/s for the validation lap, the dither being clipped Gaussian noise drawn independently at every step of 0.1 s (the dynamics are integrated inside each step with explicit Euler at 1 ms). The dither matters for identification: a controller that repeats the same inputs leaves many local parameters unexcited. One safety choice is explicit: the action is clipped to the Learning MPC controller's input bounds |\delta| \le 0.5 and |a_x| \le 10, after the dither, so every recorded input satisfies the bounds the controller will impose.

Part 3

The local-neighbourhood metric

Raw distance would mix units. The query vector holds velocities, a yaw rate, a steering angle and an acceleration; a plain norm would be dominated by whichever has the largest numerical range. The implementation scales with D = \operatorname{diag}(0.1, 1, 1, 1, 1) and uses the scaled \ell_1 distance

\boxed{d_i = \big\| D (z_i - z_q) \big\|_1,}

the smaller coefficient on v_x reducing the dominance of longitudinal-speed differences. What is not in the metric: e_\psi, s, e_y. The regression looks for similar vehicle dynamics wherever they occurred on the track; the geometry is reintroduced analytically in the kinematic rows.

The Epanechnikov kernel. With bandwidth h = 5, points with d_i < h get the weight

\boxed{w_i = \tfrac34 \Big[ 1 - \big( \tfrac{d_i}{h} \big)^2 \Big]},

largest at the query, decreasing quadratically, zero outside the bandwidth. At most 7 points are kept from each stored lap, the nearest ones inside the bandwidth. If a lap has no point inside, its seven nearest transitions are used with the bandwidth widened just enough to cover them, and the lap is flagged as a support fallback (Part 10). Why per lap: selecting globally from the pooled samples would let one dense trajectory dominate; a small subset from each successful execution keeps several of them in the regression, at most 4 \times 7 = 28 samples here.

An honest observation about this data set. At the notebook's query (sample 120) all 28 selected points lie within 0.38 of the query against a bandwidth of 5, so their weights are all within 3 percent of the maximum. That is not special to sample 120: the ranges of the five features, weighted by (0.1, 1, 1, 1, 1), add up to 4.74, less than h, so for any query inside the envelope every stored transition is inside the kernel. The seven-points-per-lap rule does the selecting and the kernel is nearly uniform; a bandwidth below about 0.5 would be needed for its shape to matter (exercise 3). The bandwidth is still a genuine hyperparameter in general, just not an active one on this RC-car data.

Part 4

Weighted affine regression

Scalar problem. For one state component let y_i = x_{i+1, j} and \phi_i = [\text{selected regressors}, 1]^\top, the final 1 creating an affine offset. The local model solves

\boxed{\theta^\star = \arg\min_\theta \sum_i w_i \big( y_i - \phi_i^\top\theta \big)^2 + \lambda \|\theta\|_2^2 .}

Matrix form and normal equations. Stacking \Phi (rows \phi_i^\top), y and W = \operatorname{diag}(w), the objective is (y - \Phi\theta)^\top W (y - \Phi\theta) + \lambda\theta^\top\theta; its gradient -2\Phi^\top W(y - \Phi\theta) + 2\lambda\theta vanishes at

\boxed{\big( \Phi^\top W \Phi + \lambda I \big)\, \theta^\star = \Phi^\top W y,}

so \theta^\star = (\Phi^\top W\Phi + \lambda I)^{-1}\Phi^\top W y when the matrix is nonsingular. \lambda = 0 is ordinary weighted least squares; the implementation uses \lambda = 10^{-8} only as a safeguard against an exactly singular normal matrix.

Why the affine constant is necessary. Linearising x^+ = f(x, u) at (x_q, u_q) gives x^+ \approx A_q x + B_q u + c_q with c_q = f(x_q, u_q) - A_q x_q - B_q u_q. Unless the operating point is the origin and the map passes through it, forcing c_q = 0 creates a systematic bias. Exercise 6 measures it: without the offset the yaw-rate error on the held-out lap nearly doubles.

Part 5

The structured dynamic rows

The learned model predicts v_x^+ from [v_x, v_y, r, a_x] and v_y^+, r^+ from [v_x, v_y, r, \delta]:

\begin{aligned} v_x^+ &\approx a_{11}v_x + a_{12}v_y + a_{13}r + b_{12}a_x + c_1, \\ v_y^+ &\approx a_{21}v_x + a_{22}v_y + a_{23}r + b_{21}\delta + c_2, \qquad r^+ \approx a_{31}v_x + a_{32}v_y + a_{33}r + b_{31}\delta + c_3. \end{aligned}

Before the kinematics are added, the learned block has the sparsity pattern

A_{\mathrm{dyn}} = \begin{bmatrix} * & * & * & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ * & * & * & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ * & * & * & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B_{\mathrm{dyn}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & * \\ * & 0 \\ * & 0 \end{bmatrix},

the stars estimated by weighted ridge least squares: the v_x row on its own, the v_y and r rows together since they share regressors. This is a structured choice. The full plant contains more couplings; the restricted regressors reduce the parameter count and encode physical prior knowledge.

Feature design. Why does v_x^+ use acceleration but not steering, when the simulator contains -F_{y,f}\sin\delta / m? Because the restriction is an approximate structured model, not a statement of physical decoupling: fewer parameters, less data, better conditioning, the obvious actuation channel, at the price that the neglected coupling must be absorbed by the state dependence and the offset. The same logic makes the lateral rows use steering but not acceleration. Exercise 7 shows that on this car, which steers up to 0.5 rad, the neglected term is not small: adding \delta to the v_x row cuts its held-out error by a factor 3.4. Adding a_x to the lateral rows helps nothing (exercise 8).

Part 6

Analytic Frenet rows

Why not regress all six equations. The kinematics of e_\psi, s, e_y are known exactly. Learn what is hard to model, keep known physics where it is trustworthy: less data needed, more interpretable. Freezing the curvature at the query, \kappa = K(s_q), the one-step Euler models with q = v_x\cos e_\psi - v_y\sin e_\psi and d = 1 - \kappa e_y are

e_\psi^+ = e_\psi + T_s \Big( r - \kappa \frac{q}{d} \Big), \qquad s^+ = s + T_s \frac{q}{d}, \qquad e_y^+ = e_y + T_s (v_x\sin e_\psi + v_y\cos e_\psi).

The Jacobian. For the heading error,

\frac{\partial e_\psi^+}{\partial v_x} = -T_s \frac{\kappa\cos e_\psi}{d}, \quad \frac{\partial e_\psi^+}{\partial v_y} = T_s \frac{\kappa\sin e_\psi}{d}, \quad \frac{\partial e_\psi^+}{\partial r} = T_s, \quad \frac{\partial e_\psi^+}{\partial e_\psi} = 1 - T_s \frac{\kappa(-v_x\sin e_\psi - v_y\cos e_\psi)}{d}, \quad \frac{\partial e_\psi^+}{\partial e_y} = -T_s \frac{q\kappa^2}{d^2};

for the progress,

\frac{\partial s^+}{\partial v_x} = T_s \frac{\cos e_\psi}{d}, \quad \frac{\partial s^+}{\partial v_y} = -T_s \frac{\sin e_\psi}{d}, \quad \frac{\partial s^+}{\partial e_\psi} = T_s \frac{-v_x\sin e_\psi - v_y\cos e_\psi}{d}, \quad \frac{\partial s^+}{\partial e_y} = T_s \frac{q\kappa}{d^2};

and for the lateral deviation,

\frac{\partial e_y^+}{\partial v_x} = T_s\sin e_\psi, \quad \frac{\partial e_y^+}{\partial v_y} = T_s\cos e_\psi, \quad \frac{\partial e_y^+}{\partial e_\psi} = T_s(v_x\cos e_\psi - v_y\sin e_\psi), \quad \frac{\partial e_y^+}{\partial e_y} = 1.

The derivative of the curvature with respect to s is neglected (exercise 12 restores it). Affine remainder: each analytic row takes the offset c_q = f(x_q) - A_q x_q, so the affine map is exact at the linearisation point. At the notebook's query, \kappa = 1.430 and the three rows are the ones shown in the table above, with offsets (-0.0248, 0.0173, 0.0174).

Part 7

One-step validation on a different lap

Why the training residual is not enough. A local regression can fit its selected points very well and still predict poorly on new transitions. The meaningful experiment: fit only on the four training laps, query the model at every point of the held-out lap, predict one step, compare with the simulator's actual next state. Two models are compared, both with the same analytic kinematic rows: the global structured model, one set of learned coefficients from all 1216 transitions with equal weights, and the local structured model, refitted around every query. The comparison isolates the value of locality.

The local model wins on all three learned states, by the largest margin on the yaw rate, where the error drops by a factor of nearly three. No validation query needed the support fallback. The kinematic rows' errors are the same in both models by construction; their size (the heading error's 4.6 \times 10^{-2} is the largest of all six) comes from the Euler discretisation and the frozen curvature, not from the data, and is shared by any controller built on these rows.

Why local models help. For a nonlinear transition the best first-order coefficients are the Jacobians at the operating point, and they change with it. A single global affine regression averages incompatible local behaviours; a local regression estimates the derivative-like behaviour only where the MPC is currently planning to operate. Exercise 9 splits the lap by curvature and speed: locality helps everywhere, most at high speed.

Part 8

Data support, conditioning, and the exercise sweeps

Distance to the database. For every query, d_{\min}(z_q) = \min_i \|D(z_i - z_q)\|_1 is a geometric indicator of how close the query is to stored experience, not a probabilistic uncertainty. On the held-out lap larger errors are somewhat more frequent at larger distances (correlation 0.43), but the scatter is large and no monotone relationship should be expected: local nonlinearities differ, the neighbours excite the regressors more or less well, conditioning varies. Note the scale: every validation query has a stored transition within 0.29, a small fraction of h = 5, so the held-out lap tests interpolation inside familiar data.

Conditioning and ridge. Local regression keeps only nearby points, which can be nearly collinear in regressor space, so \Phi^\top W\Phi can be badly conditioned: median condition number 1.1 \times 10^{5} over the lap, 95th percentile 9.3 \times 10^{5}. Replacing it by \Phi^\top W\Phi + \lambda I improves the conditioning but biases the coefficients, so \lambda is not free accuracy; the notebook keeps 10^{-8} and makes the conditioning visible rather than hiding it behind a large \lambda. It also explains the strange coefficients of Part 5: with condition numbers near 10^{6}, coefficients far from the true derivatives fit the data almost as well as the true ones.

Part 9

The affine LTV horizon

From one local model to a sequence. An MPC horizon of length N needs (A_0, B_0, c_0), …, (A_{N-1}, B_{N-1}, c_{N-1}); the experiment uses N = 14, 1.4 s at T_s = 0.1 s. At each prediction step the local data are selected around the current predicted state and input, so the model is linear at each step but time varying across the horizon. The architecture is nonlinear simulator → data → local affine model → QP → input → nonlinear simulator; the affine model is never claimed to be the car, it is a computational surrogate inside the optimiser.

The experiment. Take a 14-step segment of the held-out lap starting at sample 145, give both models the inputs that were actually applied, and ask only how well they predict the next 14 states. This is harder than one-step prediction because errors feed back into the next query. In the notebook's segment the local LTV prediction follows the simulated v_x closely over the first steps and reproduces the sharp dip around step 8 in shape though not in depth, staying above the true speed afterwards; the global model smooths the dip away. Dynamic-state RMSE over the horizon: 0.0206 local against 0.0408 global, about half. The errors do not grow monotonically: they jump at the transient and can shrink again when the trajectories come back together, and in the last steps the two error norms are comparable (0.060 against 0.055 at step 14).

Why it is called LTV. Along the horizon \|A_k - A_0\|_F reaches 1.58 and \|B_k - B_0\|_F 0.56: the controller is not using one fixed linear model. Two mechanisms: the regression neighbourhood changes as (v_x, v_y, r, \delta, a_x) changes, and the analytic rows change with e_\psi, e_y and K(s). The variation is not smooth: each fit keeps the seven nearest transitions of every lap with almost equal weights, so a small change of the query can swap neighbours and, with nearly collinear data, move the coefficients abruptly. B_k varies only through the regression, since the kinematic rows of B are zero. Support along the horizon. One model-fit diagnostic is not enough: an MPC may start well supported and predict into a poorly supported region several steps ahead, so the nearest-data distance should be evaluated along the whole horizon. Here it never exceeds 0.20, far inside the bandwidth; a horizon point approaching or crossing it would be an explicit warning that the optimiser is planning where the identified dynamics are unsupported. The regression does not turn that distance into a formal uncertainty set; that would be a robust or learning-control extension (exercises 11 and 13).

Longer horizons (exercise 10). With N = 30 from the same start, the local model's RMSE is 0.070 against 0.042 for the global one, and its final-step error 0.30 against 0.08: once the predicted state has drifted, the local model is queried in places the true trajectory never visited, and its sharper but more variable coefficients amplify the drift. One-step accuracy does not promise long-horizon accuracy, which is one more argument for the short horizons that receding-horizon control uses anyway.

Part 10

Out of distribution, bias and variance, and the way into LMPC

An out-of-distribution query. Ask for a point with four times the speed, lateral velocity, yaw rate and acceleration ever recorded: x = (4, 1, 4, 0.2, 5, 0.2), u = (0.5, 5). Its nearest stored transition is 8.546 away in the scaled metric, beyond the bandwidth, so the kernel contains no sample of any lap; the code falls back to the seven nearest transitions per lap with a widened bandwidth and flags all four laps. The object stays inspectable, but the correct engineering conclusion is not "the model still returns a number, so it is valid"; it is that the query is extrapolative and the model's reliability unverified.

Bias and variance. A small bandwidth gives a very local fit: less nonlinear-model bias, but few points, large coefficient variance, ill-conditioning, possibly no support. A large bandwidth includes more data: less variance and better conditioning, but points from different dynamics get averaged and the model drifts towards the global affine approximation. The bandwidth is a genuine identification hyperparameter, not a speed setting; on this data it is inactive, and the number of points per lap plays its role (exercise 4 shows five points beating seven and eighty being much worse). Validation must be trajectory-based. Consecutive samples satisfy x_{k+1} \approx x_k; a random split of individual samples puts near-identical neighbours on both sides and flatters the error. A whole held-out lap is the meaningful first test for an iterative task.

How this enters LMPC. Racing LMPC learns two different things: terminal learning from previous successful laps (a local safe set and a cost-to-go, as in chapter 05) and model learning from stored transitions (the (A_k, B_k, c_k) of this chapter). One says where it is safe to end the horizon and what the continuation costs; the other says how the car will move during the horizon. A racing LMPC combines a horizon of 14, a few stored laps for the model (four here) and a few for the safe set, local safe-set point selection, the local regression, the analytic linearisation, and a QP at every control step whose input goes to the nonlinear simulator. Two details of the racing cost: there is no conventional state-tracking cost, since the objective is lap time (Q_{\mathrm{LMPC}} = 0, R_{\mathrm{LMPC}} = 0), smoothness coming from an input-rate penalty \Delta u^\top \operatorname{diag}(5, 50)\,\Delta u and the learned terminal cost counting the remaining samples to the finish; and the local safe set is sized by a few laps and points, for instance 4 \times 12 = 48 points chosen from the fastest laps around the predicted terminal point rather than the hull of everything. One geometric subtlety: s = 0 and s = L are neighbouring positions with distant coordinates, so stored states near the finish line must have their progress shifted by L before convex combinations are taken; the lane bound |e_y| \le W itself survives convex combination because an interval is convex.

Why a learned LTV model can work on a highly nonlinear car, and what that does not prove. The plant does not become linear. The approach relies on short horizons, receding-horizon feedback, regression around operating points already visited, recomputation as the operating point moves, exact kinematics where physics is reliable, and iterative data that keep the controller near progressively relevant regions. A good nominal local model does not by itself guarantee recursive feasibility under mismatch, probabilistic safety, bounded closed-loop error, protection against a sudden grip change, or safety under localisation errors. Those need robust, stochastic or adaptive constructions; LMPC racing is an effective learning-control architecture, not a formal robust-MPC certificate. Pitfalls: a local linear model does not mean the system is linear; neighbours are not selected by (s, e_y); only three equations are learned; c_k cannot be dropped; a low training residual proves nothing; a large bandwidth is not automatically safer; ridge is not free accuracy; an extrapolated model is numerically defined but empirically unvalidated.

Part 11

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question before opening it.

1. What is the main difference between the simulator and the LMPC prediction model?

The simulator propagates the nonlinear bicycle and Frenet dynamics. The controller uses a locally identified affine time-varying surrogate x^+ = A_k x + B_k u + c_k.

2. Which states are learned from data?

The next-step longitudinal velocity, lateral velocity and yaw rate: v_x^+, v_y^+, r^+.

3. Which states use analytic kinematics?

Heading error e_\psi, progress s and lateral deviation e_y.

4. What query vector selects the local data?

z = [v_x, v_y, r, \delta, a_x]^\top.

5. Why is v_x scaled by 0.1 in the metric?

To stop its numerical range and units from dominating the neighbour distance relative to the other features.

6. What kernel is used?

The Epanechnikov kernel, compact-support and quadratic, w = \tfrac34[1 - (d/h)^2] for selected points inside the bandwidth h = 5.

7. Why an affine rather than a homogeneous linear model?

The expansion point is generally nonzero, so the intercept c = f(x_q, u_q) - A_q x_q - B_q u_q is required.

8. Why only acceleration in the v_x input row?

A structured grey-box choice that reduces parameters and encodes the principal longitudinal actuation channel, even though the full model contains more coupling.

9. What makes the model LTV?

The data neighbourhood and the analytic Frenet Jacobian change along the predicted trajectory, so (A_k, B_k, c_k) vary with the prediction step.

10. Why validate on a held-out lap rather than random samples?

Time-adjacent racing data are strongly correlated; a random split leaks almost identical neighbours into both sets.

11. What does the nearest-data distance tell us?

It is a geometric support diagnostic: a large distance indicates extrapolation. It is not a calibrated probabilistic uncertainty.

12. Why can ridge regularisation help?

Local samples can make \Phi^\top W\Phi nearly singular; adding \lambda I improves conditioning but introduces bias.

13. How can a local LTV model control a nonlinear vehicle?

The approximation only needs to be useful locally over a short horizon; receding-horizon feedback and repeated model updates prevent reliance on one global approximation.

14. What are the two separate learning mechanisms in racing LMPC?

Model learning provides the finite-horizon dynamics; safe-set and value learning provide the terminal geometry and cost from successful laps.

15. Does good local prediction prove closed-loop safety?

No. Prediction validation is necessary but is not a robust or stochastic safety guarantee.

Part 12

Exercises

  1. Weighted normal equations. From \sum_i w_i(y_i - \phi_i^\top\theta)^2 + \lambda\|\theta\|_2^2, derive the normal equations.
    Hint

    Write the sum as a quadratic form and set the gradient to zero.

    Solution

    With \Phi, y, W = \operatorname{diag}(w) the objective is J(\theta) = (y - \Phi\theta)^\top W(y - \Phi\theta) + \lambda\theta^\top\theta. Its gradient is \nabla J = -2\Phi^\top W(y - \Phi\theta) + 2\lambda\theta; setting it to zero gives (\Phi^\top W\Phi + \lambda I)\theta = \Phi^\top W y. The Hessian 2(\Phi^\top W\Phi + \lambda I) is positive definite for \lambda > 0 (or \lambda = 0 and full column rank), so the stationary point is the unique minimiser.

  2. Scaling. Remove the 0.1 factor on v_x, compare the selected neighbours and explain which feature dominates.
    Hint

    The demo's last toggle does it; compare the mean deviation of the selected points in each feature.

    Solution

    On this data the change is small: at the notebook's query the mean absolute deviation of the 28 selected points is (0.064, 0.008, 0.094, 0.131, 0.061) in (v_x, v_y, r, \delta, a_x) with the 0.1 scale and (0.057, 0.008, 0.095, 0.131, 0.064) without, and the held-out RMSE moves from (0.01209, 0.00090, 0.01437) to (0.01249, 0.00090, 0.01481). The reason is that v_x spans only 0.33 m/s in these laps, comparable to the other features, so it does not dominate either way; steering, with its range of 1 rad, is the feature that decides most selections. On data with a realistic speed range of tens of m/s the unscaled v_x would dominate the distance entirely, which is what the scale prevents.

  3. Bandwidth sweep. Validate for h \in \{1, 2, 5, 10\}: RMSE, support failures, conditioning; explain the bias–variance trade-off.
    Hint

    Compare the bandwidth with the scaled spread of the data, 4.74.

    Solution

    For h = 1, 2, 5, 10 the held-out RMSE of (v_x, v_y, r) is identical to three digits, (0.0121, 0.0009, 0.0144), with no support failures and a median condition number of 1.1 \times 10^{5}; every one of the 28 selected points has distance below 0.4, so all bandwidths above that give the same selection and nearly the same weights. Only at h = 0.5 does the kernel discriminate, and the v_x error improves to 0.0100. The trade-off in general: a small h lowers the model bias but raises the coefficient variance and the condition number and risks empty neighbourhoods; a large h does the reverse and tends towards the global fit. On this data the trade-off is governed by the number of points per lap instead.

  4. Maximum points per lap. Repeat with 3, 7 and 15 points per lap. Does more data always improve the local model?
    Hint

    The demo's slider; watch the RMSE readouts and the condition numbers.

    Solution

    Held-out RMSE of (v_x, v_y, r): 3 points (0.0118, 0.00092, 0.0141), 5 points (0.0114, 0.00083, 0.0131), 7 points (0.0121, 0.00090, 0.0144), 15 points (0.0143, 0.00099, 0.0162), 30 points (0.0167, 0.0012, 0.0202), 80 points (0.0200, 0.0016, 0.0286), approaching the global model's (0.0210, 0.0019, 0.0420). No: beyond about five points the extra transitions come from different operating conditions and bias the local fit, while the median condition number falls from 1.7 \times 10^{5} to 7 \times 10^{4}. Fewer points mean a sharper but noisier model; three points per lap are already slightly worse than five. The notebook's seven is a reasonable compromise, not an optimum.

  5. Ridge regularisation. Evaluate several \lambda; relate conditioning and held-out error.
    Hint

    The ridge slider.

    Solution

    \lambda = 0 and 10^{-8} are indistinguishable, RMSE (0.0121, 0.0009, 0.0144) with median condition number 1.1 \times 10^{5}; 10^{-4} lowers the condition number to 8 \times 10^{4} and the v_x error slightly, to 0.0115; 10^{-2} brings the condition number to 3.7 \times 10^{3} but raises the errors to (0.0168, 0.0020, 0.0228); \lambda = 1 gives condition number 40 and errors three to six times the baseline; \lambda = 10 is worse still. Conditioning is bought with bias: the regressors are of order one and the coefficients are not small, so a ridge that noticeably changes the normal matrix noticeably shrinks the coefficients.

  6. Remove the affine offset. Force c = 0, compare with the affine model, and explain.
    Hint

    Which rows have a large offset in the printed model?

    Solution

    Without the offset the held-out RMSE becomes (0.0111, 0.00086, 0.0253): v_x and v_y are essentially unchanged, the yaw rate error nearly doubles. The linearisation of f at a nonzero operating point has the intercept c_q = f(x_q, u_q) - A_q x_q - B_q u_q, which at the notebook's query is (-0.18, 0.011, -0.61): large for r, whose row depends strongly on v_x and \delta through the tire law. Forcing it to zero makes the regression bend the other coefficients to absorb it, which fits the neighbours but generalises badly. For v_x the intercept happens to be absorbable because v_x itself varies little around the query.

  7. Add steering to the v_x regression. Does the extra regressor justify itself?
    Hint

    Look at the neglected term -F_{y,f}\sin\delta / m for a car that steers up to 0.5 rad.

    Solution

    Yes, clearly: the held-out v_x RMSE falls from 0.01209 to 0.00352, a factor 3.4, with the other two rows unchanged. On this RC car the front tire can deliver 7.8 N on a 1.98 kg body, and at \delta = 0.5 the longitudinal component of that force is a deceleration of up to 7.8 \sin 0.5 / 1.98 \approx 1.9 m/s², larger than most recorded accelerations, so the structured row without steering is missing its biggest disturbance. The notebook's structure follows the published controller, where it is a reasonable simplification for the data it was designed for; here the data say the extra parameter is worth having.

  8. Add acceleration to the lateral rows. Physical coupling against data efficiency.
    Hint

    Where does a_x enter the lateral dynamics in the bicycle model?

    Solution

    Adding a_x to the v_y and r rows changes the held-out RMSE from (0.00090, 0.0144) to (0.0010, 0.0158): slightly worse. In the model, a_x does not appear in \dot v_y or \dot r at all; it acts on them only through v_x at later steps, which the regressor v_x already captures. The extra column is noise to be fitted, and with 28 nearly collinear samples an extra parameter costs more variance than it buys bias. The opposite of exercise 7: structure should follow the physics of each row.

  9. Global against local. Compare both models at low and high curvature, low and high speed.
    Hint

    The table of Part 7 splits the held-out lap at the medians.

    Solution

    Dynamic-state error norms (local, global): low curvature 0.0174 against 0.0491, high curvature 0.0201 against 0.0449, low speed 0.0213 against 0.0422, high speed 0.0159 against 0.0514. Locality helps everywhere, by a factor 2 to 3.2; most at high speed and on the straights, where the global average is furthest from the local behaviour, and least at low speed. Note that the local error is larger in the corners than on the straights (the transients are faster there), while the global error is larger on the straights: the two models fail in different places.

  10. Multi-step horizon. Repeat for N = 5, 14, 30; why does one-step accuracy not predict long-horizon accuracy?
    Hint

    The horizon widget's slider.

    Solution

    From sample 145: N = 5, RMSE 0.0082 local against 0.0301 global; N = 14, 0.0206 against 0.0408; N = 30, 0.0696 against 0.0422, with final-step errors 0.30 against 0.08. The local model is far better over short horizons and worse over long ones. Each prediction error moves the next query to a state the true trajectory never visited; the local model then fits neighbours of a fictitious state, and its sharp, high-variance coefficients amplify the drift, whereas the global model's averaged coefficients drift slowly. One-step accuracy measures the fit at true states; long-horizon accuracy measures the fit along the model's own trajectory, a different question. Receding-horizon control refits from the measured state every step, which is why short horizons are the right regime for this model.

  11. Data-support monitor. Design a rule that flags a prediction if any horizon point has d_{\min} > d_{\mathrm{warn}}. What should the controller do?
    Hint

    What does the fallback already do, and what is a safe action for a racing car?

    Solution

    After solving the QP, roll the planned inputs through the local model, compute d_{\min} at every predicted (x_k, u_k) and raise a warning if any exceeds d_{\mathrm{warn}}, chosen well below the bandwidth, say 1 on this data where supported queries stay below 0.3. On a warning the controller should not trust the plan beyond the first unsupported step: it can tighten the input-rate penalty or the input bounds so that the plan stays near recorded behaviour, shorten the horizon to the supported prefix, add a cost term proportional to the support distance, or fall back to the previous lap's inputs from that point, which are known to be feasible. What it must not do is execute an aggressive plan whose only justification is coefficients fitted to nothing nearby; and the warning should also trigger data collection in that region on the next laps.

  12. Curvature derivative. Derive the terms that appear if dK/ds is included in the kinematic linearisation.
    Hint

    \kappa becomes a function of s; differentiate e_\psi^+ and s^+ with respect to s.

    Solution

    With \kappa = K(s), the column for s, which is zero in the rows for e_\psi and e_y and one in the row for s, acquires extra terms through \partial\kappa/\partial s = K'(s): \partial e_\psi^+/\partial s = -T_s K'(s)\,\partial(\kappa q/d)/\partial\kappa = -T_s K'(s)\, q / d^2, since \partial(\kappa/d)/\partial\kappa = (d + \kappa e_y)/d^2 = 1/d^2; and \partial s^+/\partial s = 1 + T_s K'(s)\, q e_y / d^2. The e_y row is unchanged. The offsets change accordingly so the map stays exact at the query. On this track K' is large at the corner entries (the curvature rises from near zero to 1.6 within about a metre), so the neglected terms are not negligible there, but they only matter for the one-step prediction of e_\psi and s, which is refitted at every step anyway.

  13. Uncertainty model. Estimate a local covariance of the model error from held-out residuals; how could it be used?
    Hint

    Pool the residuals of the neighbours used in the fit.

    Solution

    For a query, take the residuals \epsilon_i = x_i^+ - (A_q x_i + B_q u_i + c_q) of the selected transitions, or better of held-out transitions near the query, and form the weighted covariance \Sigma_q = \sum_i w_i \epsilon_i\epsilon_i^\top / \sum_i w_i for the three learned rows (the kinematic rows add the discretisation error, which can be estimated the same way). In stochastic MPC it defines the disturbance of x^+ = A x + B u + c + \epsilon and turns the lane constraint into a chance constraint tightened by a multiple of the predicted standard deviation along the horizon; in tube-based robust MPC a bound such as \|\Sigma_q^{-1/2}\epsilon\| \le \beta gives the disturbance set that the tube must absorb. Either way the covariance should grow with the support distance, since residuals measured in supported regions say nothing about unsupported ones; that is the honest link between Part 10's warning and a constraint the optimiser can use.

  14. Error-dynamics learning. With a nominal physics model available, regress the residual e_i = x_{i+1} - f_{\mathrm{nom}}(x_i, u_i) instead of the full dynamics. Why does this reduce the learning burden?
    Hint

    What is left to learn if the nominal model is good?

    Solution

    Linearise the nominal model at the query, f_{\mathrm{nom}} \approx A^{\mathrm{nom}}_q x + B^{\mathrm{nom}}_q u + c^{\mathrm{nom}}_q, and fit the same local weighted regression to the residuals, e \approx \tilde A_q x + \tilde B_q u + \tilde c_q; the prediction model is the sum. If the nominal model is good, the residual is small and smooth, its coefficients are near zero, and the regression needs fewer points, tolerates a larger bandwidth or more ridge without harm, and degrades gracefully to the physics model where data are missing, which also answers the extrapolation problem of Part 10: outside the data the correction is simply switched off. The learning burden is the mismatch, not the whole dynamics. The price is that a wrong nominal model biases every prediction, so the residual must be validated like everything else.

Part 13

The chapter on one page

  1. Two systems: a nonlinear bicycle simulator, and an affine surrogate x^+ = A_k x + B_k u + c_k inside the QP.
  2. Data: a 20 m RC-car track, four noisy PID laps (1216 transitions) for fitting, one lap held out.
  3. Metric: \|D(z_i - z_q)\|_1 on (v_x, v_y, r, \delta, a_x), Epanechnikov weights, seven nearest points per lap.
  4. Regression: (\Phi^\top W\Phi + \lambda I)\theta = \Phi^\top Wy with an affine constant; \lambda = 10^{-8} as a safeguard only.
  5. Structure: v_x^+ from (v_x, v_y, r, a_x), v_y^+, r^+ from (v_x, v_y, r, \delta); kinematic rows exact.
  6. Validation: held-out one-step RMSE (0.0121, 0.0009, 0.0144) local against (0.0210, 0.0019, 0.0420) global.
  7. Diagnostics: support distance below 0.29 on the lap, condition numbers near 10^{5}; coefficients are not derivatives.
  8. Horizon: 14 steps, RMSE 0.021 against 0.041; matrices vary along the horizon; at 30 steps the local model loses.
  9. Limits: the out-of-distribution query at distance 8.5 triggers the fallback on every lap; the number is not a prediction.
  10. Into LMPC: model learning for the horizon, safe-set and value learning for the terminal; no robustness certificate comes with it.

Scope. The prediction model of a Learning MPC racing controller, built and validated as in the published method: identification laps, local kernel-weighted structured regression, analytic kinematics, trajectory-based validation, support and conditioning diagnostics. The closed-loop controller itself is not implemented; Part 10 says how the model enters it. The laps are generated in Python and shipped to this page because their random dither cannot be reproduced bit for bit in a browser; every fit, validation and sweep is recomputed here and agrees with the notebook to round-off.

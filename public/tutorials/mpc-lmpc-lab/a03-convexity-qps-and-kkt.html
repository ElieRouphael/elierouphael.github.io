Convexity, quadratic programs and the KKT conditions

Optimisation is the engine under predictive control, and the quadratic program of chapter A2 is handed to a solver at every sample. Before trusting what comes back, one has to know what makes such a problem tractable, what a solution certifies, and what the extra numbers a solver returns, the multipliers, mean. This chapter is the geometry and the optimality theory: convex sets and functions, the Lagrangian and its dual, and the four KKT conditions, each read on a two-variable problem small enough to solve by hand. Below, that problem is solved live: move the target and the bound and watch the multiplier appear, as a force and as a price.

Part 1

Optimisation as geometry

The generic constrained problem. A broad class of problems reads

\min_{z \in \mathbb R^n}\ f(z) \quad \text{subject to}\quad g_i(z) \le 0,\ i = 1, \dots, m, \qquad h_j(z) = 0,\ j = 1, \dots, p.

z holds the decision variables; in chapter A2 it is the whole input sequence U, or (X, U). The feasible set is \mathcal F = \{z : g_i(z) \le 0,\ h_j(z) = 0\}; an optimiser is a point z^\star \in \arg\min_{z \in \mathcal F} f(z), and the optimal value is the scalar f^\star = f(z^\star). The distinction matters: a point can be feasible without being optimal, and a problem can be infeasible, unbounded, or have several minimisers.

What is a convex set?

In plain words: a set with no dents and no holes, so that the straight segment between any two of its points stays inside. Boxes, half-planes, discs and their intersections are convex; an L-shape, a ring, or two separate pieces are not.

More precisely: \mathcal C \subseteq \mathbb R^n is convex if \theta x + (1 - \theta) y \in \mathcal C for every x, y \in \mathcal C and every \theta \in [0, 1]. The sets that recur in MPC are all convex: the affine set \{z : Az = b\}, the half-space \{z : a^\top z \le b\}, and the polyhedron \{z : Gz \le h\}, an intersection of finitely many half-spaces; a bounded polyhedron is a polytope, chapter A8.

Intersection
An intersection of convex sets is convex: stacking the rows of many constraints keeps the feasible set convex.
Affine image
\{Mz + c : z \in \mathcal C\} is convex when \mathcal C is: the prediction identity maps a convex set of inputs to a convex set of states.
Cartesian product
\mathcal C_1 \times \mathcal C_2 is convex: a box on every state and an interval on every input together form one convex set over the horizon.

Why convexity matters geometrically. A convex feasible set has no disconnected islands and no pockets a search can get trapped in. That alone does not make optimisation easy, the objective matters too, but it is the geometry the theory needs, and the closure properties in the box are why stacking many linear state and input constraints over a horizon, as chapter A2 does, still yields a convex feasible set.

Part 2

Convex functions and the quadratic

A function f on a convex domain is convex if

f(\theta x + (1 - \theta) y) \le \theta f(x) + (1 - \theta) f(y) \qquad \text{for all } x, y \text{ and } \theta \in [0, 1]:

the chord lies above the graph. For a differentiable convex function, f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x): every tangent plane is a global lower bound, which is what makes a zero gradient a global certificate. For a twice differentiable function, \nabla^2 f(z) \succeq 0 everywhere is sufficient. For the quadratic f(z) = \tfrac12 z^\top P z + q^\top z + r the Hessian is just P, so

\boxed{f \text{ is convex} \iff P \succeq 0}, \qquad \text{and strictly convex if } P \succ 0.

The widget's family P = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & \alpha \\ \alpha & 1 \end{pmatrix} has trace 3 and determinant 2 - \alpha^2: convex exactly for |\alpha| \le \sqrt 2, with eigenvalues 0.0134 and 2.987 at \alpha = 1.4 and -0.081 and 3.081 at \alpha = 1.5, where the ellipses open into a saddle. The condensed Hessian of chapter A2 is positive definite for every horizon, so the MPC cost is always strictly convex.

Convex optimisation problems. The standard form is \min_z f_0(z) subject to f_i(z) \le 0 and Az = b, with f_0 and every f_i convex and the equalities affine [1]. The consequence that makes it valuable:

\boxed{\text{every local optimum is a global optimum,}}

and if the objective is strictly convex on the feasible set, there is at most one optimiser. Convexity turns "search for a good local solution" into "solve a problem with global optimality structure". It is not elegance for its own sake: it decides what can be solved reliably online, at every sampling instant, with a bound on the time it takes.

Part 3

Problem classes

The structure of the problem determines the solver, and the solver's name matters less than knowing what it is given.

Structure Class Where the course meets it
Linear objective, linear constraints LP The feasibility certificate of chapter A4, the learned terminal value of chapter A13
Convex quadratic objective, linear constraints Convex QP Every linear MPC problem: chapters A5, A6, A11, A13
Indefinite quadratic objective Nonconvex QP Never, by design: R \succ 0 keeps H \succ 0
Nonlinear smooth objective or constraints NLP The unicycle of chapter A5, the raceline of chapter B3
Integer or binary decisions MILP, MIQP The yield-or-pass choice of chapters A15 and B1

A nonlinear equality h(z) = 0 generally describes a curved set, which is why the standard convex form requires the equalities to be affine; the dynamics of a nonlinear model are exactly such equalities, and that is the moment a control problem stops being convex.

Part 4

Optimality, duality and the KKT conditions

Unconstrained optimality. For \min_z f(z), a differentiable local optimum in the interior satisfies \nabla f(z^\star) = 0. For a convex differentiable f that is not merely local: it is sufficient for global optimality. For a strictly convex quadratic, z^\star = -P^{-1} q is the unique minimiser.

The Lagrangian. For \min f(z) subject to g(z) \le 0 and h(z) = 0, introduce multipliers \lambda \ge 0 and \nu \in \mathbb R^p and define

\boxed{L(z, \lambda, \nu) = f(z) + \lambda^\top g(z) + \nu^\top h(z)}.

The multipliers quantify how strongly the active constraints shape the optimum. In control, a multiplier on an input bound says how costly that limit is, one on a state boundary how strongly it bends the trajectory, and a zero multiplier says the inequality is locally irrelevant. Chapter A5 reads them off a complete control problem.

The dual function and weak duality. The Lagrange dual function is d(\lambda, \nu) = \inf_z L(z, \lambda, \nu). For any \lambda \ge 0 and any feasible z, L(z, \lambda, \nu) \le f(z), so

d(\lambda, \nu) \le f^\star :

every dual-feasible point is a lower bound on the primal optimum. The dual problem \max_{\lambda \ge 0, \nu} d(\lambda, \nu) is concave whatever the primal is; if its optimum d^\star equals f^\star we have strong duality. For convex problems a standard sufficient condition is Slater's: a feasible point exists that satisfies every non-affine inequality strictly. For a QP with affine constraints strong duality holds whenever the problem is feasible. With it, primal and dual solutions certify each other through the gap f(z) - d(\lambda, \nu), which the interior-point method of chapter A4 drives to zero.

What are the KKT conditions?

In plain words: the four facts that hold at the solution of a constrained problem: the objective's pull is exactly balanced by the constraints that are active; the point is feasible; the constraints only push, never pull; and a constraint that is not touched exerts no force at all.

More precisely: under the usual differentiability assumptions, and for convex problems with a constraint qualification such as Slater's, an optimum satisfies

\begin{aligned} &\text{1. stationarity: } \nabla f(z^\star) + \textstyle\sum_{i} \lambda_i^\star \nabla g_i(z^\star) + \sum_{j} \nu_j^\star \nabla h_j(z^\star) = 0, \\ &\text{2. primal feasibility: } g_i(z^\star) \le 0,\ h_j(z^\star) = 0, \qquad \text{3. dual feasibility: } \lambda_i^\star \ge 0, \\ &\text{4. complementary slackness: } \boxed{\lambda_i^\star g_i(z^\star) = 0}. \end{aligned}

For differentiable convex problems the KKT conditions are also sufficient: any point satisfying them is a global optimum, with no further assumption. A constraint qualification is what makes them necessary as well.

Active constraint
An inequality that holds with equality at the optimum; it may carry a positive multiplier.
Complementary slackness
If g_i(z^\star) < 0 then \lambda_i^\star = 0; if \lambda_i^\star > 0 then g_i(z^\star) = 0. Both can be zero at once: a weakly active constraint.
Multiplier of an equality
\nu_j has no sign restriction, since an equality pushes from both sides.

A QP solved by hand. Minimise \lVert z - c \rVert_2^2 with c = (1.2, 0.8) subject to z_1 + z_2 \le 1. The unconstrained minimiser is c, but 1.2 + 0.8 = 2 > 1, so the constraint must be active. With a = (1, 1), stationarity reads 2(z - c) + \lambda a = 0, so z = c - \tfrac{\lambda}{2} a. Since the inequality is active, a^\top z = 1, hence 2 - \lambda = 1 and

\lambda^\star = 1, \qquad \boxed{z^\star = (0.7,\ 0.3)}, \qquad f^\star = 0.5 .

The gradient of the objective at z^\star is (-1, -1) = -\lambda^\star a: the objective would like to move along (1, 1), straight out of the half-plane, and the constraint's normal holds it back with exactly the force \lambda^\star. That is stationarity, and the hero draws the two arrows cancelling. The course's solver returns (0.7, 0.3) and \lambda = 1 with every residual at zero, in ten iterations.

The dual of a strictly convex QP. For \min \tfrac12 z^\top P z + q^\top z subject to Az \le b with P \succ 0, minimising the Lagrangian over z gives z(\lambda) = -P^{-1}(q + A^\top \lambda) and

\boxed{d(\lambda) = -\tfrac12 (q + A^\top \lambda)^\top P^{-1} (q + A^\top \lambda) - b^\top \lambda},

to be maximised over \lambda \ge 0. For the example, with P = 2I and q = -2c, the dual maximum is -1.58 at \lambda = 1, the primal optimum with the constant c^\top c dropped is 0.5 - 2.08 = -1.58, and the gap is zero: strong duality, as the hero's right panel shows for every c and b.

Part 5

Multipliers as prices

Replace the bound 1 by a parameter b. While the constraint stays active, \lambda^\star(b) = 2 - b and f^\star(b) = \lambda^{\star 2} / 2, so

\frac{d f^\star}{d b} = -\lambda^\star :

the multiplier is a shadow price, how much the optimal cost would improve if the constraint were relaxed by one unit. The numbers script differentiates f^\star(b) numerically and finds -1.200, -1.000, -0.500, -0.100 at b = 0.8, 1.0, 1.5, 1.9, the multipliers exactly, and 0 at b = 2.1 where the constraint has gone inactive and \lambda^\star = 0. The same holds for every constraint of every QP in the course: when chapter A5 reports that the first rate bound has multiplier 9.85, it means that relaxing that bound by \delta would lower the cost by about 9.85\,\delta, and chapter B6 uses the same reading to size penalties.

Two constraints, one active. Add z_1 - z_2 \le 2 to the hand problem. The solver returns the same z^\star = (0.7, 0.3), multipliers (1, 0) and slacks (0, 1.6): the second row is not touched, so its multiplier is zero, which is complementary slackness read in the other direction. Replace the inequality z_1 + z_2 \le 1 by the equality z_1 + z_2 = 1 and the solver returns \nu = 1: the same force, now from a constraint that could have pushed either way.

Hard and soft constraints. A hard constraint a^\top z \le b must always hold. When feasibility matters more than exact enforcement, add a slack \epsilon \ge 0, write a^\top z \le b + \epsilon and penalise it, f + \rho \epsilon: a soft constraint. The multiplier says how large \rho must be: for \rho \ge \lambda^\star the slack stays at zero and the soft problem reproduces the hard solution, which chapter A5 demonstrates. Which constraints are hard is an engineering decision: actuator limits always; collision constraints should be; comfort envelopes may be softened.

Part 6

The code behind it

The package's solver, chapter A4, is built on the KKT conditions. Its residuals are the four conditions written as vectors, which is all this chapter needs from it.

advanced_mpc_lmpc/qp.py · solve_qp

def solve_qp(H, f=None, A_ub=None, b_ub=None, A_eq=None, b_eq=None,
             lb=None, ub=None, x0=None, tol=1e-9, maxiter=100, sigma=0.1, trace=None):
    """Solve the convex QP by a primal-dual interior-point method.

    H must be positive semidefinite (it is symmetrised).  `x0` is accepted for
    API compatibility and used as the starting primal point after projection
    into the bounds; an interior-point method does not need a warm start.

    The iteration solves the perturbed KKT system
        H z + f + G' lam + E' nu = 0,      G z + s = g,   s > 0,  lam > 0,
        E z = e,                           s_i lam_i = sigma * mu,
    by Newton's method, with the step shortened so that s and lam stay
    positive, and sigma = 0.1 pulling the complementarity products towards
    zero.  It stops when every residual is below `tol`, then polishes the
    answer: the constraints the iterate presses against (lam_i > s_i) are
    taken as the active set and the equality-constrained QP on that set is
    solved exactly; the polished point is kept if it is feasible and its
    multipliers are non-negative, which brings the solution to round-off.
    If `trace` is a list, every iterate (z, s, lam, residual, mu) is appended to it.
    """
    H = np.asarray(H, float)
    H = 0.5 * (H + H.T)
    n = H.shape[0]
    f = np.zeros(n) if f is None else np.asarray(f, float).reshape(n)
    G, g = stack_inequalities(n, A_ub, b_ub, lb, ub)
    if A_eq is not None:
        E = np.asarray(A_eq, float).reshape(-1, n); e = np.asarray(b_eq, float).reshape(-1)
    else:
        E = np.zeros((0, n)); e = np.zeros(0)
    m, p = len(g), len(e)

    # starting point: z inside the bounds, slacks and multipliers strictly positive
    z = np.zeros(n) if x0 is None else np.asarray(x0, float).reshape(n).copy()
    if lb is not None:
        lo = np.asarray(lb, float).reshape(-1); z = np.where(np.isfinite(lo), np.maximum(z, lo), z)
    if ub is not None:
        hi = np.asarray(ub, float).reshape(-1); z = np.where(np.isfinite(hi), np.minimum(z, hi), z)
    lam = np.ones(m)
    s = np.maximum(g - G @ z, 1.0)
    nu = np.zeros(p)

    nit, resid, mu = 0, np.inf, 0.0
    for nit in range(1, maxiter + 1):
        rd = H @ z + f + G.T @ lam + E.T @ nu          # dual residual: stationarity of the Lagrangian
        rp = G @ z + s - g                             # primal residual of the inequalities
        re = E @ z - e                                 # primal residual of the equalities
        mu = (s @ lam) / m if m else 0.0               # average complementarity s_i * lam_i
        rc = sigma * mu - s * lam                      # centred complementarity residual
        resid = max(np.abs(rd).max(initial=0.0), np.abs(rp).max(initial=0.0), np.abs(re).max(initial=0.0))
        if trace is not None:
            trace.append((z.copy(), s.copy(), lam.copy(), resid, mu))
        if resid < tol and mu < tol:
            break
        # Newton step on the reduced system: s and lam are eliminated, leaving (dz, dnu)
        D = lam / s
        M = np.zeros((n + p, n + p))
        M[:n, :n] = H + G.T @ (D[:, None] * G)
        M[:n, n:] = E.T
        M[n:, :n] = E
        t = (rc + lam * rp) / s
        rhs = np.r_[-rd - G.T @ t, -re]
        try:
            sol = np.linalg.solve(M, rhs)
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            sol = np.linalg.lstsq(M + 1e-12 * np.eye(n + p), rhs, rcond=None)[0]
        dz, dnu = sol[:n], sol[n:]
        ds = -rp - G @ dz
        dl = (rc - lam * ds) / s
        # fraction-to-the-boundary rule: keep s and lam strictly positive
        a = 1.0
        if m:
            neg_s, neg_l = ds < 0, dl < 0
            if neg_s.any(): a = min(a, float(np.min(-0.99 * s[neg_s] / ds[neg_s])))
            if neg_l.any(): a = min(a, float(np.min(-0.99 * lam[neg_l] / dl[neg_l])))
        zn, sn, ln, nn = z + a * dz, s + a * ds, lam + a * dl, nu + a * dnu
        if not (np.all(np.isfinite(zn)) and np.all(np.isfinite(sn)) and np.all(np.isfinite(ln)) and np.all(np.isfinite(nn))):
            break                                      # keep the last finite iterate for the polish
        z, s, lam, nu = zn, sn, ln, nn
        if a < 1e-12:
            break                                      # stalled at the boundary (a degenerate optimum): polish

    # polish: solve the equality-constrained QP on the active set, accept it if it is feasible and dual feasible.
    # It runs whenever the primal residuals are small, even if the dual ones are not: when more constraints are
    # active than there are variables (a degenerate optimum) the multipliers are not unique and the interior
    # point drifts among them, so the polish recovers one set by least squares, then non-negative least squares.
    prim = max((np.abs(G @ z + s - g).max() if m else 0.0), (np.abs(E @ z - e).max() if p else 0.0))
    if np.all(np.isfinite(z)) and prim < 1e-6:
        act = np.where((lam > s) | (s <= 1e-7 * (1.0 + np.abs(g))))[0] if m else np.zeros(0, int)   # pressed against, or touched
        Ga = G[act]
        k = len(act)
        Mp = np.zeros((n + k + p, n + k + p))
        Mp[:n, :n] = H; Mp[:n, n:n + k] = Ga.T; Mp[n:n + k, :n] = Ga
        Mp[:n, n + k:] = E.T; Mp[n + k:, :n] = E
        sol = np.linalg.lstsq(Mp, np.r_[-f, g[act], e], rcond=None)[0]
        zp, lp, nup = sol[:n], sol[n:n + k], sol[n + k:]
        if np.all(np.isfinite(zp)) and (not m or np.max(G @ zp - g) <= 1e-9) and (not p or np.max(np.abs(E @ zp - e)) <= 1e-9):
            grad = H @ zp + f
            if np.any(lp < -1e-9) or np.abs(grad + Ga.T @ lp + E.T @ nup).max(initial=0.0) > 1e-8:
                from scipy.optimize import nnls
                C = np.hstack([Ga.T, E.T, -E.T]) if (k or p) else np.zeros((n, 0))
                coef = nnls(C, -grad)[0] if C.shape[1] else np.zeros(0)
                lp, nup = coef[:k], coef[k:k + p] - coef[k + p:]
            rd = grad + Ga.T @ lp + E.T @ nup
            if np.all(lp >= -1e-9) and np.abs(rd).max(initial=0.0) <= 1e-7:
                z, nu = zp, nup
                lam = np.zeros(m); lam[act] = np.maximum(lp, 0.0)
                s = g - G @ z
                resid = max(np.abs(rd).max(initial=0.0), (np.abs(E @ z - e).max() if p else 0.0))
                mu = 0.0

    viol = [0.0]
    if m: viol.append(float(np.max(G @ z - g)))
    if p: viol.append(float(np.max(np.abs(E @ z - e))))
    max_v = max(viol)
    converged = bool(resid < 10 * tol and mu < 10 * tol and max_v <= 1e-7)
    message = "KKT conditions satisfied" if converged else f"not converged: residual {resid:.1e}, violation {max_v:.1e}"
    return QPResult(z, converged, float(0.5 * z @ H @ z + f @ z), message, max_v, nit, lam, nu, float(resid))

Read only the five lines after for nit in range(...). rd = H @ z + f + G.T @ lam + E.T @ nu is condition 1, stationarity, for the QP: the gradient Hz + f plus each active row's normal times its multiplier. rp = G @ z + s - g is condition 2 for the inequalities with a slack s = g - Gz that must stay positive, and re = E @ z - e for the equalities. Condition 3 is not a residual but an invariant: the fraction-to-the-boundary step keeps lam > 0 and s > 0 at every iterate. Condition 4, complementary slackness s_i \lambda_i = 0, is approached rather than imposed: mu is the average product and rc the residual of s_i \lambda_i = \sigma \mu, driven to zero as \mu shrinks. The method is therefore a Newton iteration on the KKT system, and kkt_residual in the result is the largest of those residuals at the end: the number to look at before trusting a solution. The rest of the function is chapter A4.

Part 7

Common pitfalls

  1. "A zero gradient is a minimum." Only for a convex function. For the indefinite quadratic of the widget the origin has zero gradient and is a saddle; the function is unbounded below.
  2. "A positive multiplier means the constraint is violated." It means the constraint is active and pushing: the optimum sits on it. A violated constraint is infeasibility, which no multiplier describes.
  3. "An inactive constraint can be deleted." Only at this x_0. In MPC the right-hand sides move with the state, and a row that is slack now binds later; the solver must see all of them.
  4. "The dual is just a trick." Its value is a certificate: any \lambda \ge 0 bounds the optimum from below, and a zero gap proves optimality without trusting the solver's own word.
  5. "Solver converged, so the answer is optimal." For a convex problem, yes, if the KKT residuals are small; check them. For a nonconvex one convergence certifies a local optimum at best, chapter A5.
Part 8

Check your understanding

Try to answer each question before opening it.

1. What makes a quadratic program convex?

A positive semidefinite Hessian and a convex feasible set. With affine constraints this reduces to P \succeq 0; the MPC Hessian satisfies H \succ 0 because R \succ 0.

2. Why must equality constraints be affine in the standard convex form?

A nonlinear equality h(z) = 0 describes a curved, in general nonconvex set. An affine equality preserves convexity; nonlinear dynamics do not.

3. Why is "every local optimum is global" so valuable online?

Because a solver can stop as soon as it finds a stationary point and the result is the best plan, with a predictable amount of work: what a controller that must answer every sample needs.

4. What does complementary slackness tell you?

\lambda_i^\star g_i(z^\star) = 0 for each inequality: an inactive inequality has zero multiplier, and a positive multiplier implies the constraint is active.

5. What is the engineering meaning of a Lagrange multiplier?

A local shadow price: the sensitivity of the optimal value to a small relaxation of its constraint, df^\star / db = -\lambda^\star.

6. What is the difference between weak and strong duality?

Weak duality always holds: d^\star \le f^\star. Strong duality is equality, so the dual optimum certifies the primal one; it holds for every feasible QP with affine constraints.

7. Why does the sign of \nu not matter but the sign of \lambda does?

An equality can be violated in two directions and its multiplier balances the gradient from either side; an inequality only pushes inward, so its multiplier is nonnegative.

8. Why is the KKT system of a QP almost linear?

Stationarity and feasibility are linear in (z, \lambda, \nu, s); only complementary slackness, the products s_i \lambda_i, is not. That one nonlinearity is what an interior-point method handles.

9. How large must a soft-constraint penalty be to change nothing?

At least the constraint's multiplier in the hard problem. Below it the optimiser buys a violation; at or above it the slack stays at zero.

  3. Build the KKT check yourself. Write a function that takes (H, f, G, g) and a candidate (z, \lambda) and returns the four KKT residuals. Run it on the solver's answer to the hero problem and on the wrong point z = c with \lambda = 0.

    Hint

    Stationarity is Hz + f + G^\top \lambda; primal feasibility is \max(Gz - g, 0); dual feasibility \max(-\lambda, 0); complementary slackness \lambda \odot (Gz - g).

    Solution 
    def kkt_residuals(H, f, G, g, z, lam):
    G, g = np.atleast_2d(G), np.atleast_1d(g)
    return dict(stationarity=np.abs(H @ z + f + G.T @ lam).max(),
                primal=np.maximum(G @ z - g, 0).max(), dual=np.maximum(-lam, 0).max(),
                complementarity=np.abs(lam * (G @ z - g)).max())
H, f, G, g = 2 * np.eye(2), -2 * np.array([1.2, 0.8]), [[1.0, 1.0]], [1.0]
r = solve_qp(H, f, A_ub=G, b_ub=g)
print(kkt_residuals(H, f, G, g, r.x, r.lam))                         # all zero
print(kkt_residuals(H, f, G, g, np.array([1.2, 0.8]), np.zeros(1)))  # primal 1.0: the unconstrained point is infeasible

    The second call shows the unconstrained minimiser failing exactly one condition, primal feasibility, by a^\top c - b = 1.

  4. Shadow prices. Compute f^\star(b) for b from 0.5 to 2.5 in steps of 0.1, differentiate it numerically, and compare with -\lambda^\star(b). Where does the derivative jump?

    Hint

    \lambda^\star(b) = \max(2 - b, 0); the derivative is continuous but its slope changes at b = 2.

    Solution

    For b < 2 the constraint is active, f^\star = (2 - b)^2 / 2 and the derivative is -(2 - b) = -\lambda^\star; for b \ge 2 the constraint is inactive and f^\star = 0. At b = 2 the derivative is continuous (zero from both sides) but f^\star stops curving: the kink is in the second derivative, which is where the active set changes.

    def fstar(b): r = solve_qp(H, f, A_ub=G, b_ub=[b]); return float((r.x - np.array([1.2, 0.8])) @ (r.x - np.array([1.2, 0.8])))
for b in np.arange(0.5, 2.6, 0.5):
    print(b, (fstar(b + 1e-6) - fstar(b - 1e-6)) / 2e-6, -max(2 - b, 0))

  5. The unconstrained minimiser. For P = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0.8 \\ 0.8 & 1.5 \end{pmatrix} and q = (-1, 2), find the minimiser of \tfrac12 z^\top P z + q^\top z and verify that the gradient vanishes there. Why is it unique?

    Hint

    \nabla f = Pz + q.

    Solution

    z^\star = -P^{-1} q = (0.8031, -1.7617), where the gradient is zero to round-off and the value is -2.163. It is unique because P \succ 0 (eigenvalues 1.153 and 3.347): the function is strictly convex, so the stationary point is its only minimiser.

    P5 = np.array([[3.0, 0.8], [0.8, 1.5]]); q5 = np.array([-1.0, 2.0])
z5 = -np.linalg.solve(P5, q5); print(z5, P5 @ z5 + q5)

  6. A nonconvex union. The set \{z : z_1 \le -1\} \cup \{z : z_1 \ge 1\} is a union of two convex sets. Show it is not convex, and explain why "stay on the near side or on the far side of a crossing" cannot be a single linear constraint.

    Hint

    Take one point in each piece and look at their midpoint.

    Solution

    (-1, 0) and (1, 0) are both in the set; their midpoint (0, 0) is not. A convex combination of feasible points is infeasible, so the set is not convex, and no finite set of linear inequalities describes it, since an intersection of half-spaces is convex. The choice between the two pieces is a discrete decision, which chapter A15 encodes with a binary variable.

Part 10

The chapter on one page

  1. Geometry: feasible set, optimiser, optimal value; convex sets have no islands, and half-spaces, polyhedra, their intersections, images and products are convex.
  2. Convex functions: chords above the graph, tangents below; a quadratic is convex iff P \succeq 0, which for \begin{pmatrix} 2 & \alpha \\ \alpha & 1 \end{pmatrix} means |\alpha| \le \sqrt 2.
  3. Why it matters: every local optimum of a convex problem is global, with at most one optimiser when the objective is strictly convex.
  4. Classes: LP, convex QP, NLP, mixed-integer; the course's MPC problems are convex QPs because R \succ 0.
  5. Duality: d(\lambda, \nu) \le f^\star always; equality for feasible QPs; the gap certifies a solution.
  6. KKT: stationarity, primal and dual feasibility, complementary slackness; necessary with a constraint qualification, sufficient for convex problems.
  7. By hand: z^\star = (0.7, 0.3), \lambda^\star = 1, f^\star = 0.5, gap zero; the gradient and \lambda^\star a cancel.
  8. Prices: df^\star / db = -\lambda^\star, numerically -1.000 at b = 1; a soft penalty needs \rho \ge \lambda^\star.

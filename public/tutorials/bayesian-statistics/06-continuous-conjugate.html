Continuous data

A lab weighs a sample on a scale whose readings wobble by about 0.3 g. The supplier says the sample weighs 10.0 g, give or take 0.5 g. Every reading adds information, and in this chapter "information" gets a precise meaning: precision, one over the variance. Precisions simply add. Part 1

Waiting times

The model. Waiting times y_1, \dots, y_n between events that happen at an unknown rate \lambda, with a gamma prior on the rate:

y_i \mid \lambda \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Exponential}(\lambda), \qquad \lambda \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta).

The likelihood is \prod_i \lambda e^{-\lambda y_i} = \lambda^n e^{-\lambda \sum y_i}, so

p(\lambda \mid y) \propto \lambda^n e^{-\lambda\sum y_i} \cdot \lambda^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta\lambda} = \lambda^{\alpha+n-1} e^{-(\beta + \sum y_i)\lambda},
\boxed{\;\lambda \mid y \sim \text{Gamma}\Big(\alpha + n,\ \beta + \sum_i y_i\Big)\;}

Add one to the shape for each wait, and add the total time waited to the rate.

Compare with the Poisson-gamma model of chapter 05: there the shape counted events and the rate counted periods; here the shape counts events (one per wait) and the rate adds up the time. They are two views of the same process: counting events in fixed time, or timing the gaps between them.

Example. A bus is scheduled "every 10 minutes", so we expect a rate of about 0.1 buses per minute, but we are not sure how reliable the timetable is. A Gamma(4, 40) prior has mean 0.1 and is worth 4 waits totalling 40 minutes. We then time 10 gaps between buses: 12.5, 7.1, 15.8, 9.3, 21.0, 11.2, 8.4, 14.6, 10.9 and 13.7 minutes.

A subtlety: what we usually care about is the mean wait \frac{1}{\lambda}, and the posterior mean of \frac{1}{\lambda} is not one over the posterior mean of \lambda. For \lambda \sim Gamma(a, b), \mathbb{E}\big[\frac{1}{\lambda}\big] = \frac{b}{a-1}, while \frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[\lambda]} = \frac{b}{a}. Compute the posterior of the quantity you care about; don't transform a summary afterwards. With samples it is automatic: transform each sample.

The rate λ (buses per minute)

The mean wait (minutes)

waits = np.array([12.5, 7.1, 15.8, 9.3, 21.0, 11.2, 8.4, 14.6, 10.9, 13.7])   # minutes between buses
a0, b0 = 4.0, 40.0
a1, b1 = a0 + len(waits), b0 + waits.sum()
post = stats.gamma(a=a1, scale=1 / b1)
lam = post.rvs(200_000, random_state=rng)
next_wait = rng.exponential(1 / lam)        # posterior predictive: draw λ, then a wait given λ
posterior Gamma(14, 164.5); mean rate 0.0851 per minute
mean wait: E[1/λ] = 12.65 min, but 1/E[λ] = 11.75 min, sample mean 12.45 min
by sampling: E[1/λ] = 12.65 min, 95% interval for the mean wait (7.4, 21.5) min
P(next wait > 20 min) = 0.200   (plug-in exponential with 1/E[λ]: 0.182)
For the curious: the chance of a long wait, exactly (optional)

The notebook estimates P(\text{next wait} > 20) by simulation, but it has a closed form. Given \lambda, an exponential wait exceeds t with probability e^{-\lambda t}. Average that over the Gamma(a, b) posterior, and recognise a gamma integral:

P(\tilde y > t \mid y) = \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}\,\frac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)}\lambda^{a-1}e^{-b\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda = \Big(\frac{b}{b + t}\Big)^{a}.

For the bus, \big(\frac{164.5}{184.5}\big)^{14} \approx 0.201, matching the simulation's 0.200. The plug-in answer e^{-20\,\mathbb{E}[\lambda]} \approx 0.182 is smaller: forgetting that the rate is uncertain makes long waits look rarer than they are. This predictive is called the Lomax (or Pareto II) distribution, and its tail falls off like a power of t rather than exponentially.

Part 2

A mean, with known noise

The model. Measurements y_1, \dots, y_n of a quantity \mu by an instrument with a known noise level \sigma, and a normal prior on \mu:

y_i \mid \mu \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Normal}(\mu, \sigma^2), \qquad \mu \sim \text{Normal}(m_0, s_0^2).

One observation first. Dropping constants, the log of likelihood × prior is

-\frac{(y-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} - \frac{(\mu - m_0)^2}{2s_0^2} = -\frac{1}{2}\Big[\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{s_0^2}\Big)\mu^2 - 2\Big(\frac{y}{\sigma^2} + \frac{m_0}{s_0^2}\Big)\mu\Big] + \text{const}.

A quadratic in \mu with a negative leading coefficient is the log of a normal density, so the posterior is normal. To read off its mean and variance, complete the square: for any a > 0, a\mu^2 - 2b\mu = a\big(\mu - \frac{b}{a}\big)^2 - \frac{b^2}{a}, and the last term is a constant. With a = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{s_0^2} and b = \frac{y}{\sigma^2} + \frac{m_0}{s_0^2}, the posterior is Normal\big(\frac{b}{a},\ \frac{1}{a}\big).

Precision, one over the variance, makes this clean: a is the prior's precision \frac{1}{s_0^2} plus one measurement's precision \frac{1}{\sigma^2}, and the posterior mean \frac{b}{a} weights y and m_0 by their precisions.

n observations. The data enter only through their average \bar y, which is Normal\big(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\big), so replace y by \bar y and \sigma^2 by \frac{\sigma^2}{n}:

\boxed{\;\mu \mid y \sim \text{Normal}(m_1, s_1^2), \qquad \frac{1}{s_1^2} = \frac{1}{s_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}, \qquad m_1 = s_1^2\Big(\frac{m_0}{s_0^2} + \frac{n\bar y}{\sigma^2}\Big)\;}

Precisions add, and the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean.

The prior is worth \frac{\sigma^2}{s_0^2} readings: for the lab, \frac{0.3^2}{0.5^2} = 0.36, about a third of one reading. With a very vague prior (s_0 \to \infty) the posterior becomes Normal\big(\bar y, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\big), whose 95% interval \bar y \pm 1.96\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} is numerically the classical confidence interval. The posterior predictive for one more reading is Normal(m_1,\ s_1^2 + \sigma^2): the variances add, because a new reading is \mu plus independent noise.

This is the demo at the top. With the notebook's five readings (10.41, 10.12, 10.63, 10.27 and 10.48 g), the prior's precision of 4 and five readings' 11.1 each add up to 59.6, so the posterior sd is \frac{1}{\sqrt{59.6}} \approx 0.130 g.

sigma = 0.30
m0, s0 = 10.0, 0.5
readings = np.array([10.41, 10.12, 10.63, 10.27, 10.48])
n, ybar = len(readings), readings.mean()

prec1 = 1 / s0**2 + n / sigma**2
s1 = np.sqrt(1 / prec1)
m1 = (m0 / s0**2 + n * ybar / sigma**2) / prec1
sample mean 10.382 g; prior is worth 0.36 readings
posterior Normal(10.356, 0.130^2);  95% credible interval (10.102, 10.610) g
next reading: predictive Normal(10.356, 0.327^2)
grid check: mean 10.356, sd 0.130

The notebook also checks the formula against a brute-force grid posterior with 40,001 points (chapter 04's method): same mean, same sd.

Part 3

Unknown mean and noise

Usually the noise \sigma^2 is unknown too, so we need a joint prior on (\mu, \sigma^2). The conjugate choice is the normal-inverse-gamma prior [1], [3]:

\sigma^2 \sim \text{InvGamma}(\alpha, \beta), \qquad \mu \mid \sigma^2 \sim \text{Normal}\Big(m,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{w}\Big).

Two new pieces:

  • The inverse-gamma distribution: \sigma^2 \sim InvGamma(\alpha, \beta) means that \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \sim Gamma(\alpha, \beta). Its mean is \frac{\beta}{\alpha - 1} for \alpha > 1. To sample it, sample a gamma and take one over it.
  • The prior on \mu is scaled by \sigma^2: w is the number of observations the prior guess m is worth.

The posterior. The same completing-the-square argument, done for both parameters together, gives

\sigma^2 \mid y \sim \text{InvGamma}\Big(\alpha + \frac{n}{2},\ \ \beta + \frac{(n-1)s^2}{2} + \frac{wn}{2(w+n)}(\bar y - m)^2\Big), \qquad \mu \mid \sigma^2, y \sim \text{Normal}\Big(\frac{n\bar y + wm}{n + w},\ \frac{\sigma^2}{n + w}\Big),

where s^2 = \frac{\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2}{n-1} is the sample variance. The rate of the inverse-gamma collects two things: the spread within the data, and a penalty when the data's average disagrees with the prior guess m.

The posterior of \mu alone. We rarely care about \sigma^2 itself. Integrating it out of the joint posterior gives a Student t with 2\alpha + n degrees of freedom, location m^* = \frac{n\bar y + wm}{n + w} and scale \gamma = \sqrt{\beta' / ((n + w)\,\alpha')}, with \alpha' = \alpha + \frac{n}{2}. Heavier tails than a normal are exactly what honest uncertainty about \sigma should produce; it is the Bayesian counterpart of the familiar t confidence interval.

Sampling the joint posterior needs no special algorithm: draw \sigma^2 from its inverse-gamma, then \mu from its normal given that \sigma^2. Each pair is an exact, independent draw. This "marginal, then conditional" pattern, called composition sampling, returns in chapter 08.

Example. The battery life, in hours, of 12 phones of a new model. The prior is weak: m = 10 hours worth w = 1 phone, and \alpha = 2, \beta = 1 for the variance. Each dot below is one draw of (\mu, \sigma) from the joint posterior.

Joint posterior draws of (μ, σ)

The mean μ alone

life = np.array([9.8, 10.4, 11.1, 9.2, 10.9, 10.1, 9.6, 10.7, 11.4, 10.0, 9.9, 10.6])
n, ybar, s2 = len(life), life.mean(), life.var(ddof=1)
m, w, alpha, beta = 10.0, 1.0, 2.0, 1.0

alpha1 = alpha + n / 2
beta1 = beta + (n - 1) * s2 / 2 + w * n / (2 * (w + n)) * (ybar - m) ** 2
m_star = (n * ybar + w * m) / (n + w)
gamma = np.sqrt(beta1 / ((n + w) * alpha1))
t_post = stats.t(df=2 * alpha + n, loc=m_star, scale=gamma)

# Composition sampling: σ² from its inverse-gamma, then μ given σ²
sig2 = 1 / rng.gamma(alpha1, 1 / beta1, size=200_000)
mu = rng.normal(m_star, np.sqrt(sig2 / (n + w)))
n = 12, sample mean 10.308, sample sd 0.654
σ² | y ~ InvGamma(8.0, 3.398),  posterior mean of σ² = 0.485
μ | y ~ t_16(location 10.285, scale 0.1808);  95% credible interval (9.901, 10.668)
classical 95% t confidence interval  (9.893, 10.724)
sampled μ: mean 10.285, 95% interval (9.902, 10.668)

The scatter shows why the posterior of \mu alone has heavy tails: \mu is spread wider exactly in the draws where \sigma is large, and averaging normals of different widths produces a t. Slide the number of phones down to 2 or 3: the uncertainty about \sigma grows, and the t's tails grow with it.

For the curious: why averaging normals gives a t (optional)

The posterior of \mu alone is the joint posterior with \sigma^2 integrated out:

p(\mu \mid y) = \int_0^\infty \underbrace{p(\mu \mid \sigma^2, y)}_{\text{normal}}\ \underbrace{p(\sigma^2 \mid y)}_{\text{inverse-gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma^2.

It is a mixture of normal curves, all centred at m^*, with widths that vary as \sigma^2 varies. Each normal on its own has thin tails, but the wide ones, from large \sigma^2, put a little probability far out, and together they produce tails that fall off like a power of \mu instead of like e^{-\mu^2}. Doing the integral with the inverse-gamma's density gives exactly the Student t; William Gosset found the same distribution in 1908, from the frequentist side, while working on small samples at the Guinness brewery.

Part 4

The conjugate models so far

Data modelParameterConjugate priorPosterior
Binomial(n, \theta)\thetaBeta(\alpha, \beta)Beta(\alpha + y,\ \beta + n - y)
Poisson(\lambda), n counts\lambdaGamma(\alpha, \beta)Gamma(\alpha + \sum y_i,\ \beta + n)
Exponential(\lambda), n waits\lambdaGamma(\alpha, \beta)Gamma(\alpha + n,\ \beta + \sum y_i)
Normal, \sigma known\muNormal(m_0, s_0^2)Normal; precisions add
Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)Normal-InvGammaNormal-InvGamma; \mu alone is a t

All of these data models belong to the exponential families, and every exponential family has a conjugate prior of the same form: the data enter through a few totals (the sufficient statistics, such as \sum y_i and n), and updating adds those totals to the hyperparameters [2]. That is why "prior mean, worth k observations" worked every time.

Part 5

The chapter on one page

  1. Waiting times: Gamma(\alpha, \beta) + n waits → Gamma(\alpha + n, \beta + \sum y_i). Summarise \frac{1}{\lambda} directly: \mathbb{E}\big[\frac{1}{\lambda}\big] is not \frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[\lambda]}.
  2. Normal mean, known noise: complete the square. Precisions add, and the posterior mean is precision-weighted.
  3. Unknown noise too: normal-inverse-gamma prior; the posterior of the mean alone is a Student t, wider than a normal because \sigma is uncertain.
  4. Composition sampling: draw from a marginal, then from a conditional, to get exact joint draws.
  5. Exponential families all have conjugate priors that add sufficient statistics to hyperparameters.

Part 6

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek. The notebook has a code cell ready for each of them.

  1. A long average gap. For the bus example, what is the posterior probability that the true mean gap is more than 12 minutes? Compute it both from the gamma posterior of \lambda and from the samples of \frac{1}{\lambda}.
    Hint

    A mean gap above 12 minutes means a rate below \frac{1}{12} per minute.

    Solution

    P\big(\frac{1}{\lambda} > 12\big) = P\big(\lambda \lt \frac{1}{12}\big), the Gamma(14, 164.5) CDF at \frac{1}{12}: post.cdf(1/12) gives 0.504. From the samples, (1 / lam > 12).mean() gives the same up to Monte Carlo noise. So it is a coin flip whether the buses are, on average, later than every 12 minutes, even though the timetable says 10.

  2. The mean of one over a gamma. Prove \mathbb{E}\big[\frac{1}{\lambda}\big] = \frac{\beta}{\alpha - 1} for \lambda \sim Gamma(\alpha, \beta).
    Hint

    This is exercise 2 of chapter 02: multiply the density by \frac{1}{\lambda} and recognise a Gamma(\alpha - 1, \beta) kernel.

    Solution
    \mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{\lambda}\Big] = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^\infty \lambda^{\alpha-2}e^{-\beta\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(\alpha-1)}{\beta^{\alpha-1}} = \frac{\beta}{\alpha-1}.

    using \Gamma(\alpha) = (\alpha-1)\Gamma(\alpha-1), valid for \alpha > 1. For the bus, \frac{164.5}{13} = 12.65 minutes, as in the notebook.

  3. How many readings? In the weighing example, how many readings would be needed for the posterior standard deviation to fall below 0.05 g? Does the answer depend on the readings' values?
    Hint

    Precisions add. The target precision is \frac{1}{0.05^2}.

    Solution

    We need \frac{1}{0.5^2} + \frac{n}{0.3^2} \ge \frac{1}{0.05^2}, that is 4 + 11.1\,n \ge 400, so n \ge 35.6: 36 readings. The answer doesn't depend on what the readings are: with a known noise level, the posterior variance depends only on how many readings there are, not on their values. Try it in the demo with "take a reading".

  4. One reading at a time. Show that updating the normal-mean model with the readings one at a time gives the same posterior as the batch formula. Use the precision form.
    Hint

    Write each step as: new precision = old precision + \frac{1}{\sigma^2}, and new (precision × mean) = old (precision × mean) + \frac{y_i}{\sigma^2}.

    Solution

    Track two numbers: the precision P and the precision-weighted mean Q = P \cdot m. The prior gives P_0 = \frac{1}{s_0^2}, Q_0 = \frac{m_0}{s_0^2}, and each reading adds \frac{1}{\sigma^2} to P and \frac{y_i}{\sigma^2} to Q. After all n readings, P_n = \frac{1}{s_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2} and Q_n = \frac{m_0}{s_0^2} + \frac{n\bar y}{\sigma^2}, in any order, because addition doesn't care about order. Then m_n = \frac{Q_n}{P_n} and s_n^2 = \frac{1}{P_n}: exactly the batch formula. It is chapter 04's sequential updating, made into arithmetic.

  5. A biased prior. For the battery data, shrink the prior on \mu towards m = 12, with w = 1 and with w = 20. Which conclusion changes, and why does w matter more than m?
    Hint

    Set the sliders of the widget in Part 3. Watch both the interval for \mu and the spread of \sigma in the scatter.

    Solution

    With w = 1, the posterior mean moves only from 10.28 to 10.44 hours, with a 95% interval of (9.99, 10.89): one imaginary phone among 13 can't pull far. With w = 20, the prior outweighs the 12 real phones: the mean jumps to 11.37 and the interval to (10.87, 11.86), which no longer even contains the sample mean 10.31. Something else changes too: the posterior mean of \sigma^2 rises from 0.49 to 2.01, because the penalty term \frac{wn}{2(w+n)}(\bar y - m)^2 treats the disagreement between prior and data as extra noise. The guess m only matters in proportion to its weight w; with a small w, even a wrong guess does little harm.

  6. A known mean. Derive the posterior for \sigma^2 with a known mean \mu and an InvGamma(\alpha, \beta) prior.
    Hint

    The inverse-gamma density is proportional to (\sigma^2)^{-\alpha-1}e^{-\beta/\sigma^2}. Collect the powers of \sigma^2 and the terms in the exponent.

    Solution
    p(\sigma^2 \mid y) \propto (\sigma^2)^{-n/2} e^{-\frac{\sum_i (y_i - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \cdot (\sigma^2)^{-\alpha-1} e^{-\beta/\sigma^2} = (\sigma^2)^{-(\alpha + n/2) - 1} e^{-\big(\beta + \frac{1}{2}\sum_i (y_i - \mu)^2\big)/\sigma^2}.

    So \sigma^2 \mid y \sim InvGamma\big(\alpha + \frac{n}{2},\ \beta + \frac{1}{2}\sum_i (y_i - \mu)^2\big): half a unit of shape per observation, and half the sum of squared deviations added to the rate. It is the w \to \infty limit of Part 3's formula, where the mean is known exactly.

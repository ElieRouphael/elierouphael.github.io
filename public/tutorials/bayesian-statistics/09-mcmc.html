Monte Carlo and Metropolis

Most real posteriors have no formula. All we can do is evaluate prior × likelihood at any point we choose, without its constant. Remarkably, that is enough: a random walk that steps around, always accepting moves uphill and sometimes moves downhill, ends up visiting each region in proportion to its posterior probability. Below, watch it happen one step at a time. This last chapter builds that random walk, the Metropolis algorithm, from scratch, and shows how to tell whether it worked.

Part 1

Monte Carlo integration

Every posterior summary is an expectation. A mean is \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y], a probability is \mathbb{E}[I_{\{\theta \in A\}} \mid y], a predictive density is \mathbb{E}[p(\tilde y \mid \theta) \mid y]. Given S independent draws \theta^{(1)}, \dots, \theta^{(S)} from the posterior,

\mathbb{E}[g(\theta) \mid y] \approx \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} g\big(\theta^{(s)}\big),

and by the central limit theorem of chapter 02, the error is approximately normal with standard deviation \frac{\mathrm{sd}(g(\theta))}{\sqrt{S}}. We have been doing this since chapter 05. Here it is on the A/B test, where the exact answer, 0.9586, is known: estimate P(\theta_B > \theta_A) with S draws, a hundred times over, and look at the spread.

The error shrinks like \frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}, whatever the dimension of θ.

That last part is why sampling beats grids in many dimensions (chapter 04, Part 7): a grid needs 100^d points, but the Monte Carlo error depends only on how many draws you take.

Part 2

When there is no formula

Conjugate models are the exception. Change the prior to one that is not conjugate, or the likelihood to anything more realistic, and the posterior is known only up to its constant:

p(\theta \mid y) = \frac{q(\theta)}{Z}, \qquad q(\theta) = p(y \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)\ \text{(easy to evaluate)}, \qquad Z = \int q(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\ \text{(hard)}.

We cannot sample it directly, and grids fail beyond a few dimensions. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) produces samples using nothing but evaluations of q.

Example: a robust prior. We measure a quantity \mu with known noise \sigma = 1. Our prior guess is \mu \approx 0, but we want a prior that gives way gracefully if the data strongly disagree, so we use a heavy-tailed Cauchy prior (a t with 1 degree of freedom, chapter 02) instead of a normal. Five measurements, 3.6, 4.9, 3.2, 4.4 and 4.1, average 4.04. Normal likelihood times Cauchy prior is not a known distribution. It is one-dimensional, though, so a grid gives the exact answer to check the sampler against. That is the demo at the top.

What is Markov chain Monte Carlo?

In plain words: a random walk through the possible parameter values, designed so that the time it spends in each region is proportional to that region's posterior probability. Record where it goes, and you have samples.

More precisely: MCMC constructs a Markov chain, a sequence where each step depends only on the current position, whose long-run (stationary) distribution is the posterior. After a burn-in, the chain's states are used as draws for Monte Carlo averages.

Markov chain
A random sequence where the next state depends only on the current one.
Stationary distribution
A distribution that the chain keeps once it has it: if today's state follows it, so does tomorrow's.
Burn-in
The first steps, discarded because they still remember the starting point.
Mixing
How quickly the chain moves around the posterior; poor mixing means strongly correlated draws.
Part 3

The Metropolis algorithm

Build a random walk whose long-run distribution is the posterior [1]:

  1. Start at some value \theta^{(0)}.
  2. Propose a move: \theta^* = \theta^{(s)} + \varepsilon, with \varepsilon \sim \text{Normal}(0, \delta^2). The proposal is symmetric, and \delta is the step size.
  3. Accept it with probability \min\Big(1,\ \frac{q(\theta^*)}{q(\theta^{(s)})}\Big): set \theta^{(s+1)} = \theta^*. Otherwise stay: \theta^{(s+1)} = \theta^{(s)}.

Moves uphill are always accepted; moves downhill are accepted in proportion to how far down they go. Only the ratio \frac{q(\theta^*)}{q(\theta)} is needed, so the unknown constant Z cancels. In code we work with \log q to avoid underflow (chapter 03), and accept when \log u \lt \log q(\theta^*) - \log q(\theta) for u \sim Uniform(0, 1).

The price is that successive draws are correlated, and the first ones depend on where the chain started. So we discard an initial burn-in, and we need more draws than independent sampling would.

def metropolis(log_q, start, step, n_steps, rng):
    """Random-walk Metropolis. Works for scalar or vector parameters."""
    theta = np.atleast_1d(np.asarray(start, float))
    lq = log_q(theta)
    chain = np.empty((n_steps, theta.size))
    accepted = 0
    for s in range(n_steps):
        proposal = theta + step * rng.standard_normal(theta.size)
        lq_prop = log_q(proposal)
        if np.log(rng.random()) < lq_prop - lq:
            theta, lq = proposal, lq_prop
            accepted += 1
        chain[s] = theta
    return chain, accepted / n_steps

y = np.array([3.6, 4.9, 3.2, 4.4, 4.1])
def log_q(mu):                                    # log likelihood + log Cauchy prior, constants dropped
    mu = mu[0]
    return -0.5 * np.sum((y - mu) ** 2) - np.log1p(mu**2)

chain, acc = metropolis(log_q, start=0.0, step=1.0, n_steps=20_000, rng=rng)
draws = chain[1000:, 0]                           # discard burn-in
acceptance rate 0.47
posterior mean: Metropolis 3.939, grid 3.944
sample mean 4.04; a Normal(0, 1) prior would give 3.37 (the Cauchy prior gives way to the data)

The Cauchy prior gives way to the data: its posterior mean, 3.94, sits close to the measurements' 4.04, while a Normal(0, 1) prior with the same centre would drag the answer to 3.37. Switch the prior in the demo to see both.

For the curious: why it works (detailed balance) (optional)

Write \pi for the posterior and T(\theta \to \theta') for the chance of moving from \theta to \theta'. For two different values \theta and \theta', with g the symmetric proposal density,

\pi(\theta)\,T(\theta \to \theta') = \pi(\theta)\,g(\theta' - \theta)\,\min\Big(1, \frac{\pi(\theta')}{\pi(\theta)}\Big) = g(\theta' - \theta)\,\min\big(\pi(\theta), \pi(\theta')\big),

which is symmetric in \theta and \theta'. This detailed balance says that, in the long run, the flow of probability from \theta to \theta' equals the flow back. So if the chain is distributed as \pi at one step, it is still distributed as \pi at the next: \pi is the stationary distribution. Under mild conditions the chain converges to it from any starting point [2], [3]. Hastings generalised the rule to asymmetric proposals by including the ratio \frac{g(\theta \mid \theta^*)}{g(\theta^* \mid \theta)} [2].

Part 4

Tuning the step size

The step size \delta controls how fast the chain explores:

  • Too small: almost every proposal is accepted, but each moves a tiny distance, so the chain crawls and successive draws are nearly identical.
  • Too large: proposals mostly land in low-probability regions and are rejected, so the chain sits still for long stretches.
  • About right: for a one-dimensional, roughly normal posterior, an acceptance rate around 40–45% is near optimal, falling towards 23% in high dimensions [4].

The correlation between draws is measured by the effective sample size (ESS): the number of independent draws that would give the same Monte Carlo error,

\text{ESS} = \frac{S}{1 + 2\sum_{k \ge 1} \rho_k},

where \rho_k is the correlation between draws k steps apart (the autocorrelation). We estimate it by summing the autocorrelations until they first drop below zero. Here are three chains of 5,000 steps, started at 4.0, with three step sizes; the effective sample sizes count the last 4,500 steps of each:

def ess(x):
    x = x - x.mean()
    acf = np.correlate(x, x, "full")[x.size - 1:] / (x.var() * x.size)
    neg = np.nonzero(acf[1:] < 0)[0]
    first_neg = neg[0] + 1 if neg.size else acf.size
    return x.size / (1 + 2 * acf[1:first_neg].sum())
Part 5

Two parameters: golf putts

Golfers putt from different distances. For each distance, from 2 to 20 feet, there are 60 attempts, and we record how many go in. The probability of success falls with distance, and we model it with logistic regression:

y_j \sim \text{Binomial}(n_j, p_j), \qquad \log\frac{p_j}{1 - p_j} = a + b\,x_j,

with weakly informative priors a \sim Normal(0, 5^2) and b \sim Normal(0, 1). No conjugate prior exists for this likelihood. The data are simulated from a = 2.5, b = -0.25: 53, 51, 50, 36, 34, 20, 21, 15, 9 and 3 putts made out of 60.

Two practical points:

  • Run several chains from scattered starting points. If they end up exploring the same region, that is evidence they have converged; if not, something is wrong.
  • \hat R ("R-hat", the Gelman-Rubin statistic) compares the variance between chains with the variance within chains [5]. Values close to 1, below about 1.01 by current advice [6], indicate agreement.

All four chains agree (\hat R \approx 1), but notice the effective sample size: about 900 out of 40,000 draws. The posterior of (a, b) is a long, thin diagonal ridge, because a steeper slope is compensated by a larger intercept (the same pivot as in chapter 08). A random walk with independent steps in a and b can only shuffle slowly along it. Tick centre the distance: in those coordinates the posterior is round, and with the same step sizes the effective sample size of the slope more than doubles. Gradient-based samplers (Part 6) handle such ridges automatically.

Part 6

Where to go next

Random-walk Metropolis is the simplest MCMC algorithm, and the one to understand first. Practical work uses its descendants:

  • Gibbs sampling [7], [8] draws each parameter in turn from its posterior given the others. When those conditionals are known distributions, as in the normal-inverse-gamma model of chapter 06, no tuning is needed (exercise 5).
  • Hamiltonian Monte Carlo uses the gradient of \log q to make long, informed moves, and its adaptive version NUTS [9] tunes itself. It scales to models with thousands of parameters.
  • Probabilistic programming languages let you write the model and get NUTS for free: Stan [10] and, in Python, PyMC [11]. The golf model above is about ten lines in either.
  • Hierarchical models are the natural next topic: many related groups (players, schools, hospitals), each with its own parameter, sharing a common prior whose parameters are also learned. They are where Bayesian methods most clearly beat the alternatives, and they need exactly the MCMC tools of this chapter.

Part 8

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek. The notebook has a code cell ready for each of them.

  1. Cauchy or normal? Replace the Cauchy prior in the measurement example by Normal(0, 1) and compare the posteriors. Then move the data to average 1 instead of 4. When does the choice between the two priors matter?
    Hint

    Use the two menus of the demo at the top. The normal prior is conjugate: its posterior mean is \frac{n\bar y}{n + 1}.

    Solution

    With the data averaging 4.04, the posterior means are 3.94 (Cauchy) and 3.37 (normal): the normal prior, with its thin tails, refuses to believe the data are that far from 0 and pulls hard, while the Cauchy's heavy tails let the data win. With the data shifted to average 1.00, both give 0.83. The choice matters only when the data conflict with the prior; when they agree, any reasonable prior gives the same answer. That is the point of a robust, heavy-tailed prior: it behaves like a normal when the data agree with it, and steps aside when they don't.

  2. Detailed balance, both cases. Show that the Metropolis acceptance rule satisfies detailed balance even when \pi(\theta') > \pi(\theta). Handle the two cases of the minimum separately.
    Hint

    Compute \pi(\theta)\,T(\theta \to \theta') and \pi(\theta')\,T(\theta' \to \theta) separately in the case \pi(\theta') > \pi(\theta), and compare.

    Solution

    If \pi(\theta') > \pi(\theta), the move \theta \to \theta' is accepted with probability 1, so \pi(\theta)\,T(\theta \to \theta') = \pi(\theta)\,g. The reverse move is accepted with probability \frac{\pi(\theta)}{\pi(\theta')}, so \pi(\theta')\,T(\theta' \to \theta) = \pi(\theta')\,g\,\frac{\pi(\theta)}{\pi(\theta')} = \pi(\theta)\,g. Equal, using g(\theta' - \theta) = g(\theta - \theta'). The case \pi(\theta') \le \pi(\theta) is the same with the roles swapped. In both cases each side equals g \cdot \min(\pi(\theta), \pi(\theta')), as in the box of Part 3.

  3. The 50% distance. For the golf model, compute the posterior of the distance at which the success probability falls to 50%, -\frac{a}{b}, from the samples.
    Hint

    A probability of 50% is log-odds 0: a + bx = 0. Apply the formula to every draw, as for the 150-drinks temperature of chapter 08.

    Solution

    Applying -\frac{a}{b} to the notebook's 40,000 draws gives a median of 10.7 feet, with a 95% interval of (9.9, 11.4) feet. The widget of Part 5 shows it in its caption. Its interval is much narrower, relatively, than those of a and b: the ridge-shaped correlation between them cancels out in the ratio.

  4. A chain lost far away. Start one golf chain far away, at (20, 5), with only 3,000 steps. Compute \hat R with and without it. What does \hat R detect?
    Hint

    In the widget of Part 5, tick "add a fifth chain", choose 3,000 steps, and try each burn-in setting.

    Solution

    Keeping all 3,000 steps, the far chain pushes \hat R for the slope to about 1.15, against about 1.03 to 1.05 without it: a clear warning. But the far chain reaches the posterior within about 500 steps, so once its first 1,000 steps are discarded, \hat R falls back to about 1.01 to 1.04. \hat R detects chains that have not yet forgotten where they started; it says nothing about a chain that has. Notice also that the four ordinary chains alone give \hat R around 1.01 to 1.02 even after that burn-in: 2,000 kept steps per chain are not enough for the strict threshold. The honest conclusion is "run longer", which the 12,000-step setting confirms.

  5. A Gibbs sampler. Implement a Gibbs sampler for the normal model with unknown mean and variance of chapter 06, alternating \mu \mid \sigma^2, y and \sigma^2 \mid \mu, y (derive the second one). Check it against the exact t of chapter 06.
    Hint

    The first conditional is in chapter 06. For the second, multiply the likelihood, the normal prior on \mu given \sigma^2, and the inverse-gamma prior, and collect the powers of \sigma^2.

    Solution
    \sigma^2 \mid \mu, y \sim \text{InvGamma}\Big(\alpha + \frac{n+1}{2},\ \beta + \frac{1}{2}\sum_i (y_i - \mu)^2 + \frac{w}{2}(\mu - m)^2\Big).

    The extra one half in the shape comes from the prior on \mu, whose variance is proportional to \sigma^2. Alternating this draw with \mu \mid \sigma^2, y \sim \text{Normal}\big(\frac{n\bar y + wm}{n + w}, \frac{\sigma^2}{n + w}\big) for the battery data gives a mean of 10.285 and a 95% interval of (9.900, 10.666), matching the exact t_{16} of chapter 06, (9.901, 10.668). No step size to tune, and no rejections.

  6. Let the computer do it. Install PyMC and fit the golf model with NUTS. Compare its posterior means, run time and effective sample size with your Metropolis sampler.
    Hint

    pip install pymc. The model is a pm.Normal for each of a and b, and a pm.Binomial likelihood with p=pm.math.invlogit(a + b * dist). Then pm.sample() and arviz.summary.

    Solution

    What to expect: the posterior means and intervals agree with the Metropolis ones to within Monte Carlo error, which is a strong check of your own sampler. NUTS takes a few seconds, most of it compiling the model, and its effective sample size is typically a large fraction of the number of draws, often above half, against about 1 in 45 for random-walk Metropolis here. The ridge that slowed Metropolis down hardly matters to NUTS, because the gradient steers it along the ridge. Exact numbers depend on the PyMC version and the computer, so this solution doesn't quote any.

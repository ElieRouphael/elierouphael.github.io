Part 1

From a list to a continuum

Back to the shooter of chapter 03: 13 made out of 20. In chapter 01 we applied Bayes' theorem to a short list of hypotheses, and nothing stops us from doing the same here with a list of candidate values for her success rate \theta, say 0.05, 0.15, \dots, 0.95, all equally plausible to begin with. The likelihood of each candidate is \theta^{13}(1-\theta)^7, and the posterior is prior times likelihood, normalised.

Make the list finer and finer, and the bars of the posterior trace out a smooth curve (you watched this happen in Part 6 of chapter 02). In the limit, the prior probabilities become a density p(\theta), and the sum in the denominator becomes an integral:

p(\theta \mid y) = \frac{p(y \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(y)}, \qquad p(y) = \int_0^1 p(y \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

What is a posterior?

In plain words: everything you believe about an unknown number after seeing the data: which values are plausible, and how plausible each one is.

More precisely: the posterior is the probability distribution of the parameter given the data, p(\theta \mid y). Bayes' theorem builds it from the prior p(\theta) and the likelihood p(y \mid \theta).

Prior p(\theta)
A density for the parameter before the data: what you knew or assumed.
Likelihood p(y \mid \theta)
How probable the observed data are for each value of the parameter (chapter 03).
Posterior p(\theta \mid y)
A density for the parameter after the data. It answers every question about \theta that the model can.
Evidence p(y)
The probability of the data under the model as a whole, averaged over the prior. Also called the marginal likelihood.
Part 2

Proportional to

The denominator p(y) is a single number: it does not depend on \theta. So Bayes' theorem is usually written with the sign \propto, "equal up to a constant factor that does not involve \theta":

p(\theta \mid y) \;\propto\; p(y \mid \theta)\,p(\theta).

This is more than a shorthand. It has three consequences that the rest of the course leans on:

  • Constants can be dropped. Any factor of the likelihood or the prior that doesn't contain \theta changes nothing. The \binom{20}{13} in the binomial likelihood is irrelevant to the posterior.
  • The shape is everything. The product of likelihood and prior fixes the posterior's shape completely; the constant comes back at the end, because the posterior must have area 1.
  • Recognise the shape, skip the integral. If the product looks like a known density up to a constant, you have the posterior without computing anything. Chapter 05 is built on this.
For the curious: what the evidence measures (optional)

The constant p(y) is still meaningful. It is the likelihood averaged over the prior, so it measures how well the model as a whole, prior included, predicted the data:

p(y) = \int_0^1 p(y \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \mathbb{E}_{\text{prior}}\big[p(y \mid \theta)\big].

For the shooter, the flat prior gives p(y) = \frac{1}{21} \approx 0.048, the informed prior 0.109, and the sceptical prior only 0.0046: the informed prior expected something close to 13 out of 20, the sceptical one bet on very low or very high scores. The ratio of two evidences, here 0.109 / 0.048 \approx 2.3, is the Bayes factor: the likelihood ratio of chapter 01, now comparing two whole models instead of two hypotheses. Exercise 1 computes it.

Part 3

The grid, and the prior's role

Numerically, the recipe is three steps, and it is exactly what the demo at the top does:

  1. Choose a fine grid of \theta values: here 2,001 points between 0 and 1.
  2. Multiply prior × likelihood at each point.
  3. Normalise: divide by the sum times the grid spacing, so that the result has area 1.

It works for any prior shape, even one you sketch by hand, which makes it perfect for building intuition. The notebook compares three priors for the shooter:

  • flat: every \theta equally plausible;
  • informed: records of similar players say \theta is usually 0.7 to 0.8 (a normal shape centred at 0.75 with standard deviation 0.05);
  • sceptical: an odd prior that thinks the player is either poor (around 0.3) or excellent (around 0.9), but not in between.

The posterior is always a compromise between the prior and the likelihood.

With the flat prior it has exactly the likelihood's shape. With the informed prior it sits between the data (0.65) and the prior (0.75), and it is narrower than either, because two sources of information roughly agree. With the sceptical prior the posterior keeps both bumps: the prior insists the player is either poor or excellent, and 20 throws are not enough to overrule that, so the data can only pull each bump towards 0.65 and shift weight to the "excellent" bump, the closer one. Now slide the throws up to 60 with the same 65% made: every prior gives way, and the posteriors start to agree. With little data the prior matters a lot; with a lot of data, hardly at all.

theta = np.linspace(0, 1, 2001)
dtheta = theta[1] - theta[0]
likelihood = stats.binom.pmf(y, n, theta)

def normalise(f):
    return f / (f.sum() * dtheta)               # so that it integrates to 1 on the grid

priors = {
    "flat": np.ones_like(theta),
    "informed": stats.norm.pdf(theta, 0.75, 0.05),
    "sceptical": stats.norm.pdf(theta, 0.3, 0.06) + stats.norm.pdf(theta, 0.9, 0.04),
}
posteriors = {name: normalise(normalise(pr) * likelihood) for name, pr in priors.items()}
Part 4

Reading a posterior

The posterior is the full answer, but we usually report a few numbers.

Point estimates. A single best guess. Three are common: the posterior mean \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y], the posterior median (half the probability on each side), and the posterior mode, the highest point, also called the MAP estimate (maximum a posteriori). With a flat prior, the mode is the MLE of chapter 03.

Credible intervals. A 95% credible interval is any interval (a, b) with P(a \lt \theta \lt b \mid y) = 0.95. Two choices are common:

  • equal-tailed: cut 2.5% off each end, between the 0.025 and 0.975 quantiles;
  • highest posterior density (HPD): the shortest region with 95% probability. Every point inside is more plausible than every point outside [5].

Unlike a confidence interval, a credible interval means exactly what it sounds like: given the model and the data, \theta is in it with probability 0.95. The widget below reads the posterior you built at the top. Try it with the sceptical prior: the HPD region splits into two pieces.

equal-tailed highest density (HPD)

Probabilities of statements. Anything of the form "P(\theta > 0.5 \mid y)" is an area under the posterior, which on a grid is a sum. It answers the question chapter 03 couldn't: how likely is it that this player makes more than half of her free throws? The notebook's summary table, for the flat and informed priors:

prior        mean  median   mode     sd    95% equal-tailed           95% HPD   P(θ>0.5)
flat        0.636   0.640  0.650  0.100   (0.430, 0.819)   (0.440, 0.827)   0.905
informed    0.729   0.729  0.730  0.044   (0.642, 0.812)   (0.643, 0.814)   1.000

Wald 95% confidence interval from chapter 03: (0.441, 0.859)

With the flat prior the credible interval is numerically close to the Wald confidence interval, but the two answer different questions: the credible interval is a probability statement about \theta; the confidence interval is a statement about the method.

For the curious: why three different "best guesses" (optional)

A point estimate is a decision, and the right one depends on what a mistake costs you [6]. If you guess a and the truth is \theta:

  • if the cost is the squared error (\theta - a)^2, the best guess is the posterior mean (exercise 3 proves it);
  • if the cost is the absolute error |\theta - a|, it is the median: moving a towards the side with more probability always helps, until both sides hold half;
  • if you are only rewarded for being exactly right (or within a tiny distance), it is the mode.

For a symmetric single-peaked posterior the three coincide. For a skewed one they spread out, and for a two-bumped one the mean can land in the valley between the bumps, where the posterior is low: a "best guess" that is itself implausible. The sceptical prior in the widget above shows it.

Part 5

Predicting new data

The player will take 10 more throws. How many will go in? If we knew \theta, the answer would be Binomial(10, \theta). We don't, so we average over everything we believe about \theta. That average is the posterior predictive distribution:

p(\tilde y \mid y) = \int p(\tilde y \mid \theta)\,p(\theta \mid y)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

It carries two kinds of uncertainty: the randomness of the future throws even if we knew \theta, and our remaining uncertainty about \theta itself. Plugging in a single estimate, as in Binomial(10, 0.65), keeps the first and forgets the second, so it is overconfident.

plug-in binomial predictive 

k = np.arange(11)
w = posteriors["flat"] * dtheta
predictive = np.array([(stats.binom.pmf(kk, 10, theta) * w).sum() for kk in k])
plug_in = stats.binom.pmf(k, 10, y / n)
print(f"predictive mean {np.sum(k * predictive):.2f}, sd {np.sqrt(np.sum(k**2 * predictive) - np.sum(k * predictive)**2):.2f}")
print(f"plug-in    mean {np.sum(k * plug_in):.2f}, sd {np.sqrt(np.sum(k**2 * plug_in) - np.sum(k * plug_in)**2):.2f}")
predictive mean 6.36, sd 1.79
plug-in    mean 6.50, sd 1.51

The same idea applied before any data, with the prior in place of the posterior, gives the prior predictive distribution: the data the model expects to see. Tick the box above to see it. It is a useful check that a prior makes sense: the sceptical prior, for example, expects 20 throws to give either about 6 or about 18 makes, and almost never 13.

Part 6

One throw at a time

If the throws are conditionally independent given \theta, so that p(y_1, y_2 \mid \theta) = p(y_1 \mid \theta)\,p(y_2 \mid \theta), then

p(\theta \mid y_1, y_2) \propto p(y_2 \mid \theta)\,\underbrace{p(y_1 \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)}_{\propto\, p(\theta \mid y_1)} \;\propto\; p(y_2 \mid \theta)\,p(\theta \mid y_1).

The posterior after the first observation is the prior for the second.

Feeding the data in one at a time, in any order, gives exactly the posterior from all the data at once. Learning is cumulative, and the data need not be stored: only the current posterior. Below are the notebook's 20 throws in the order it shuffled them (X is a make, a dot a miss). Step through them, then shuffle the order and play again: the end point never changes.

throws = np.array([1] * y + [0] * (n - y))
rng.shuffle(throws)
belief = normalise(np.ones_like(theta))
for t in throws:
    belief = normalise(belief * (theta if t else 1 - theta))
print("throws in the order seen:", "".join("X" if t else "." for t in throws))
print(f"largest difference from the all-at-once posterior: {np.abs(belief - posteriors['flat']).max():.1e}")
throws in the order seen: X.XX.XXXX.X.X..XXXX.
largest difference from the all-at-once posterior: 1.1e-14

The difference of 10^{-14} is rounding error in the computer's arithmetic: mathematically the two are identical.

Part 7

The limits of the grid

The grid is exact up to the spacing and works with any prior, so why ever do anything else? Because it scales badly. With d unknown parameters and 100 grid points for each, the grid has 100^d points. At a million points per second:

Parameters dGrid pointsTime
1 (this chapter)1000.1 milliseconds
210,00010 milliseconds
31,000,0001 second
510^{10}3 hours
1010^{20}3 million years

Two ways out organise the rest of the course:

  • Conjugate priors (chapters 05 and 06): pairs of likelihood and prior whose posterior has a known closed form, so no numerical integration is needed at all.
  • Monte Carlo sampling (chapter 09): draw random samples from the posterior instead of evaluating it everywhere. The effort then grows with what you want to know, not with the number of grid points.
Part 8

The chapter on one page

  1. Bayes' theorem for densities: p(\theta \mid y) \propto p(y \mid \theta)\,p(\theta). Constants that don't involve \theta can be dropped.
  2. Grid approximation: multiply prior and likelihood on a fine grid and normalise. Works for any prior; hopeless beyond a few parameters.
  3. The posterior is a compromise between prior and likelihood. With little data the prior matters; with a lot, the data take over.
  4. Report a point estimate (mean, median or mode), a credible interval (equal-tailed or HPD), and probabilities of the statements you care about.
  5. Predict by averaging over the posterior: the posterior predictive is wider than a plug-in, because it admits that \theta is uncertain. Update one observation at a time, in any order.

Part 9

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek. The notebook has a code cell ready for each of them.

  1. Which prior predicted better? Compute the marginal likelihood p(y) for the flat and the informed prior on the grid. Which prior "predicted" 13 out of 20 better? Relate the ratio to the likelihood ratio of chapter 01.
    Hint

    On the grid, p(y) \approx \sum_i p(y \mid \theta_i)\,p(\theta_i)\,\Delta\theta: the same sum as the normalising constant. Use the full binomial likelihood, with \binom{20}{13}.

    Solution

    The flat prior gives p(y) = 0.048 (exactly \frac{1}{21}: under a flat prior every number of makes from 0 to 20 is equally likely). The informed prior gives p(y) = 0.109. The informed prior predicted the data about 2.3 times better: it put its weight on rates near 0.75, where 13 of 20 is quite likely, while the flat prior spread its bets over rates that make 13 of 20 nearly impossible. The ratio is a Bayes factor: in chapter 01, the likelihood ratio compared two hypotheses about one person; here it compares two whole models. Combined with prior odds on the models, it gives posterior odds on the models, exactly as in Part 6 of chapter 01.

  2. How sure is the prior? Replace the informed prior's standard deviation 0.05 by 0.02 and by 0.2. Describe how the posterior mean moves in each case, and explain why.
    Hint

    The posterior is a compromise. Which side of the compromise has more information in each case: the prior, or 20 throws?

    Solution

    With sd 0.02 the posterior mean is 0.746, almost the prior's 0.75: a prior that sure is worth far more than 20 throws. With sd 0.05 it is 0.729, as in the notebook. With sd 0.2 it is 0.661, close to the data's 0.65: a vague prior gives way to the data. The weight of each side is its precision (one over its variance). The likelihood of 20 throws has a standard deviation of about 0.107 (chapter 03), so a prior with sd 0.02 is about 30 times more precise, and one with sd 0.2 about 3.5 times less.

  3. Why the mean? Prove that the posterior mean minimises \mathbb{E}[(\theta - a)^2 \mid y] over a.
    Hint

    Expand the square. The expectation is a quadratic in a.

    Solution

    With m = \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y],

    \mathbb{E}[(\theta - a)^2 \mid y] = \mathbb{E}[\theta^2 \mid y] - 2am + a^2 = \mathrm{Var}(\theta \mid y) + (m - a)^2.

    The variance doesn't depend on a, and (m - a)^2 \ge 0 is zero exactly at a = m. So the posterior mean is the best guess under squared error, and the smallest expected squared error is the posterior variance.

  4. What the sceptic expects. Compute the prior predictive distribution of the number made in 20 throws under the sceptical prior. What does it say about the kind of data this prior expects?
    Hint

    Same code as the posterior predictive, with the normalised prior in place of the posterior and 20 throws instead of 10. Or tick "use the prior instead" in the widget of Part 5 after choosing the sceptical prior, with 20 future throws.

    Solution

    It has two humps, around 6 makes (probability up to 0.08) and around 18–19 makes (up to 0.13), and almost nothing in between: 13 makes has a prior predictive probability of only 0.0046, and the whole range 8–14 only 0.14. The prior expects either a poor shooter or an excellent one. The actual data, 13 of 20, are exactly what it considered least likely, which is why its evidence in exercise 1 is the smallest of the three priors: a prior predictive check would have flagged this prior as a poor match for these data.

  5. Eight or more. Using the flat-prior posterior, find P(\tilde y \ge 8) for the next 10 throws, both from the posterior predictive and from the plug-in binomial.
    Hint

    Sum the predictive probabilities for 8, 9 and 10. The widget of Part 5 shows both numbers with k = 8.

    Solution

    The posterior predictive gives 0.281; the plug-in Binomial(10, 0.65) gives 0.262. The predictive puts more weight in both tails, because a rate higher than 0.65 is still plausible (making 8 or more likelier) and so is a lower one (for 3 or fewer: 0.063 against 0.026). The plug-in, pretending to know \theta exactly, is too sure of a middling result.

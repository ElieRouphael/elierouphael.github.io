One rule

Bayesian statistics represents everything uncertain with a probability distribution and updates it with Bayes' theorem when data arrive:

p(\theta\mid y) \;\propto\; p(y\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)
Prior · p(θ)

What you believe about the unknown θ before the data. Drawn in grey throughout the course.

Likelihood · p(y | θ)

How probable the observed data are for each value of θ: the model of how data arise. Drawn in orange.

Posterior · p(θ | y)

What you believe after seeing the data. Everything you report comes from it. Drawn in blue.

The course is about doing this well: choosing likelihoods and priors, computing the posterior exactly when a conjugate model allows and by sampling when it does not, and reading the result honestly, with credible intervals, predictions, model checks and a sensitivity analysis.

Chapters

The first four chapters build the probability and inference toolkit and contrast the Bayesian and classical answers to the same question. The next four develop the conjugate models, priors and regression. The last replaces formulas with sampling.

  1. 00 What Bayesian statistics is Three meanings of probability, the prior-likelihood-posterior recipe, and a first posterior watched as data arrive. You compute A flat prior sharpens into a narrow posterior around the true cure rate as 200 patients arrive.
  2. 01 Probability and Bayes' theorem Conditional probability, the law of total probability, Bayes' theorem and its odds form for repeated evidence. You compute At 1% prevalence, a positive result from a 95%-sensitive test means only an 8.8% chance of disease.
  3. 02 Random variables and distributions Expectation and variance, the standard discrete and continuous families, scipy's parameterisations, and the central limit theorem. You compute Averages of a heavily skewed exponential look normal by n = 30.
  4. 03 Likelihood and frequentist inference The likelihood function, maximum likelihood, Fisher information, and what a confidence interval does and does not promise. You compute A nominal 95% Wald interval covers the truth only 64% of the time at θ = 0.05, n = 20.
  5. 04 From prior to posterior Continuous Bayes by grid approximation, point estimates and credible intervals, the posterior predictive, sequential updating. You compute Updating one throw at a time matches the all-at-once posterior to 1e-14.
  6. 05 Conjugate models for counts and proportions The beta-binomial and Poisson-gamma models derived, priors as imaginary data, and closed-form predictive distributions. You compute An A/B test: version B converts better with posterior probability 0.96.
  7. 06 Conjugate models for continuous data Exponential-gamma, the normal mean by completing the square, and the normal-inverse-gamma model with its t marginal. You compute Exact composition sampling reproduces the analytic t posterior for a mean.
  8. 07 Choosing priors Eliciting a prior from an expert, improper and flat priors, Jeffreys priors, prior predictive checks and sensitivity analysis. You compute Jeffreys' interval covers 98% where the Wald interval covers 64%.
  9. 08 Bayesian linear regression Reference and conjugate priors for regression, exact posterior sampling, credible and prediction bands, ridge as a posterior mean. You compute Credible intervals that match least squares, plus a posterior for any derived quantity.
  10. 09 Beyond conjugacy: Monte Carlo and Metropolis Monte Carlo error, the Metropolis algorithm from scratch, step-size tuning, effective sample size and R-hat. You compute Four Metropolis chains agree on a golf-putting logistic regression, R-hat 1.00.

Running it yourself

Every chapter page shows the notebook with its outputs, so you can read the whole course here. To run and change the code, download the course (the notebooks, their markdown sources and the requirements) and run:

pip install -r requirements.txt
jupyter notebook            # then open notebooks/00_overview.ipynb

Each notebook is self-contained and runs in seconds on a laptop, using only numpy, scipy and matplotlib. Every chapter ends with exercises and references.

Sources

The order of topics follows the arc of Herbert Lee's course Bayesian Statistics: From Concept to Data Analysis (University of California, Santa Cruz). The explanations, examples and code are written from scratch for this course. For more depth, the best next books are Hoff's A First Course in Bayesian Statistical Methods, McElreath's Statistical Rethinking, and Gelman et al.'s Bayesian Data Analysis; each chapter lists its own references.

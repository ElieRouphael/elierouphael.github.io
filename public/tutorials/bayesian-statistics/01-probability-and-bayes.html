Part 1

Chances as fractions

Probability starts with an experiment: anything whose result you don't know in advance. Draw one card from a well-shuffled deck. The 52 cards are the possible outcomes, and together they form the sample space, written \Omega (the Greek letter omega).

An event is a set of outcomes that you care about: "the card is a king" is the set of 4 kings; "the card is a heart" is the set of 13 hearts. When every outcome is equally likely, the probability of an event is simply a fraction:

P(\text{king}) = \frac{\text{number of kings}}{\text{number of cards}} = \frac{4}{52} \approx 0.077

Probabilities don't always come from counting equally likely outcomes, but they always obey the same three rules, written down by Andrey Kolmogorov in 1933 [1]:

  1. Never negative. Every event has a probability of at least 0.
  2. Something happens. The whole sample space has probability 1: P(\Omega) = 1.
  3. Separate events add up. If A and B cannot happen together, the probability that one or the other happens is P(A) + P(B).

Everything else follows from these three. Two consequences are used all the time:

  • Complement. "Not A", written A^c, happens exactly when A doesn't, so P(A^c) = 1 - P(A). The chance of not drawing a king is 1 - \frac{4}{52} = \frac{48}{52}.
  • Union. "A or B" has probability P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B). Adding the kings (4) and the hearts (13) counts the king of hearts twice, so it is taken away once: \frac{4 + 13 - 1}{52} = \frac{16}{52}.

Try it below. Pick two events, then a view: the cards in that event light up, and the fraction underneath is its probability.

For the curious: the three rules, and how the others follow (optional)

In symbols, a probability P gives every event a number such that

P(A) \ge 0, \qquad P(\Omega) = 1, \qquad P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) \ \text{ whenever } A \cap B = \varnothing.

Here A \cup B ("A union B") means "A or B or both", A \cap B ("A intersection B") means "A and B", and \varnothing is the empty event.

Complement. A and A^c don't overlap and together make \Omega, so rule 3 then rule 2 give P(A) + P(A^c) = P(\Omega) = 1. With A = \Omega this also shows P(\varnothing) = 0.

Union. Split A \cup B into three pieces that don't overlap: "A but not B", "B but not A", and "both". Also A is "A but not B" plus "both", and the same for B. Adding with rule 3:

P(A \cup B) = P(A \cap B^c) + P(A^c \cap B) + P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B).

Bigger events are more likely. If every outcome of A is also in B, then B is A plus "B but not A", so P(B) = P(A) + P(B \cap A^c) \ge P(A). In particular no probability exceeds 1.

Part 2

Shrinking the world

Now a friend peeks at the card and tells you: "it's a heart". What is the chance that it is a king? You no longer care about all 52 cards, only about the 13 hearts, and exactly one of them is a king. The answer is \frac{1}{13}.

That is conditional probability: the probability of A given that B has happened, written P(A \mid B). Learning B shrinks the world to B, and inside that smaller world you take the fraction that is also in A:

P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)}, \qquad \text{provided } P(B) > 0.

Dividing by P(B) rescales the smaller world so that it has probability 1 again. Multiplying both sides by P(B) gives the multiplication rule, which works in both directions:

P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A).

Keep that double equation in mind: it is Bayes' theorem in disguise (Part 4).

Independence

For the kings and the hearts, P(\text{king} \mid \text{heart}) = \frac{1}{13}, which is the same as P(\text{king}) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13}. Learning the suit told you nothing about the rank. Two events like that are independent:

P(A \mid B) = P(A), \qquad \text{or equivalently} \qquad P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A)\,P(B).

Not all events are independent. If your friend says "it's a face card" (a jack, queen or king), the world shrinks to 12 cards, 4 of which are kings: P(\text{king} \mid \text{face card}) = \frac{4}{12} = \frac{1}{3}, much more than \frac{1}{13}. Try both in the deck:

Order matters. P(\text{king} \mid \text{face card}) = \frac{1}{3}, but P(\text{face card} \mid \text{king}) = 1: every king is a face card. Confusing P(A \mid B) with P(B \mid A) is the single most common mistake in reasoning with probabilities, and the screening test of the hero demo is exactly that mistake. Use the B given A view to compare the two.

For the curious: independent is not the same as "can't happen together" (optional)

It is tempting to think that two events that cannot happen together, like "the card is a heart" and "the card is a spade", are independent. They are the opposite. Before you learn anything, the chance of a spade is 13 in 52, one in four. Once you learn that the card is a heart, you know for certain that it is not a spade:

P(\text{spade}) = \frac{13}{52} = \frac{1}{4}, \qquad \text{but} \qquad P(\text{spade} \mid \text{heart}) = 0.

Learning "heart" changed the chance of "spade" from \frac{1}{4} to 0, so the two events are not independent.

In general, if A and B both have positive probability and cannot happen together, then P(A \cap B) = 0 while P(A)\,P(B) > 0: they are dependent, as strongly as possible. Independence means "knowing one tells you nothing about the other", not "they never meet".

Part 3

Adding up the ways

Back to the screening test. Before anyone looks at a result, how likely is a random person to test positive? There are two ways to get a positive result: you have the condition and the test catches it, or you don't have it and the test raises a false alarm. The two ways can't both happen, so their probabilities add, and each one is a multiplication rule:

P(+) = P(+ \mid D)\,P(D) + P(+ \mid D^c)\,P(D^c) = 0.95 \times 0.01 + 0.10 \times 0.99 = 0.0095 + 0.099 = 0.1085.

Here D is "has the condition", D^c is "doesn't", and 0.10 is the false-alarm rate, one minus the specificity of 0.90. The tree below draws the same calculation. It follows the settings of the demo at the top, so change them and come back.

This is the law of total probability. In general, suppose the hypotheses H_1, \dots, H_m form a partition: exactly one of them is true. Then any event E splits into the pieces "E and H_i", which don't overlap, and

P(E) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} P(E \mid H_i)\,P(H_i).

The probability of the evidence is a weighted average: how likely it is under each hypothesis, weighted by how likely each hypothesis is.

Part 4

Bayes' theorem

The test's makers tell you P(+ \mid D) = 0.95: how often people with the condition test positive. You want the other direction, P(D \mid +): how often people who test positive have the condition. Turning a conditional probability around is exactly what Bayes' theorem does, and it takes one line. The multiplication rule of Part 2 can be written two ways,

P(H \mid E)\,P(E) = P(H \text{ and } E) = P(E \mid H)\,P(H),

so dividing by P(E), and expanding P(E) with the law of total probability of Part 3:

\boxed{\;P(H_j \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H_j)\,P(H_j)}{\sum_{i=1}^{m} P(E \mid H_i)\,P(H_i)}\;}

What is Bayes' theorem?

In plain words: it is the rule for changing your mind. Start with how plausible each explanation was before; multiply by how well each one predicted what you then saw; rescale so that the results add up to 1.

More precisely: for a list of hypotheses of which exactly one is true, the probability of each hypothesis after seeing the evidence is proportional to its probability before, times the probability that it gave to the evidence.

Hypothesis
One possible explanation of the world: "this person has the condition", "machine C made this part".
Prior, P(H)
How probable the hypothesis was before the evidence. For the screening test: 1%, the base rate.
Likelihood, P(E \mid H)
How probable the evidence would be if the hypothesis were true: how well it predicted what you saw.
Evidence, P(E)
How probable the evidence is overall, averaged over all hypotheses. The same number for every hypothesis; it only rescales.
Posterior, P(H \mid E)
How probable the hypothesis is after the evidence. This is the answer.

Because the denominator is the same for every hypothesis, the theorem is often written with "\propto", which means "proportional to":

\text{posterior} \;\propto\; \text{likelihood} \times \text{prior}.

A hypothesis gains probability when it predicted the evidence better than the average hypothesis did, and loses probability when it predicted it worse.

For the curious: why "better than average" (optional)

Divide both sides of Bayes' theorem by the prior:

\frac{P(H_j \mid E)}{P(H_j)} = \frac{P(E \mid H_j)}{P(E)} = \frac{P(E \mid H_j)}{\sum_i P(E \mid H_i)\,P(H_i)}.

The left side is the factor by which the hypothesis's probability grows or shrinks. The bottom of the right side is, by the law of total probability, the prior-weighted average likelihood. So a hypothesis is multiplied by more than 1 exactly when its likelihood beats the average. In the screening test a positive result has likelihood 0.95 under "has the condition" and an average likelihood of 0.1085, so the probability of the condition is multiplied by \frac{0.95}{0.1085} \approx 8.8: from 1% to 8.8%.

Thomas Bayes found the rule for one special case in an essay published after his death in 1763 [2]; Pierre-Simon Laplace stated it in general in 1774 and used it far more widely [3].

Part 5

The screening test, explained

Now the question from the top of the page. A condition affects 1% of people. A screening test detects it in 95% of the people who have it (its sensitivity) and correctly gives a negative result for 90% of the people who don't (its specificity). A randomly chosen person tests positive. With D for "has the condition":

P(D \mid +) = \frac{P(+ \mid D)\,P(D)}{P(+ \mid D)\,P(D) + P(+ \mid D^c)\,P(D^c)} = \frac{0.95 \times 0.01}{0.95 \times 0.01 + 0.10 \times 0.99} = \frac{0.0095}{0.1085} \approx 0.088.

Less than 9%, even though the test is "95% accurate". The reason is the base rate, the 1%. The condition is rare, so the false alarms coming from the 99% of healthy people far outnumber the true positives coming from the 1% who are ill. Ignoring the base rate is one of the best documented errors in human reasoning [4].

Natural frequencies

The formula is correct but hard to feel. Counting people instead of multiplying probabilities makes it obvious [5], and it is what the demo at the top shows. Out of 10,000 people, 100 have the condition and 95 of them test positive. Of the 9,900 who don't, 10%, that is 990, test positive anyway. So of the 95 + 990 = 1{,}085 positives, only 95 are real: \frac{95}{1{,}085} \approx 8.8\%. The same test means very different things in different crowds. For a rare condition, what matters most is the specificity: every percent of false alarms among the many healthy people swamps the few true cases. That is why a positive screening result is usually followed by a second, different test (Part 6).

Checking it by brute force

The notebook checks the formula by simulating a million people, each one decided at random, and counting. The draw a random crowd button in the demo does the same with 10,000.

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
prevalence, sensitivity, specificity = 0.01, 0.95, 0.90
N = 1_000_000
has_condition = rng.random(N) < prevalence
positive = np.where(has_condition, rng.random(N) < sensitivity, rng.random(N) > specificity)

exact = sensitivity * prevalence / (sensitivity * prevalence + (1 - specificity) * (1 - prevalence))
print(f"exact  P(D | +) = {exact:.4f}")
print(f"simulated       = {has_condition[positive].mean():.4f}  ({positive.sum():,} positives)")
exact  P(D | +) = 0.0876
simulated       = 0.0878  (108,576 positives)
For the curious: how close should the simulation be? (optional)

Among n positives, each one has the condition with probability p = 0.0876, independently. The fraction that has it is an average of n yes/no results, and its typical error (standard deviation) is

\sqrt{\frac{p\,(1-p)}{n}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.0876 \times 0.9124}{108{,}576}} \approx 0.0009.

The simulation's 0.0878 is 0.0002 away from the exact 0.0876, well within that. In the demo, with only about 1,085 positives, the typical error is about 0.009, so a random crowd usually lands within about one percentage point of 8.8%. Chapter 09 makes this precise: it is the Monte Carlo error.

Part 6

Odds and repeated tests

A person who tests positive usually takes a second test. How should the second result change the 8.8%? Bayes' theorem works again, but there is a form of it that makes repeated updating almost effortless: odds. The odds of H compare it with its opposite,

O(H) = \frac{P(H)}{P(H^c)}, \qquad \text{and back:} \qquad P(H) = \frac{O(H)}{1 + O(H)}.

A probability of 1% is odds of 1 to 99, written \frac{1}{99} \approx 0.0101. Now write Bayes' theorem once for H and once for H^c, and divide. The awkward denominator P(E) is the same in both, so it cancels:

\underbrace{\frac{P(H \mid E)}{P(H^c \mid E)}}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E \mid H^c)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}} \times \underbrace{\frac{P(H)}{P(H^c)}}_{\text{prior odds}}

New odds = old odds × likelihood ratio. Each piece of evidence is one multiplication.

For the screening test, the likelihood ratio of a positive result is \frac{0.95}{0.10} = 9.5: a positive is 9.5 times more likely from someone who has the condition than from someone who doesn't. The prior odds \frac{1}{99} become \frac{9.5}{99} \approx 0.096, which is a probability of \frac{0.096}{1.096} \approx 0.088, as before.

Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior

Suppose a second, independent test of the same kind is also positive. Multiply by 9.5 again: \frac{9.5^2}{99} \approx 0.91, a probability of about 48%. Two positives take you from 1% to 48%. A negative result has likelihood ratio \frac{0.05}{0.90} \approx 0.056, so a third test that comes back negative divides the odds by about 18 and brings the probability down to about 5%. Build your own sequence below. Test 2 is a different test, less sensitive (80%) but far more specific (99%).

Two things stand out in the chart. Its vertical scale is chosen so that each result moves the line by the same distance every time, whatever the starting point. And shuffle the order never changes the end point: multiplication doesn't care about order, so updating one result at a time gives exactly the same answer as using all of them at once. Chapter 04 proves the general version.

The notebook runs the sequence +, +, − on test 1:

def update_odds(p, likelihood_ratio):
    odds = p / (1 - p) * likelihood_ratio
    return odds / (1 + odds)

LR_pos = sensitivity / (1 - specificity)          # 9.5
LR_neg = (1 - sensitivity) / specificity          # a negative result: about 0.056

p = prevalence
for result in ["+", "+", "-"]:
    p = update_odds(p, LR_pos if result == "+" else LR_neg)
    print(f"after a {result} result: P(D) = {p:.4f}")
after a + result: P(D) = 0.0876
after a + result: P(D) = 0.4769
after a - result: P(D) = 0.0482

Multiplying likelihood ratios is only right if the tests are independent given the person's true state: once you know whether someone has the condition, one result tells you nothing more about the other. Two runs of the same test in the same lab often fail for the same reason, such as a substance in the blood that fools both. Then the second positive adds much less than a factor of 9.5, and multiplying would make you far too sure.

For the curious: evidence adds up (optional)

Take logarithms of the odds form, and multiplication becomes addition. For results E_1, \dots, E_n that are independent given H and given H^c,

\log O(H \mid E_1, \dots, E_n) = \log O(H) + \sum_{k=1}^{n} \log \frac{P(E_k \mid H)}{P(E_k \mid H^c)}.

Each result adds its own "weight of evidence" to a running total. With base-10 logarithms, the prior is \log_{10} \frac{1}{99} \approx -2.00, a positive on test 1 adds \log_{10} 9.5 \approx +0.98, and a negative adds \log_{10} 0.056 \approx -1.26. The chart above uses exactly this scale, labelled in probabilities, which is why every result is a step of fixed size. Alan Turing used the same bookkeeping to break the Enigma code, counting evidence in tenths of a base-10 logarithm that he called decibans.

The condition behind it is conditional independence, given H and given H^c:

P(E_1, E_2 \mid H) = P(E_1 \mid H)\,P(E_2 \mid H).

It is a statement about the tests, not a law of nature, and it is the first thing to question when evidence seems to pile up too fast.

Part 7

More than two suspects

Nothing in Bayes' theorem needs exactly two hypotheses. A factory has three machines. Machine A makes 50% of the parts, B makes 30% and C makes 20%. Their defect rates are 1%, 2% and 5%. A part is found to be defective. Which machine made it?

The priors are the production shares, and the likelihoods are the defect rates. The law of total probability gives the evidence, and Bayes' theorem the rest:

P(\text{defective}) = 0.5 (0.01) + 0.3 (0.02) + 0.2 (0.05) = 0.005 + 0.006 + 0.010 = 0.021,
P(A \mid \text{def}) = \tfrac{0.005}{0.021} \approx 0.24, \qquad P(B \mid \text{def}) = \tfrac{0.006}{0.021} \approx 0.29, \qquad P(C \mid \text{def}) = \tfrac{0.010}{0.021} \approx 0.48.

Machine C makes the fewest parts but is now the most likely source, because it is much better at explaining a defect. The table below does the same three steps for you: multiply each prior by its likelihood, sum, divide. Change the shares or the defect rates, and inspect more parts from the same machine.

MachineShare (prior)Defect rateLikelihoodPrior × likelihoodPosterior

Because the computation is always the same three steps, it is worth writing once. The function below is Bayes' theorem for any finite list of hypotheses:

def bayes(prior, likelihood):
    """Posterior over a finite set of hypotheses: prior times likelihood, normalised."""
    unnorm = np.asarray(prior, float) * np.asarray(likelihood, float)
    return unnorm / unnorm.sum()

post = bayes([0.5, 0.3, 0.2], [0.01, 0.02, 0.05])
print("posterior:", ", ".join(f"{m}: {p:.3f}" for m, p in zip("ABC", post)))
posterior: A: 0.238, B: 0.286, C: 0.476
For the curious: the whole theorem as one line of vector arithmetic (optional)

Stack the priors into a vector \boldsymbol{\pi} = (P(H_1), \dots, P(H_m)) and the likelihoods into \boldsymbol{\ell} = (P(E \mid H_1), \dots, P(E \mid H_m)). With \odot for element-by-element multiplication,

\text{posterior} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\pi} \odot \boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\top} \boldsymbol{\ell}},

where the denominator \boldsymbol{\pi}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\ell} = \sum_i \pi_i \ell_i is the law of total probability. That is exactly unnorm / unnorm.sum(). Two facts follow at once. Multiplying all the likelihoods by the same constant changes nothing, because it cancels: only ratios of likelihoods matter, as in Part 6. And a hypothesis with prior 0 stays at 0 forever, whatever the evidence: never give a prior of exactly 0 to something you are not prepared to rule out for good.

Part 8

From three hypotheses to a hundred

So far there was always a short list of hypotheses. In statistics the "hypotheses" are usually the possible values of an unknown number. Say a new treatment cures some fraction \theta ("theta") of patients, and nobody knows \theta: it could be anything from 0 to 1. Treat each value \theta = 0, 0.01, 0.02, \dots, 1 as one hypothesis, 101 of them, all equally likely to begin with.

A patient is cured. Under hypothesis \theta that had probability \theta, so that is the likelihood; a patient who is not cured has likelihood 1 - \theta. Then it is the same bayes function as for the factory, with 101 machines instead of 3.

The bars start flat and, patient after patient, pile up around the true cure rate and get narrower. The answer is not a single number but a whole distribution: a best guess, a range, and the probability of any statement you like, such as "the treatment cures more than half of patients". Make the grid finer and finer, and the sum in Bayes' theorem becomes an integral and the bars become a smooth curve. Chapter 02 builds the vocabulary for that (random variables and distributions), and chapter 04 makes the jump.

Part 9

The chapter on one page

  1. Probabilities obey three rules: never negative, the whole space has probability 1, separate events add. Complement and union follow.
  2. Conditioning shrinks the world: P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}. Independence means that conditioning changes nothing.
  3. Total probability adds up the ways evidence can arise: P(E) = \sum_i P(E \mid H_i) P(H_i).
  4. Bayes' theorem turns P(E \mid H) into P(H \mid E): posterior \propto likelihood × prior. Never ignore the prior: base rates matter.
  5. Odds make updating repeatable: new odds = old odds × likelihood ratio, one multiplication per independent piece of evidence, in any order.

Part 10

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek. The notebook has a code cell ready for each of them.

  1. Complements stay independent. Show that if A and B are independent, then so are A and B^c.
    Hint

    "A" splits into "A and B" plus "A and not B". Use rule 3, then independence.

    Solution
    P(A \cap B^c) = P(A) - P(A \cap B) = P(A) - P(A)\,P(B) = P(A)\,\big(1 - P(B)\big) = P(A)\,P(B^c).

    That is the definition of independence for A and B^c. In words: if learning B tells you nothing about A, learning "not B" can't tell you anything either.

  2. When is a positive worth worrying about? In the screening example, how common would the condition need to be for a single positive test to give P(D \mid +) \ge 0.5? Solve it with the odds form, then check it with the demo.
    Hint

    A probability of 0.5 is odds of 1. The posterior odds are 9.5 times the prior odds.

    Solution

    You need 9.5 \times \frac{p}{1-p} \ge 1, so \frac{p}{1-p} \ge \frac{1}{9.5}, which gives

    p \ge \frac{1}{1 + 9.5} = \frac{1}{10.5} \approx 0.095.

    The condition must affect at least about 9.5% of the people tested. In the demo, a prevalence of 9% gives 48% and 10% gives 51%. This is why doctors test people who already have symptoms: among them, the "prevalence" is much higher than in the general population.

  3. Which second test? A second test has sensitivity 0.80 and specificity 0.99. After a first positive, which is more informative: a positive from this test, or from another copy of the first test? Compare their likelihood ratios.
    Hint

    The likelihood ratio of a positive is sensitivity divided by the false-alarm rate, 1 - \text{specificity}. Then try both in the widget of Part 6.

    Solution

    Test 2 has likelihood ratio \frac{0.80}{0.01} = 80, against 9.5 for test 1. After the first positive the odds are \frac{9.5}{99} \approx 0.096. A positive on test 2 multiplies them by 80, to about 7.7, a probability of 88%. Another positive on test 1 only reaches 48%. Test 2 catches fewer cases, but its positives are far more convincing because it almost never raises a false alarm. (Its negatives are weaker, though: likelihood ratio \frac{0.20}{0.99} \approx 0.20, against 0.056.)

  4. Monty Hall. A prize is behind one of three doors. You pick door 1. The host, who knows where the prize is and always opens an empty door that you did not pick, opens door 3. Use bayes to find the probability that the prize is behind door 2. Be careful with the likelihood of the host opening door 3 under each hypothesis.
    Hint

    The hypotheses are "prize behind door 1, 2, 3", each with prior \frac{1}{3}. The evidence is "the host opened door 3". If the prize is behind door 1, the host can open door 2 or door 3.

    Solution

    The likelihoods of "host opens door 3" are: \frac{1}{2} if the prize is behind door 1 (he picks door 2 or 3 at random), 1 if it is behind door 2 (door 3 is the only empty door he may open), and 0 if it is behind door 3 (he never reveals the prize).

    bayes([1/3, 1/3, 1/3], [1/2, 1, 0])     # -> [0.333, 0.667, 0.0]

    The prize is behind door 2 with probability \frac{2}{3}: switching doubles your chance. The host's choice is evidence, because he was forced to avoid the prize.

  5. A second defective part. In the factory example, a second part from the same (unknown) machine is also defective. Update the posterior. Is updating twice with one defect the same as updating once with a likelihood equal to the defect rate squared? Why?
    Hint

    Use the posterior after the first defect as the new prior, or press found a defective part twice in the widget of Part 7.

    Solution

    Starting from (0.238, 0.286, 0.476) and multiplying by the defect rates (0.01, 0.02, 0.05) gives, after normalizing, A: 0.075, B: 0.179, C: 0.746. Updating once from the original priors with the squared rates, 0.5 \times 0.01^2, 0.3 \times 0.02^2, 0.2 \times 0.05^2, gives exactly the same numbers. It works because, once you know the machine, the two parts are defective independently of each other, so the probability of two defects from a machine is its rate squared. If defects came in batches (a bad day on one machine), that would no longer be true, and neither would the shortcut.

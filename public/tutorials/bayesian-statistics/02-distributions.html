Part 1

Numbers that come out of chance

In chapter 01 the outcomes were things like "a king" or "has the condition". Statistics mostly works with numbers: the number of heads in ten flips, the time until the next bus, the fraction of patients a treatment cures. A random variable is a number produced by an experiment. It is written with a capital letter, X, and a particular value it can take with a small one, x.

A random variable is described by its distribution: which values it can take, and how likely each is. There are two kinds.

  • Discrete random variables take values you can list, such as counts or yes/no (1/0). Each value has a probability, given by the probability mass function (PMF) f(x) = P(X = x), and the probabilities add up to 1: \sum_x f(x) = 1. For a fair die, f(x) = \frac{1}{6} for x = 1, \dots, 6.
  • Continuous random variables take any value in a range, such as times, lengths or proportions. They have a probability density function (PDF) f(x) \ge 0 with total area 1, \int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 1, and probabilities are areas under it:
P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Both kinds have a cumulative distribution function (CDF), F(x) = P(X \le x): the area to the left of x. It climbs from 0 to 1, and the probability of landing between a and b is the difference F(b) - F(a). Drag the edges below.

Density: probability is area

CDF: the area to the left

A density is not a probability. The density f(x) = 2x reaches 2 at x = 1, which no probability can. It is probability per unit of x, like kilograms per metre for a rope of uneven thickness. And for a continuous variable, any single exact value has probability 0: the area above a single point is zero.

What is a distribution?

In plain words: a complete description of an uncertain number: every value it could take, and how likely each one is.

More precisely: the distribution of a random variable X is the rule that gives P(X \in A) for every set of values A. It can be written as a PMF (discrete), a density (continuous), or a CDF (either).

For the curious: indicator functions, and the density as a slope (optional)

An indicator function keeps track of where a density is non-zero: I_{\{x \in S\}} equals 1 if x is in the set S and 0 otherwise. The density that is 2x on [0, 1] and zero elsewhere is written

f(x) = 2x\,I_{\{0 \le x \le 1\}}.

When you integrate, the indicator sets the limits. When you multiply densities, as Bayes' theorem does, the indicators multiply too, which keeps only the values allowed by both: the intersection of their supports.

The CDF and the density are two views of the same thing. The CDF is the running area under the density, and the density is the slope of the CDF:

F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t, \qquad f(x) = F'(x).

Every CDF starts at 0 on the far left, ends at 1 on the far right, and never goes down. Where the density is high, the CDF climbs steeply; where the density is zero, the CDF is flat. Compare the two panels above.

Part 2

The centre and the spread

A whole distribution is a lot of information. Two numbers summarise it surprisingly well: where it is centred, and how spread out it is.

The expectation, or mean, is the probability-weighted average of the values. It is the balance point of the distribution: cut the shape out of cardboard, and it balances on the mean.

\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\,f(x) \quad \text{(discrete)}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \quad \text{(continuous)}.

The same recipe gives the average of any function of X: \mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \int g(x)\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x. The variance is the average squared distance from the mean \mu = \mathbb{E}[X], and the standard deviation (sd) is its square root, back in the units of X:

\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mu)^2\big] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2, \qquad \mathrm{sd}(X) = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}.

Two rules are used constantly. For constants a, b, c, the mean of a sum is the sum of the means, always; variances add only when X and Y are independent, and constants come out squared:

\mathbb{E}[aX + bY + c] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y] + c, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(aX + bY + c) = a^2\,\mathrm{Var}(X) + b^2\,\mathrm{Var}(Y) \ \text{ if independent.}

A worked example

Take the density f(x) = 2x on [0, 1] from Part 1. Its mean and variance are two short integrals:

\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_0^1 x \cdot 2x\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{2}{3}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X^2] = \int_0^1 x^2 \cdot 2x\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{4}{9} = \frac{1}{18}.

To check it by simulation we need random draws from f. The inverse-CDF method turns uniform random numbers into draws from any distribution whose CDF you can invert. Here F(x) = x^2, so if U is uniform between 0 and 1, then X = F^{-1}(U) = \sqrt{U} has density f. In the widget, a uniform number U on the vertical axis goes across to the curve and down to X.

Through the CDF: U goes across, X comes down

The draws so far, and the density 2x

The notebook does the same check with a million draws, and also integrates numerically:

f = lambda x: 2 * x
mean = integrate.quad(lambda x: x * f(x), 0, 1)[0]
second = integrate.quad(lambda x: x**2 * f(x), 0, 1)[0]
print(f"by integration: E[X] = {mean:.4f}, Var(X) = {second - mean**2:.4f}   (exact 2/3, 1/18 = {1/18:.4f})")

x = np.sqrt(rng.random(1_000_000))        # inverse-CDF sampling
print(f"by simulation:  E[X] = {x.mean():.4f}, Var(X) = {x.var():.4f}")
by integration: E[X] = 0.6667, Var(X) = 0.0556   (exact 2/3, 1/18 = 0.0556)
by simulation:  E[X] = 0.6668, Var(X) = 0.0555

The average of a function is not the function of the average: in general \mathbb{E}[g(X)] \neq g(\mathbb{E}[X]). For X uniform between 0 and 1, \mathbb{E}[X^2] = \frac{1}{3}, but (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 = \frac{1}{4}. This comes back in chapter 06, and in exercise 2.

For the curious: why the shortcut for the variance, and why the inverse-CDF trick works (optional)

The variance shortcut. Expand the square and use the linearity of expectation, with \mu a constant:

\mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mu)^2\big] = \mathbb{E}[X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - 2\mu\,\mathbb{E}[X] + \mu^2 = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mu^2.

The inverse-CDF trick. Let U be uniform on (0, 1), so P(U \le u) = u, and let X = F^{-1}(U). Because F is increasing, X \le x exactly when U \le F(x), so

P(X \le x) = P\big(U \le F(x)\big) = F(x).

X has CDF F, which is what we wanted. Steep parts of F catch many values of U in a short stretch of x: that is where the density is high. Most random-number libraries are built on this idea or its relatives.

Part 3

Counting things

A few families of distributions come up again and again. Each one is a shape with one or two dials, its parameters. These are the discrete ones the course uses:

A binomial is a sum of n independent Bernoullis: count the successes. The Poisson describes counts of events that happen independently at a constant average rate: calls to a help desk, typos on a page, radioactive decays. It is also the limit of a Binomial(n, \lambda/n) as n grows: many opportunities, each one unlikely.

Example. A help desk receives on average 3 calls per hour. If calls arrive independently at a steady rate, the number in an hour is X \sim \text{Poisson}(3) (read "X is distributed as Poisson 3"), and

P(X \ge 5) = 1 - \sum_{x=0}^{4} \frac{3^x e^{-3}}{x!} \approx 0.185.

Rates add over time, so the number of calls in two hours is Poisson(6), and a completely quiet two-hour stretch has probability e^{-6} \approx 0.0025.

by_formula = 1 - sum(3**k * np.exp(-3) / np.prod(range(1, k + 1)) for k in range(5))
print(f"P(X >= 5) by formula: {by_formula:.4f}   scipy: {stats.poisson.sf(4, 3):.4f}")
print(f"P(no calls in 2 hours) = {stats.poisson.pmf(0, 6):.5f} = e^-6 = {np.exp(-6):.5f}")
P(X >= 5) by formula: 0.1847   scipy: 0.1847
P(no calls in 2 hours) = 0.00248 = e^-6 = 0.00248
For the curious: why the binomial becomes a Poisson (optional)

Split an hour into n tiny slots, each with a call with probability \lambda/n, independently. The number of calls is Binomial(n, \lambda/n):

P(X = x) = \frac{n!}{x!\,(n-x)!} \Big(\frac{\lambda}{n}\Big)^{x} \Big(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\Big)^{n-x} = \frac{\lambda^x}{x!} \cdot \frac{n!}{(n-x)!\,n^x} \cdot \Big(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\Big)^{n} \Big(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\Big)^{-x}.

As n \to \infty with x fixed, the second factor tends to 1 (it is \frac{n}{n} \cdot \frac{n-1}{n} \cdots, with only x terms), the third tends to e^{-\lambda}, and the last to 1. What is left is the Poisson PMF \frac{\lambda^x e^{-\lambda}}{x!}. Tick compare with the Poisson in the widget and raise n while keeping np near 3 to see it happen.

\Gamma is the gamma function, which extends the factorial to all positive numbers: \Gamma(n) = (n-1)! for whole numbers. The families are connected to each other:

  • Exponential(\lambda) is Gamma(1, \lambda), and the sum of n independent Exponential(\lambda) waiting times is Gamma(n, \lambda).
  • Uniform(0, 1) is Beta(1, 1).
  • If X \sim Normal(\mu, \sigma^2), then \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \sim Normal(0, 1); sums of independent normals are normal, with means and variances adding.
  • t_\nu approaches Normal(0, 1) as \nu \to \infty.

The beta and gamma families will be the workhorses of chapters 05 and 06: the beta for unknown proportions, the gamma for unknown rates. Explore their shapes:

The scipy trap

The table uses the rate \beta for the gamma and \lambda for the exponential, as most Bayesian books do. scipy uses a scale, which is one over the rate: Gamma(\alpha, \beta) is stats.gamma(a=alpha, scale=1/beta), and Exponential(\lambda) is stats.expon(scale=1/lam). In the same way, stats.norm takes the standard deviation, not the variance. Getting this wrong gives answers that look plausible and are wrong; tick show the scipy mistake above to see how plausible. The notebook therefore checks the table against scipy:

alpha, beta = 3.0, 2.0
g = stats.gamma(a=alpha, scale=1 / beta)                 # rate beta  <->  scale 1/beta
print(f"Gamma(3, 2):  scipy mean {g.mean():.3f} vs alpha/beta {alpha / beta:.3f};"
      f"  var {g.var():.3f} vs alpha/beta^2 {alpha / beta**2:.3f}")
b = stats.beta(2, 5)
print(f"Beta(2, 5):   scipy mean {b.mean():.4f} vs {2 / 7:.4f};  var {b.var():.5f} vs {2 * 5 / (7**2 * 8):.5f}")

# A sum of 4 Exponential(2) waiting times is Gamma(4, 2)
waits = rng.exponential(scale=1 / 2, size=(200_000, 4)).sum(axis=1)
print(f"sum of 4 Exp(2): mean {waits.mean():.3f} (Gamma(4,2) mean {4 / 2}),  var {waits.var():.3f} ({4 / 2**2})")

For the curious: where the constants in front come from (optional)

Every density must have total area 1, and the constant in front is exactly what makes that true. The gamma function is defined by an integral,

\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-x}\,\mathrm{d}x, \qquad \Gamma(\alpha + 1) = \alpha\,\Gamma(\alpha), \qquad \Gamma(1) = 1,

so \Gamma(n) = (n-1)!. Substituting x = \beta t gives \int_0^\infty t^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta t}\,\mathrm{d}t = \Gamma(\alpha)/\beta^\alpha, which is why the gamma density carries \beta^\alpha / \Gamma(\alpha). In the same way, the beta function

B(\alpha, \beta) = \int_0^1 x^{\alpha-1} (1-x)^{\beta-1}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\,\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)}

is the constant of the beta density. This matters for Bayesian work: when a posterior has the shape x^{a-1}(1-x)^{b-1}, you know at once that it is a Beta(a, b) and what its constant is, without doing any integral. Chapters 05 and 06 use this trick again and again.

The sum of independent Exponential(\lambda) waiting times is consistent with Gamma(n, \lambda) at least in its first two moments: by the rules of Part 2, the sum has mean n/\lambda and variance n/\lambda^2, exactly the gamma's \alpha/\beta and \alpha/\beta^2. The full proof that the shapes match uses a convolution or moment-generating functions [2].

Part 5

Why bell curves are everywhere

Back to the demo at the top. Let X_1, \dots, X_n be independent draws from the same distribution, with mean \mu and a finite variance \sigma^2, and let \bar X_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i X_i be their average. The central limit theorem says that

\frac{\sqrt{n}\,(\bar X_n - \mu)}{\sigma} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Normal}(0, 1) \qquad \text{as } n \to \infty.

Whatever the shape of a single draw, the average of many is approximately normal, centred on μ, with spread σ/√n.

That is why normal distributions show up wherever a measurement is the sum of many small effects, and it is why the frequentist intervals of chapter 03 and the posterior approximations of later chapters work. Each button below sets the demo at the top and draws 10,000 averages:

A single exponential waiting time is very skewed: most are short, a few are long. The average of 5 is already less skewed, and the average of 30 is close to the normal curve with standard deviation 1/\sqrt{30} \approx 0.18. A rare event (a yes 5% of the time) is much slower to get there: with n = 30 its averages are still lumpy and lopsided. Exercise 4 asks why.

The theorem has conditions, and the Cauchy distribution breaks them: its tails are so heavy that it has no mean and no variance. The average of 100 Cauchy draws is exactly as wild as a single one, and the histogram never narrows. Averaging only tames things that have a finite variance.

For the curious: where σ/√n comes from (optional)

The mean and spread of an average follow from the rules of Part 2 alone, with no need for the full theorem. With independent draws,

\mathbb{E}[\bar X_n] = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{E}[X_i] = \mu, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n) = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}.

So the sd of the average is \sigma/\sqrt{n}: to halve the spread you need four times as many draws. The demo's readouts check both formulas as you draw. What the theorem adds is the shape: the bell. How quickly it appears depends on how lopsided a single draw is. The lopsidedness (skewness) of the average shrinks like 1/\sqrt{n} times the skewness of one draw, which is 2 for the exponential and about 4.1 for a yes/no event with probability 5%.

Part 6

Bayes' theorem for densities

At the end of chapter 01, the unknown cure rate \theta was replaced by 101 hypotheses, \theta = 0, 0.01, \dots, 1, and Bayes' theorem gave each one a probability. With the vocabulary of this chapter, the cure rate is a continuous random variable: the prior is a density p(\theta), the likelihood is the probability of the data p(y \mid \theta), and the sum over hypotheses becomes an integral:

p(\theta \mid y) = \frac{p(y \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)}{\int p(y \mid \theta')\,p(\theta')\,\mathrm{d}\theta'}.

It is the same theorem. Below, 3 of 10 patients were cured and the prior is flat. Make the grid of hypotheses finer: the bars (each one's probability divided by the spacing, so that they are on the density scale) close in on a smooth curve, which turns out to be the Beta(4, 8) density of Part 4.

Chapter 04 puts this to work with grids of thousands of hypotheses; chapter 05 shows why the answer is exactly a beta density; and chapter 03 first looks at the likelihood on its own, which is how classical statistics uses it.

Part 7

The chapter on one page

  1. A random variable is a number produced by chance. Its distribution is a PMF (discrete), a density (continuous; probabilities are areas), or a CDF (either).
  2. Mean and variance summarise a distribution: \mathbb{E}[X] = \int x f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x, \mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mu^2. Means always add; variances add for independent variables.
  3. Standard families: Bernoulli, binomial, geometric and Poisson for counts; uniform, exponential, gamma, beta, normal and t for measurements. Know their means and variances.
  4. scipy uses scales, not rates: Gamma(α, β) is stats.gamma(a=alpha, scale=1/beta), and stats.norm takes the sd.
  5. The central limit theorem: averages of n independent draws with finite variance are approximately Normal(μ, σ²/n).

Part 8

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek. The notebook has a code cell ready for each of them.

  1. Another density. For f(x) = 3x^2 on [0, 1], find \mathbb{E}[X], \mathrm{Var}(X), and a sampler based on the inverse CDF. Check all three in code.
    Hint

    Follow the worked example of Part 2. The CDF is F(x) = \int_0^x 3t^2\,\mathrm{d}t.

    Solution
    \mathbb{E}[X] = \int_0^1 3x^3\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{3}{4}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X^2] = \int_0^1 3x^4\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{3}{5}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X) = \frac{3}{5} - \frac{9}{16} = \frac{3}{80} = 0.0375.

    The CDF is F(x) = x^3, so X = U^{1/3}. A million draws of rng.random(1_000_000) ** (1/3) give a mean of 0.7501 and a variance of 0.0375.

  2. The average of a function. Show that \mathbb{E}[1/X] \neq 1/\mathbb{E}[X] for X \sim Gamma(3, 2) by computing both. You will need \mathbb{E}[1/X] = \frac{\beta}{\alpha - 1} for \alpha > 1; prove it by writing the integral and recognising a gamma density.
    Hint

    Multiply the gamma density by 1/x: the power of x drops by one. What gamma density has that power, and what is its constant?

    Solution
    \mathbb{E}[1/X] = \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{x} \cdot \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta x}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(\alpha-1)}{\beta^{\alpha-1}} = \frac{\beta}{\alpha - 1}.

    The integral of x^{\alpha-2} e^{-\beta x} is the constant of a Gamma(\alpha - 1, \beta) density, and \Gamma(\alpha) = (\alpha - 1)\Gamma(\alpha - 1). For Gamma(3, 2): \mathbb{E}[1/X] = \frac{2}{2} = 1, but 1/\mathbb{E}[X] = 1/1.5 \approx 0.667. Small values of X make 1/X very large, and they pull its average up.

  3. Waiting for the fifth call. Calls arrive at rate 3 per hour. What is the distribution of the waiting time until the fifth call, and its mean? Verify by simulation.
    Hint

    The waits between calls are independent Exponential(3). Part 4 says what their sum is.

    Solution

    The wait until the fifth call is the sum of five independent Exponential(3) waits, so it is Gamma(5, 3), with mean \frac{5}{3} hours, that is 100 minutes, and variance \frac{5}{9} (an sd of about 45 minutes). In code: rng.exponential(scale=1/3, size=(100_000, 5)).sum(axis=1).mean() comes out at about 1.667. Watch the scale: scale=3 would give a mean of 15 hours.

  4. A slow bell. Repeat the CLT experiment with a Bernoulli(0.05) variable (the "rare event" in the demo). How large must n be before the histogram of averages looks normal? Why is it slower than for the exponential?
    Hint

    Look at how lopsided one draw is: almost always 0, rarely 1. The box "where σ/√n comes from" in Part 5 says how lopsidedness shrinks with n.

    Solution

    One draw is extremely lopsided: its skewness is \frac{1 - 2p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}} \approx 4.1, against 2 for the exponential. The skewness of the average is that divided by \sqrt{n}. The exponential at n = 30 has 2/\sqrt{30} \approx 0.37; to get the same for the rare event you need (4.1/0.37)^2 \approx 130 draws. Averages are also stuck on the grid 0, \frac{1}{n}, \frac{2}{n}, \dots, so with np around 1 or 2 the histogram is a few lonely bars. In the demo, n = 200 (where np = 10) looks convincingly normal; n = 30 does not.

  5. The peak of a beta. Show that the Beta(\alpha, \beta) density with \alpha, \beta > 1 has its mode (its highest point) at \frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + \beta - 2}.
    Hint

    Maximise the logarithm of the density instead: it has the same peak and an easier derivative. The constant in front doesn't matter.

    Solution

    The log density is (\alpha - 1)\log x + (\beta - 1)\log(1 - x) plus a constant. Setting its derivative to zero:

    \frac{\alpha - 1}{x} - \frac{\beta - 1}{1 - x} = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x = \frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + \beta - 2}.

    With \alpha, \beta > 1 the log density goes to -\infty at both ends, so this single critical point is the maximum. For Beta(2, 5) the mode is \frac{1}{5} = 0.2, a little left of the mean \frac{2}{7} \approx 0.29; check it in the widget of Part 4.

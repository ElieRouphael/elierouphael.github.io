Bayesian linear regression

A kiosk records the day's high temperature and the number of iced drinks it sells, for 25 days. How many more drinks does each extra degree bring, and how sure can we be? Part 1

The model

We observe n pairs (x_i, y_i), here a temperature and a number of drinks, and model the outcome as a straight line plus noise:

y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Normal}(0, \sigma^2).

With several predictors there are more \betas, and matrix notation keeps it tidy. Stack the outcomes in a vector y, and the predictors in an n \times p design matrix X whose first column is all ones (for the intercept). Then y = X\beta + \varepsilon, and the likelihood is

p(y \mid \beta, \sigma^2) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2} \exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\,\lVert y - X\beta \rVert^2\Big).

A key identity. Let \hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y be the least-squares estimate, and \text{RSS} = \lVert y - X\hat\beta \rVert^2 its residual sum of squares. Writing y - X\beta = (y - X\hat\beta) + X(\hat\beta - \beta) and expanding, the cross term vanishes (exercise 1), so

\lVert y - X\beta \rVert^2 = \text{RSS} + (\beta - \hat\beta)^\top X^\top X\,(\beta - \hat\beta).

As a function of \beta, the likelihood is a multivariate normal kernel centred at \hat\beta. That is what makes everything below exact.

What is Bayesian regression?

In plain words: instead of one best-fitting line, a whole cloud of plausible lines, each weighted by how well it explains the data and how plausible it was to begin with.

More precisely: a posterior distribution over the coefficients \beta and the noise level \sigma of a linear model, from which any question about the line, a prediction, or a quantity derived from them gets a probability answer.

Design matrix X
One row per observation, one column per predictor, plus a column of ones.
Coefficients \beta
The intercept and the slopes.
Least squares \hat\beta
The line that minimises the sum of squared vertical distances to the points.
Residuals
The vertical distances y_i - x_i^\top\hat\beta; RSS is the sum of their squares.
Part 2

The reference prior

With no strong prior information, the standard choice is flat on \beta and \frac{1}{\sigma^2} on the variance, the Jeffreys-style prior of chapter 07:

p(\beta, \sigma^2) \propto \frac{1}{\sigma^2}.

It is improper, but the posterior is proper when n > p and the columns of X are not redundant. By the identity of Part 1,

p(\beta, \sigma^2 \mid y) \;\propto\; (\sigma^2)^{-n/2-1} \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\text{RSS} + (\beta - \hat\beta)^\top X^\top X (\beta - \hat\beta)}{2\sigma^2}\Big).

Read it in two steps, exactly as in chapter 06.

Given \sigma^2, the dependence on \beta is a normal kernel:

\beta \mid \sigma^2, y \sim \text{Normal}\big(\hat\beta,\ \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}\big).

Integrating out \beta (a normal integral, which contributes a factor (\sigma^2)^{p/2}) leaves

p(\sigma^2 \mid y) \propto (\sigma^2)^{-\frac{n-p}{2}-1} e^{-\text{RSS}/(2\sigma^2)} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \sigma^2 \mid y \sim \text{InvGamma}\Big(\frac{n-p}{2},\ \frac{\text{RSS}}{2}\Big).

Each coefficient on its own is a t with n - p degrees of freedom:

\beta_j \mid y \sim t_{n-p}\Big(\hat\beta_j,\ s^2\big[(X^\top X)^{-1}\big]_{jj}\Big), \qquad s^2 = \frac{\text{RSS}}{n - p}.

Under the reference prior, Bayesian and classical regression give the same numbers.

These are exactly the estimates, standard errors and t intervals that classical least squares reports [1], [3]. The Bayesian version adds the probability interpretation ("the slope is between 3.1 and 5.2 with probability 95%") and, through sampling, easy answers to questions about any function of the parameters.

Part 3

Temperature and iced drinks

The kiosk's 25 days are simulated, so the true relationship is known: intercept 20, slope 4.5 drinks per °C, noise sd 12. We sample the posterior exactly by composition, as in chapter 06: draw \sigma^2 from its inverse-gamma, then \beta from its normal given that \sigma^2. That is what draws the lines of the demo.

XtX_inv = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
beta_hat = XtX_inv @ X.T @ drinks
rss = np.sum((drinks - X @ beta_hat) ** 2)

S = 20_000
sigma2 = 1 / rng.gamma((n - p) / 2, 2 / rss, size=S)                 # InvGamma((n-p)/2, RSS/2)
L = np.linalg.cholesky(XtX_inv)
beta = beta_hat + np.sqrt(sigma2)[:, None] * (rng.standard_normal((S, p)) @ L.T)
intercept  least squares   24.63 ± 23.43 (classical 95% CI)  |  posterior mean   24.66, 95% credible (  1.67,  48.34)
slope      least squares    4.16 ±  1.04 (classical 95% CI)  |  posterior mean    4.15, 95% credible (  3.09,   5.17)
noise sd: s = 14.55; posterior median of σ = 14.78
P(slope > 4 | data) = 0.620

Because we have samples, any derived quantity has a posterior for free. At what temperature do expected sales reach 150 drinks? Solve \beta_0 + \beta_1 x = 150 for each sample: x = \frac{150 - \beta_0}{\beta_1}. The notebook gets a median of 30.2 °C, with a 95% interval of (28.0, 33.5) °C, a question with no simple classical formula.

The samples also show something surprising about the intercept and the slope:

Posterior draws of (intercept, slope)

Temperature for 150 expected drinks

The strong negative correlation happens because all the temperatures are far from zero. A line through the cloud of points can pivot: a steeper line needs a lower intercept to pass through the same middle. Centring the predictor removes the correlation and gives the intercept a meaning, "expected sales on an average day", which is worth doing in practice.

Part 4

Two kinds of band

Two different questions can be asked about a new temperature \tilde x:

  • What are the expected sales at \tilde x? The mean \beta_0 + \beta_1\tilde x is uncertain only because \beta is. Its posterior gives a credible band for the regression line.
  • How many drinks will be sold on one particular day at \tilde x? That adds the day-to-day noise: \tilde y = \beta_0 + \beta_1\tilde x + \varepsilon. Its posterior predictive distribution gives a much wider prediction band. Analytically it is t_{n-p}\big(\tilde x^\top\hat\beta,\ s^2(1 + \tilde x^\top (X^\top X)^{-1}\tilde x)\big); by simulation it takes one extra line: add a normal draw with each sample's own \sigma.

Tick both bands in the demo at the top. The line is known far better than any single day. Below, the two distributions at one temperature:

expected sales (credible) one day's sales (predictive) 

share of observed days inside the 95% prediction band: 1.00

All 25 observed days fall inside the 95% prediction band. With 25 days, one or two outside would have been just as normal; none outside is no cause for concern either.

Part 5

An informative prior, and ridge

When prior information exists, the conjugate prior is chapter 06's normal-inverse-gamma, now for a vector:

\beta \mid \sigma^2 \sim \text{Normal}(m_0,\ \sigma^2 V_0), \qquad \sigma^2 \sim \text{InvGamma}(a_0, b_0).

Completing the square in \beta once more gives [2], [4]

V_n = (V_0^{-1} + X^\top X)^{-1}, \qquad m_n = V_n\,(V_0^{-1} m_0 + X^\top y), \qquad a_n = a_0 + \frac{n}{2}, \qquad b_n = b_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}\big(y^\top y + m_0^\top V_0^{-1} m_0 - m_n^\top V_n^{-1} m_n\big),

with \beta \mid \sigma^2, y \sim Normal(m_n, \sigma^2 V_n) and \sigma^2 \mid y \sim InvGamma(a_n, b_n). Precisions add again: V_n^{-1} = V_0^{-1} + X^\top X, and the posterior mean combines the prior mean and the data, each weighted by its precision.

Ridge regression is a posterior mean

Take m_0 = 0 and V_0 = \frac{1}{\lambda} I for the slopes (the intercept is usually left alone, by centring). Then m_n = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top y, which is exactly the ridge regression estimator of machine learning [5]. The ridge penalty \lambda is a prior belief that the coefficients are small, and a larger \lambda is a more confident prior. Tighten it below, with all 25 days and with only the first 5:

Posterior mean slope as λ grows

The fitted lines at this λ

xc = temp - temp.mean()                           # centre, so the intercept prior can stay vague
def posterior_slope(lam, k):
    Xk = np.column_stack([np.ones(k), xc[:k]])
    V0_inv = np.diag([1e-8, lam])                 # almost flat on the intercept, precision lam on the slope
    Vn = np.linalg.inv(V0_inv + Xk.T @ Xk)
    return (Vn @ Xk.T @ drinks[:k])[1]

A stronger prior pulls the slope towards 0, and with less data it gives way sooner: 5 days of data are overruled by a prior that 25 days can still resist. That is the whole logic of regularisation, seen as a prior.

Part 6

Checking the model

A posterior is only as good as the model behind it. A posterior predictive check asks: if the model were right, would data like ours look typical [6], [7]? Simulate many replicated data sets y^{\text{rep}} from the posterior predictive at the observed temperatures, compute a test statistic T on each, and see where T(y) for the real data falls. A value in the far tail signals a feature of the data that the model cannot reproduce.

The notebook uses two statistics: the largest absolute residual from the least-squares line (sensitive to outliers), and the correlation between the residuals and temperature squared (sensitive to curvature that a straight line misses).

Part 7

The chapter on one page

  1. Linear model: y = X\beta + \varepsilon. The key identity makes the likelihood a normal kernel in \beta, centred at least squares.
  2. Reference prior p(\beta, \sigma^2) \propto \frac{1}{\sigma^2}: \sigma^2 is inverse-gamma, \beta given \sigma^2 is normal, each coefficient is a t. The numbers match classical regression.
  3. Samples answer any derived question, such as the temperature for 150 expected drinks.
  4. Credible band for the line, prediction band for a single day: the second is much wider.
  5. Informative prior: precisions add. Ridge regression is the posterior mean under a normal prior centred at zero. Posterior predictive checks test whether the model can reproduce features of the data.

Part 8

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek. The notebook has a code cell ready for each of them.

  1. The cross term. Show that the cross term in \lVert y - X\beta \rVert^2 vanishes, using the normal equations X^\top X\hat\beta = X^\top y.
    Hint

    The cross term is 2\,(y - X\hat\beta)^\top X(\hat\beta - \beta). Look at X^\top(y - X\hat\beta).

    Solution

    By the normal equations, X^\top(y - X\hat\beta) = X^\top y - X^\top X\hat\beta = 0: the residuals are orthogonal to every column of X. So the cross term 2\,(y - X\hat\beta)^\top X(\hat\beta - \beta) = 2\,\big(X^\top(y - X\hat\beta)\big)^\top(\hat\beta - \beta) = 0, whatever \beta is. Geometrically, least squares projects y onto the space spanned by the columns of X, and the residual is perpendicular to that space: Pythagoras does the rest.

  2. Centring. Centre the temperature and refit. What happens to the posterior correlation between intercept and slope, and to the meaning of the intercept?
    Hint

    Tick "centre the temperature" in the widget of Part 3. The correlation comes from the off-diagonal of (X^\top X)^{-1}.

    Solution

    Uncentred, the correlation is −0.97. Centred, the off-diagonal of X^\top X is \sum_i (x_i - \bar x) = 0, so (X^\top X)^{-1} is diagonal and the correlation is 0. The slope is unchanged; the intercept becomes the expected sales at the average temperature of 21.7 °C, which is 114.8 drinks, the average sales: a meaningful, well-determined number instead of sales at 0 °C, far outside the data.

  3. A hot day. Using the samples, compute the posterior probability that a 30 °C day sells more than 170 drinks. Compare with the analytic t predictive distribution.
    Hint

    For each sample, draw one day's sales: \beta_0 + 30\beta_1 plus a normal draw with that sample's \sigma. The widget of Part 4 shows the same distribution.

    Solution

    Both give about 0.096. The predictive is a t_{23} centred at 149.3 drinks with scale 15.4: one day in ten at 30 °C beats 170. The expected sales at 30 °C are much better known, (138.7, 159.8) with 95% probability; it is the day-to-day noise that makes 170 reachable.

  4. Five days and a good prior. Use the informative prior with m_0 = (0, 5), V_0 = \mathrm{diag}(10^6, 0.1), a_0 = 2, b_0 = 200, on the first 5 days only. How much does it help, compared with the reference prior on the same 5 days?
    Hint

    Use the formulas of Part 5 for m_n, V_n, a_n, b_n. The slope's marginal posterior is a t with 2a_n degrees of freedom and scale \sqrt{\frac{b_n}{a_n}\,[V_n]_{11}}.

    Solution

    With the reference prior, 5 days give a slope of 4.57 with a 95% interval of (0.69, 8.45): almost useless, because a t with 3 degrees of freedom has very heavy tails. With the informative prior (slope about 5, with sd \sigma\sqrt{0.1}; noise variance around 200), the slope is 4.59 with (2.71, 6.48): half the width. A good prior is worth a lot when data are scarce, which is exactly when you need it, and it was a correct prior here, since the true slope is 4.5.

  5. A curve. Generate data with a curved relationship (add 0.3\,(x - 23)^2 to the mean) and rerun the posterior predictive check. Which statistic detects the problem?
    Hint

    Press "curved data" in the widget of Part 6. It adds the curve to the notebook's data, keeping the same noise.

    Solution

    The curvature statistic catches it: well under 1% of replicated data sets (about 1 in 1,000 or fewer) have a correlation between residuals and temperature² as large as the real one, a clear failure. The largest residual does not: about 80% of replicates have a residual at least as large. A posterior predictive check is only as good as the statistic you choose, so choose statistics that target the ways you suspect the model could be wrong.

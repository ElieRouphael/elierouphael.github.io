Part 1

Conjugacy

In chapter 04 the posterior was computed numerically on a grid. For some pairs of likelihood and prior, no computation is needed at all: the posterior turns out to be in the same family as the prior, with updated parameters. Bayes' theorem then reduces to adding a few numbers.

What is a conjugate prior?

In plain words: a prior whose shape survives the update. Before the data it is, say, a beta curve; after the data it is still a beta curve, only moved and sharpened.

More precisely: a family of priors is conjugate to a likelihood if, for every data set, the posterior belongs to the same family [1]. Updating then means updating the family's parameters.

Hyperparameters
The parameters of the prior, such as \alpha and \beta, as opposed to the model's parameter \theta.
Kernel
The part of a density that involves the variable, without the constant in front: \theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1} is the kernel of a Beta(a, b).
Update rule
How the data change the hyperparameters, such as "add the successes to \alpha".

The way to find a conjugate posterior is the proportionality trick of chapter 04: multiply likelihood and prior, drop every factor that doesn't involve the parameter, and recognise what is left as the kernel of a known density.

Part 2

The beta-binomial model

The model. y successes in n independent trials with success probability \theta, and a beta prior:

y \mid \theta \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \theta), \qquad \theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta).

The posterior. Multiply, and keep only what involves \theta:

p(\theta \mid y) \;\propto\; \underbrace{\theta^{y}(1-\theta)^{n-y}}_{\text{likelihood}}\; \underbrace{\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}}_{\text{prior}} = \theta^{\alpha+y-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta+n-y-1}.

That is the kernel of a beta density, so

\boxed{\;\theta \mid y \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + y,\ \beta + n - y)\;}

Add the successes to α and the failures to β. That is the whole update.

The flat prior of chapter 04 is Beta(1, 1), so the shooter's posterior, 13 makes and 7 misses, was Beta(14, 8) all along: no grid needed.

A prior is data you have already seen

Beta(\alpha, \beta) behaves exactly like having already seen \alpha successes and \beta failures, so \alpha + \beta is the prior's effective sample size: what it is worth in observations. The posterior mean makes this precise. It is a weighted average of the prior mean and the observed proportion:

\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = \frac{\alpha + y}{\alpha + \beta + n} = \underbrace{\frac{\alpha + \beta}{\alpha + \beta + n}}_{\text{weight on prior}} \cdot \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{n}{\alpha + \beta + n}}_{\text{weight on data}} \cdot \frac{y}{n}.

The weights are the prior's worth and the real sample size, each divided by their total. As n grows, the data win. Pulling an estimate towards a prior value like this is called shrinkage. Set a prior by its typical value and what it's worth, then add data:

For the curious: the weighted average, and what "worth 2 observations" means (optional)

The weighted-average form is just algebra: write \alpha = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}(\alpha+\beta) and y = \frac{y}{n} n in the numerator of \frac{\alpha + y}{\alpha + \beta + n}, and split the fraction in two. The weights are positive and add up to 1, so the posterior mean always lies between the prior mean and \frac{y}{n} (exercise 1).

Even the flat Beta(1, 1) prior is "worth" 2 observations: one imaginary success and one imaginary failure. That is why its posterior mean for the shooter is \frac{14}{22} \approx 0.636, not 0.65. Laplace used exactly this in 1774 for his "rule of succession": after y successes in n trials, the probability of a success next time is \frac{y+1}{n+2}.

Part 3

An A/B test

Back to the shop of the demo at the top. Version A, the current checkout page, was shown to 240 visitors and 26 bought something. Version B was shown to 250 and 41 bought. Past data say this shop's conversion rates are usually between 5% and 20%, and a Beta(3, 27) prior encodes that: its mean is 0.10 and it is worth 30 visitors, which is weak next to the real 240 and 250. Both versions get the same prior, so any difference between the posteriors comes from the data:

\theta_A \mid y \sim \text{Beta}(3 + 26,\ 27 + 214), \qquad \theta_B \mid y \sim \text{Beta}(3 + 41,\ 27 + 209).

"Is B better?" is the posterior probability P(\theta_B > \theta_A \mid y). There is a formula (exercise 3), but Monte Carlo is simpler and more general: draw many pairs (\theta_A, \theta_B) from the two posteriors, and count how often \theta_B > \theta_A. Each dot below is one such pair; the dots above the diagonal are the draws in which B is better.

The same draws give the whole posterior of the difference \theta_B - \theta_A and of the relative lift \frac{\theta_B}{\theta_A} - 1 at no extra cost: compute it for each pair, and look at the histogram (the lower chart in the demo).

a0, b0 = 3, 27
nA, yA = 240, 26
nB, yB = 250, 41
postA = stats.beta(a0 + yA, b0 + nA - yA)
postB = stats.beta(a0 + yB, b0 + nB - yB)

S = 200_000
tA, tB = postA.rvs(S, random_state=rng), postB.rvs(S, random_state=rng)
p_better = (tB > tA).mean()
exact = integrate.quad(lambda t: postA.pdf(t) * postB.sf(t), 0, 1)[0]   # P(θB > θA) = ∫ pA(t) P(θB > t) dt
lift = tB / tA - 1
version A: observed 0.108, posterior mean 0.107, 95% credible interval (0.073, 0.147)
version B: observed 0.164, posterior mean 0.157, 95% credible interval (0.117, 0.202)

P(θB > θA | data): Monte Carlo 0.9584, numerical integral 0.9586
relative lift of B: median +47%, 95% interval (-5%, +130%)
P(lift > 20%) = 0.818

This is the kind of answer decision-makers actually want: "B is better with probability about 96%; the most likely lift is around +45%, but the data are still compatible with anything from a small loss to more than doubling the conversion rate." A p-value answers a different question: how surprising the data would be if A and B were identical.

Part 4

Predicting future successes

How many of the next 100 visitors to version B will buy? The posterior predictive of chapter 04 has a closed form here, the beta-binomial distribution. With (\alpha', \beta') the posterior parameters,

p(\tilde y \mid y) = \int_0^1 \binom{m}{\tilde y}\theta^{\tilde y}(1-\theta)^{m-\tilde y}\,\frac{\theta^{\alpha'-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta'-1}}{B(\alpha', \beta')}\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \binom{m}{\tilde y}\frac{B(\alpha' + \tilde y,\ \beta' + m - \tilde y)}{B(\alpha', \beta')},

where B is the beta function of chapter 02. The integral is easy because the integrand is an unnormalised beta density: its area is a beta function. scipy calls the result stats.betabinom.

plug-in binomial beta-binomial predictive 

m = 100                                           # the next 100 visitors to version B
pred = stats.betabinom(m, a0 + yB, b0 + nB - yB)
sim = rng.binomial(m, postB.rvs(200_000, random_state=rng))
print(f"buyers among next {m} B visitors: formula mean {pred.mean():.2f} sd {pred.std():.2f} | "
      f"simulation mean {sim.mean():.2f} sd {sim.std():.2f}")
print(f"P(at least 20 buyers) = {pred.sf(19):.3f}")
buyers among next 100 B visitors: formula mean 15.71 sd 4.23 | simulation mean 15.73 sd 4.22
P(at least 20 buyers) = 0.183

The simulation check is the recipe of chapter 04 made concrete: draw a \theta from the posterior, then a \tilde y from the binomial with that \theta; repeat. It agrees with the formula to two decimals.

Part 5

The Poisson-gamma model

The model. Counts y_1, \dots, y_n of events in equal periods, at an unknown rate \lambda, with a gamma prior (shape \alpha, rate \beta):

y_i \mid \lambda \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Poisson}(\lambda), \qquad \lambda \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta).

The posterior. The likelihood is \prod_i \frac{\lambda^{y_i} e^{-\lambda}}{y_i!} \propto \lambda^{\sum y_i} e^{-n\lambda}, so

p(\lambda \mid y) \propto \lambda^{\sum y_i} e^{-n\lambda} \cdot \lambda^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta\lambda} = \lambda^{\alpha + \sum y_i - 1} e^{-(\beta + n)\lambda},
\boxed{\;\lambda \mid y \sim \text{Gamma}\Big(\alpha + \sum_i y_i,\ \beta + n\Big)\;}

Add the total count to the shape, and the number of periods to the rate.

The prior acts like \beta earlier periods in which \alpha events were seen in total, and the posterior mean is again a weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean:

\mathbb{E}[\lambda \mid y] = \frac{\alpha + \sum y_i}{\beta + n} = \frac{\beta}{\beta + n} \cdot \frac{\alpha}{\beta} + \frac{n}{\beta + n} \cdot \bar y.

Predicting a new count. For tomorrow's count \tilde y, the posterior predictive is a negative binomial: in scipy, stats.nbinom(a1, b1 / (b1 + 1)), with (\alpha', \beta') = (a1, b1) the posterior parameters. Its variance is larger than its mean, unlike a Poisson's, because it includes our uncertainty about \lambda.

Example. A café owner records customer complaints on each of 7 days: 6, 3, 7, 5, 8, 4, 6. The chain's other cafés typically get about 3 a day, and the owner encodes that as a Gamma(6, 2) prior: mean 3, worth 2 days of data. The posterior is Gamma(6 + 39, 2 + 7) = Gamma(45, 9). Edit the days, or add one:

The rate λ (complaints per day)

Tomorrow's complaints

counts = np.array([6, 3, 7, 5, 8, 4, 6])
a0, b0 = 6.0, 2.0
a1, b1 = a0 + counts.sum(), b0 + len(counts)
post = stats.gamma(a=a1, scale=1 / b1)
pred = stats.nbinom(a1, b1 / (b1 + 1))
posterior Gamma(45, 9): mean 5.000 = 0.22 x prior mean 3.0 + 0.78 x sample mean 5.571
95% credible interval for λ: (3.65, 6.56) complaints per day
tomorrow: predictive mean 5.00, var 5.56 (a Poisson would have var = mean); simulated var 5.56
P(10 or more complaints tomorrow) = 0.0398
For the curious: why predictions are wider than a Poisson (optional)

The law of total variance splits the uncertainty about a prediction in two:

\mathrm{Var}(\tilde y) = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Var}(\tilde y \mid \lambda)\big] + \mathrm{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[\tilde y \mid \lambda]\big).

The first term is the noise you would have even if you knew \lambda exactly; the second is the extra spread from not knowing it. For a Poisson count both pieces are easy, because a Poisson's mean and variance are both \lambda. Exercise 5 works them out: for the café, 5.00 from the Poisson noise plus 0.56 from the uncertainty about the rate. With more days of data the second term shrinks towards zero, and the predictive approaches a plain Poisson.

Part 6

Choosing the prior's numbers

Reading a prior as imaginary data makes conjugate priors easy to set:

  1. Choose a prior mean m from background knowledge: a typical conversion rate, a typical number of complaints.
  2. Choose what the prior is worth, k observations: how many real data points would make you change your mind.
  3. Solve: for a beta prior, \alpha = mk and \beta = (1-m)k; for a gamma prior on a Poisson rate, \beta = k periods and \alpha = mk events.

Part 7

The chapter on one page

  1. Conjugate prior: the posterior stays in the prior's family. Find it by multiplying and recognising a kernel.
  2. Beta-binomial: Beta(\alpha, \beta) + y of n → Beta(\alpha + y, \beta + n - y). Predictive: beta-binomial.
  3. Poisson-gamma: Gamma(\alpha, \beta) + counts over n periods → Gamma(\alpha + \sum y_i, \beta + n). Predictive: negative binomial.
  4. A prior is imaginary data, worth \alpha + \beta trials or \beta periods. The posterior mean is a weighted average of prior mean and data: shrinkage.
  5. Monte Carlo answers any question about several posteriors at once, such as P(\theta_B > \theta_A) or the lift, by drawing and counting.

Part 8

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek. The notebook has a code cell ready for each of them.

  1. Always in between. Show that the posterior mean of the beta-binomial model always lies between the prior mean and \frac{y}{n}.
    Hint

    Look at the weights in the weighted-average formula of Part 2.

    Solution

    The posterior mean is w \cdot \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta} + (1 - w) \cdot \frac{y}{n} with w = \frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha+\beta+n}. Since \alpha, \beta > 0 and n \ge 1, the weight w is strictly between 0 and 1. A weighted average with weights in (0, 1) lies between the two numbers it averages. (For the sceptical prior of chapter 04 this was not true, because that prior was not a beta.)

  2. Flat or strong. In the A/B test, redo the analysis with flat Beta(1, 1) priors and with a strong Beta(30, 270) prior. How do P(\theta_B > \theta_A) and the lift interval change?
    Hint

    In the demo at the top, Beta(1, 1) is a typical rate of 50% worth 2 visitors; Beta(30, 270) is 10% worth 300.

    Solution
    PriorP(\theta_B > \theta_A)Median lift95% lift interval
    Beta(1, 1)0.963+50%(−4%, +139%)
    Beta(3, 27)0.959+47%(−5%, +130%)
    Beta(30, 270)0.905+25%(−10%, +74%)

    The flat prior changes almost nothing: 2 imaginary visitors are nothing next to 490 real ones. The strong prior is worth 300 visitors, more than either group, and it pulls both rates towards 10%: B's 16.4% is pulled down much more than A's 10.8%, so the lift shrinks to about +25% and the probability that B is better drops to 0.91. A strong shared prior is a sceptical one: it demands more evidence before declaring a difference.

  3. The exact probability. Derive P(\theta_B > \theta_A) as a one-dimensional integral, as used in the code, and explain why it is correct.
    Hint

    Condition on the value of \theta_A, and use the law of total probability of chapter 01, with an integral instead of a sum.

    Solution
    P(\theta_B > \theta_A \mid y) = \int_0^1 p_A(t)\,P(\theta_B > t \mid y)\,\mathrm{d}t = \int_0^1 p_A(t)\,\big(1 - F_B(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t.

    For each possible value t of \theta_A, weighted by its posterior density p_A(t), we ask how likely \theta_B is to exceed it. This is valid because the two posteriors are independent: the versions have separate data and independent priors, so knowing \theta_A tells us nothing more about \theta_B. scipy's postB.sf(t) is 1 - F_B(t).

  4. An eighth day. The café opens on an eighth day and gets 12 complaints. Update the posterior by adding one observation, and check that it matches refitting with all 8 days.
    Hint

    One more day: add its count to the shape and 1 to the rate. The button "day 8: 12 complaints" in the widget does it.

    Solution

    From Gamma(45, 9), one more day with 12 complaints gives Gamma(45 + 12, 9 + 1) = Gamma(57, 10), with mean 5.7 and a 95% interval of (4.32, 7.27). Refitting from the prior with all 8 days: Gamma(6 + 51, 2 + 8) = Gamma(57, 10), the same, as Part 6 of chapter 04 promised. A single bad day moves the estimate from 5.0 to 5.7, but not to 12: the other 9 days' worth of information (7 real, 2 imaginary) hold it back.

  5. Two sources of spread. Show that the negative binomial predictive has variance \frac{\alpha'}{\beta'} + \frac{\alpha'}{\beta'^2}, and interpret the two terms.
    Hint

    Use the law of total variance from the box in Part 5, with the gamma posterior's mean and variance from chapter 02.

    Solution

    Given \lambda, the count is Poisson, so \mathrm{Var}(\tilde y \mid \lambda) = \mathbb{E}[\tilde y \mid \lambda] = \lambda. With \lambda \sim Gamma(\alpha', \beta'):

    \mathrm{Var}(\tilde y) = \mathbb{E}[\lambda] + \mathrm{Var}(\lambda) = \frac{\alpha'}{\beta'} + \frac{\alpha'}{\beta'^2}.

    The first term is Poisson noise: the day-to-day randomness even with a known rate. The second is our uncertainty about the rate itself. For the café, \frac{45}{9} + \frac{45}{81} = 5 + 0.56 = 5.56, the notebook's value. With more days, \beta' grows and the second term vanishes.

  6. Fixing the Wald interval. The Wald interval of chapter 03 failed when y = 0. Compute the 95% equal-tailed credible interval under a Beta(½, ½) prior for y = 0, n = 20. Then compute its frequentist coverage at \theta = 0.05 with the coverage function of chapter 03.
    Hint

    The posterior is Beta(0.5, 20.5). For the coverage, write an interval function that returns the 0.025 and 0.975 quantiles of Beta(y + \frac{1}{2}, n - y + \frac{1}{2}).

    Solution

    The posterior Beta(0.5, 20.5) gives the interval (0.00002, 0.117): unlike the Wald interval it doesn't collapse to the single point 0, and it admits that a rate of a few percent is perfectly compatible with 0 successes in 20. Used as a frequentist procedure (this is the Jeffreys interval, chapter 07), it covers \theta = 0.05 98.4% of the time with n = 20, against 64% for Wald, and over all \theta its coverage averages 95.1% and never drops below 89%. A Bayesian interval can also be a very good frequentist one.

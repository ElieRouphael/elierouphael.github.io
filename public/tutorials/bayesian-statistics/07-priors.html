Choosing priors

A product manager says of a new feature: "I'd guess about 20% of users will adopt it, and I'd be very surprised, maybe one chance in ten, if it were more than 40%." That sentence is a prior. Below, it becomes a beta distribution, and the page reads the prior back to you in plain words, so you can check that it says what was meant. Choosing a prior is a modelling decision, and like any decision it can be made well or badly. This chapter is about making it well.

Part 1

Where priors come from

A prior summarises what is known about a parameter before the current data. In practice it comes from one or more of four sources:

  • Previous data: earlier studies, historical records, a pilot experiment.
  • Expert knowledge: people who know the domain, asked carefully [4].
  • Structure: physical limits (a rate is positive, a probability is between 0 and 1) and plausible orders of magnitude.
  • Convenience: a conjugate family, chosen because it is easy to update, with its numbers set to match the knowledge above.

Two facts make this less fraught than it first seems. With enough data, the likelihood dominates any reasonable prior: chapters 04 to 06 showed the prior's weight shrinking like 1/n. And the prior is written down explicitly, so it can be criticised, which is more than can be said for many hidden choices in a data analysis.

Part 2

From an expert to a prior

The product manager's sentence contains two statements: a mode (most likely value) of 0.2, and a 90th percentile of 0.4, since "one chance in ten of more than 40%" means 90% of the probability lies below 0.4. A beta prior has two parameters, so two statements pin it down.

The mode of Beta(\alpha, \beta) is \frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + \beta - 2} (exercise 5 of chapter 02). Setting it to 0.2 gives \beta = 4\alpha - 3, which leaves one unknown, \alpha. We choose it so that the 90th percentile is 0.4: a one-dimensional root-finding problem, which the demo at the top solves every time you move a slider.

def gap(a):
    return stats.beta.ppf(0.9, a, 4 * a - 3) - 0.4

a = optimize.brentq(gap, 1.01, 50)
b = 4 * a - 3
prior = stats.beta(a, b)
elicited prior: Beta(3.28, 10.12)
check: mode 0.200, P(θ > 0.4) = 0.100, mean 0.245, worth about 13.4 observations
central 95% of the prior: (0.064, 0.497)

Always feed the result back to the expert, in their own terms.

"So you are saying there is a 2.5% chance that adoption is below 6%, and that your opinion is worth about as much as watching 13 users?" If that sounds wrong to them, the prior is wrong, whatever the equations say. The demo writes exactly this kind of sentence for any pair of statements you give it.

For the curious: how the root is found (optional)

For each candidate \alpha, compute the 90th percentile of Beta(\alpha, 4\alpha - 3) and compare it with 0.4. Small \alpha gives a wide prior whose 90th percentile is above 0.4; large \alpha gives a narrow one whose 90th percentile is close to the mode 0.2, below 0.4. Somewhere in between the difference crosses zero, and bisection finds it: try the midpoint of an interval where the sign changes, keep the half where it still changes, and repeat. Each step halves the uncertainty, so 40 steps pin \alpha down to about one part in 10^{12}. scipy's brentq is a faster cousin of the same idea, and the demo uses plain bisection.

The method needs the two statements to be compatible: a 90th percentile below the mode is impossible for a single-peaked prior. The demo refuses such a pair rather than inventing an answer.

Part 3

Flat and improper priors

When little is known, a natural idea is a flat prior, p(\theta) \propto 1. For a probability this is Beta(1, 1), a proper distribution. For a parameter with an unbounded range, such as a normal mean \mu, a flat prior cannot have area 1: \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} 1\,\mathrm{d}\mu = \infty. Such a prior is improper.

Improper priors can still be used, as long as the posterior is proper: likelihood times prior must have a finite area. Standard examples:

  • Normal mean, p(\mu) \propto 1: the posterior is Normal(\bar y, \sigma^2/n), the s_0 \to \infty limit of chapter 06. Proper for any n \ge 1.
  • Normal variance, p(\sigma^2) \propto 1/\sigma^2: the limit of InvGamma(\alpha, \beta) as \alpha, \beta \to 0. Proper once n \ge 2.
  • Binomial, the Haldane prior p(\theta) \propto \theta^{-1}(1-\theta)^{-1}, "Beta(0, 0)": the posterior is Beta(y, n - y), proper only if 0 \lt y \lt n.

The last case shows the danger. With y = 0 successes in 20, the "posterior" is \theta^{-1}(1-\theta)^{19}, and its area is infinite near \theta = 0. Nothing in the formula warns you: you have to check. Below, the area is computed from a small \varepsilon up to 1. Shrink \varepsilon and watch it keep growing.

n = 20
for eps in [1e-2, 1e-4, 1e-8, 1e-16]:
    val = integrate.quad(lambda t: t**-1 * (1 - t) ** (n - 1), eps, 1, limit=200, points=[1e-3])[0]
    print(f"∫ from {eps:.0e} to 1 of θ^-1 (1-θ)^19 dθ = {val:6.2f}")
∫ from 1e-02 to 1 of θ^-1 (1-θ)^19 dθ =   1.24
∫ from 1e-04 to 1 of θ^-1 (1-θ)^19 dθ =   5.66
∫ from 1e-08 to 1 of θ^-1 (1-θ)^19 dθ =  14.87
∫ from 1e-16 to 1 of θ^-1 (1-θ)^19 dθ =  33.29

Each time \varepsilon shrinks by a factor of 10, the area grows by about \log 10 \approx 2.3: it grows like \log(1/\varepsilon), without limit. With a single success the factor \theta^{-1} is cancelled by the likelihood's \theta^1, and the area settles at \frac{1}{19}.

Part 4

Flat is not uninformative

"Flat" depends on how the parameter is written. If \theta is uniform on (0, 1), its log-odds \phi = \log\frac{\theta}{1-\theta} is not uniform. By the change-of-variables formula,

p_\phi(\phi) = p_\theta\big(\theta(\phi)\big)\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}\phi}\right| = \frac{e^\phi}{(1 + e^\phi)^2},

the standard logistic density, which bunches up around 0. Conversely, a flat prior on the log-odds is the Haldane prior on the probability scale, piled up at 0 and 1. "I know nothing about \theta" and "I know nothing about the log-odds of \theta" are different statements, and a flat prior can't express both. Compare the three priors below on both scales.

Part 5

Jeffreys priors

Harold Jeffreys proposed a rule that gives the same answer whichever way you write the parameter [1]:

\boxed{\;p(\theta) \propto \sqrt{I(\theta)}\;}, \qquad I(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\log f(y \mid \theta)\ \middle|\ \theta\right],

where I(\theta) is the Fisher information of one observation: the expected version of the curvature that gave standard errors in chapter 03. Where one observation is very informative, Jeffreys' prior puts more weight.

Why it doesn't care how you write the parameter. If \phi is a smooth one-to-one function of \theta, the chain rule gives I_\phi(\phi) = I_\theta(\theta)\,(\mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}\phi)^2, so \sqrt{I_\phi} = \sqrt{I_\theta}\,|\mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}\phi|: exactly the change-of-variables rule for densities. Computing Jeffreys' prior for \theta and transforming it gives Jeffreys' prior for \phi. Check it in the widget above: the Jeffreys prior on the log-odds scale is \sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)}, the square root of the Bernoulli's information in log-odds form.

Examples.

  • Bernoulli. -\partial^2_\theta \log f = \frac{y}{\theta^2} + \frac{1-y}{(1-\theta)^2}, with expectation \frac{1}{\theta} + \frac{1}{1-\theta} = \frac{1}{\theta(1-\theta)}. Jeffreys' prior is \theta^{-1/2}(1-\theta)^{-1/2}: Beta(½, ½), halfway between flat and Haldane.
  • Poisson. I(\lambda) = 1/\lambda, so p(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{-1/2} (improper).
  • Normal mean (\sigma known): I(\mu) = 1/\sigma^2 is constant, so the flat prior.
  • Normal scale (\mu known): p(\sigma) \propto 1/\sigma, equivalently p(\sigma^2) \propto 1/\sigma^2.

Jeffreys priors often have good frequentist properties too. The 95% equal-tailed credible interval under Beta(½, ½), the Jeffreys interval, is one of the best intervals for a proportion [5]. Here is its exact coverage, computed as in chapter 03, against the Wald interval that failed there and the flat-prior interval of exercise 5:

at θ = 0.05: Wald 0.639, Jeffreys 0.984

Jeffreys' rule is less attractive with several parameters, where it can give odd answers; "reference priors" and related formal rules refine it [3], [6].

Part 6

Prior predictive checks

A prior that looks harmless on the parameter can imply absurd data. The prior predictive distribution of chapter 04 makes this visible: draw a parameter from the prior, then data from the likelihood, and ask whether the result looks like anything the real world could produce.

Take the café complaints of chapter 05. A popular "vague" choice for a Poisson rate is Gamma(0.001, 0.001): mean 1, variance 1,000. Its prior predictive for one day's complaints shows what it really believes: nearly always zero complaints, and once in a while hundreds. A weakly informative prior such as Gamma(2, 0.5), with mean 4 and most of its mass below about 12, still lets the data speak, but rules out the absurd [7].

'vague' Gamma(0.001, 0.001)        P(0 complaints) = 0.99;  median, 90%, 99%, 99.9% quantiles: [  0   0   0 267]
weakly informative Gamma(2, 0.5)   P(0 complaints) = 0.11;  median, 90%, 99%, 99.9% quantiles: [ 3  9 15 21]

What is a weakly informative prior?

In plain words: a prior that knows roughly what scale the answer is on, and nothing more. It rules out the absurd and stays out of the data's way otherwise.

More precisely: a prior deliberately wider than your actual knowledge, but proper and concentrated on values that are physically or practically plausible, so that its prior predictive distribution produces believable data [7], [8].

Informative prior
Encodes real knowledge, such as earlier studies or an expert, and is meant to influence the answer.
Weakly informative prior
Encodes only the scale; the data dominate as soon as there are a few observations.
"Vague" prior
Tries to say nothing, and often says something absurd instead, as Gamma(0.001, 0.001) does.

With 10 observations the choice of prior visibly changes the answer: Beta(20, 20), worth 40 observations, drags the estimate towards 0.5. With 200 observations all five posteriors nearly coincide.

Part 8

The chapter on one page

  1. Priors come from previous data, experts, structure and convenience. Write them down and they can be criticised.
  2. Elicit with as many statements as parameters, solve for the hyperparameters, and feed the result back in plain words.
  3. Improper priors are fine only if the posterior is proper, and nothing warns you when it isn't. Check.
  4. Flat is not uninformative: it depends on how the parameter is written. Jeffreys' rule p(\theta) \propto \sqrt{I(\theta)} doesn't, and often gives good frequentist intervals.
  5. Check the prior predictive, prefer weakly informative priors to "vague" ones, and report a sensitivity analysis.

Part 9

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek. The notebook has a code cell ready for each of them.

  1. Your own inbox. Elicit a gamma prior for the number of emails you receive per hour from two statements of your own: a typical value, and an upper bound you would be surprised to exceed. Check its prior predictive distribution for a working day.
    Hint

    Use the same recipe as Part 2 with a gamma: its mode is (\alpha - 1)/\beta, so a mode m gives \beta = (\alpha - 1)/m; then solve for \alpha with the 90th percentile. For a day of 8 hours, the count is Poisson(8\lambda).

    Solution

    An example: "typically 6 an hour, and I'd be surprised (one chance in ten) by more than 12". Solving gives about Gamma(5.0, 0.665), with mean 7.5 per hour. Over an 8-hour day, its prior predictive (a negative binomial) has mean 60 emails, and 90% of days between about 22 and 112. If that range looks too wide or too narrow for your inbox, adjust the statements: that is the point of the check.

  2. An improper prior for a variance. Show that the posterior under the improper prior p(\sigma^2) \propto 1/\sigma^2, with a known mean \mu, is InvGamma\big(\frac{n}{2},\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_i (y_i - \mu)^2\big), and find the smallest n for which it is proper.
    Hint

    It is exercise 6 of chapter 06 with \alpha = \beta = 0. An inverse-gamma is proper when both its parameters are positive.

    Solution

    The likelihood is (\sigma^2)^{-n/2} e^{-S/(2\sigma^2)} with S = \sum_i (y_i - \mu)^2; times (\sigma^2)^{-1} it is (\sigma^2)^{-n/2 - 1} e^{-(S/2)/\sigma^2}, the kernel of InvGamma(n/2,\ S/2). It is proper when n/2 > 0 and S/2 > 0: a single observation is enough, as long as it differs from \mu. (With the mean unknown as well, one degree of freedom goes to estimating it, and you need n \ge 2, as stated in Part 3.)

  3. Jeffreys for a waiting time. Derive Jeffreys' prior for the rate \lambda of an exponential distribution. Is the resulting posterior proper after one observation?
    Hint

    \log f(y \mid \lambda) = \log\lambda - \lambda y. Differentiate twice.

    Solution

    -\partial^2_\lambda \log f = 1/\lambda^2, which doesn't depend on y, so I(\lambda) = 1/\lambda^2 and Jeffreys' prior is p(\lambda) \propto 1/\lambda, improper. After one wait y_1, the posterior is \lambda^{-1} \cdot \lambda e^{-\lambda y_1} = e^{-\lambda y_1}: an Exponential(y_1), that is Gamma(1, y_1), which is proper. In general it is Gamma(n, \sum y_i): the Gamma(0, 0) limit of chapter 06.

  4. Invariance, by numbers. Verify numerically that transforming Jeffreys' Beta(½, ½) prior to the log-odds scale gives \sqrt{I_\phi(\phi)} computed directly for the Bernoulli in log-odds form.
    Hint

    In log-odds form, \theta = 1/(1 + e^{-\phi}) and the Bernoulli's information is I_\phi(\phi) = \theta(1-\theta). The change of variables multiplies by \mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}\phi = \theta(1-\theta).

    Solution

    Transforming: \theta^{-1/2}(1-\theta)^{-1/2} \cdot \theta(1-\theta) = \sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)}. Directly: \sqrt{I_\phi(\phi)} = \sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)}. The same function of \phi, up to the constant 1/\pi that normalises Beta(½, ½). On a grid of \phi values, the ratio of the two is constant to machine precision. The middle button of the widget in Part 4 draws this curve.

  5. The flat-prior interval. Add the flat-prior Beta(y + 1, n - y + 1) credible interval to the coverage plot. How does it compare with Jeffreys?
    Hint

    Tick "flat prior" in the widget of Part 5, and move the read-off point to the edges.

    Solution

    With n = 20, both average about 95% over all \theta (95.2% flat, 95.1% Jeffreys), far better than Wald's 85%. They differ at the edges: at \theta = 0.05 the flat-prior interval covers 92.5% and Jeffreys 98.4%; at its worst, the flat-prior interval drops to 80%, Jeffreys only to 89%. The flat prior's imaginary success and failure pull the interval towards 0.5, which hurts exactly where \theta is near 0 or 1.

  6. Is B robustly better? For the A/B test of chapter 05, run a sensitivity analysis of P(\theta_B > \theta_A) over a range of shared beta priors. Is the conclusion robust?
    Hint

    Vary the prior's worth from 2 to 1,000 visitors, and its typical rate from 10% to 50%. The demo of chapter 05 does each case in one slider move.

    Solution
    Shared priorP(\theta_B > \theta_A)
    Beta(1, 1), worth 20.963
    10%, worth 30 (the notebook's)0.959
    10%, worth 1000.944
    10%, worth 3000.905
    50%, worth 1000.869
    10%, worth 1,0000.817

    The direction is robust: every reasonable prior says B is more likely better than not. The strength is not: "about 96%" holds for priors worth up to about a hundred visitors, but a prior worth more than the whole test pulls it down towards 80%. The honest report is "B is probably better, with probability 0.90 to 0.96 under reasonable priors", together with a note that a few hundred more visitors would settle it.

Understanding time series

A practical course in time-series analysis and forecasting that follows one real dataset from start to finish: six years of hourly air-quality measurements from Beijing. Every method, from moving averages to neural networks, answers the same question on the same data, is checked against simple baselines, and is finally tested on a year that stayed locked until the last chapter.

Elie Rouphael · Assumes Python and a little pandas; no prior time-series knowledge needed

One question, asked honestly

From chapter 04 on, every model answers the same question: given daily PM2.5 up to today, forecast each of the next seven days. They're all scored the same way, with a rolling-origin backtest that only ever lets a model see the past:

2010–2013 · history

Always available for fitting. Every model learns from it.

2014 · development

Where models are compared and tuned, one forecast origin per day, on a shared scoreboard.

2015 · locked

Opened once, in the capstone, after every decision was made. The honest final test.

Along the way the course keeps asking what makes a forecast good: beating naive and seasonal baselines, avoiding leakage, checking prediction intervals against what actually happened, and knowing where the limit is. Here, with perfect weather forecasts the errors would drop sharply, and chapter 07 measures by how much.

Chapters

Chapters 01–03 build the foundations: handling time in pandas, decomposing a series, and stationarity. Chapter 04 sets up the baselines and the backtest. Chapters 05–10 each add a model family: exponential smoothing, ARMA, SARIMAX with weather, Prophet, machine learning and deep learning. Chapter 11 opens the locked year.

  1. 01 Working with Time in pandas Timestamps from raw columns, index health, missing-data codes, gaps, resampling, trailing windows, lags, calendar features, long and wide tables. You find Winter's smog shows up in the extremes: severe hours are several times more common, while the typical day barely changes.
  2. 02 Anatomy of a Time Series Trend, seasonality and remainder, additive vs. multiplicative, classical decomposition from scratch, STL, the periodogram and component strength. You find Seasonal strength is 0.98 for CO₂ but only 0.47 for PM2.5, which is mostly weather-driven episodes.
  3. 03 Stationarity Random walks vs. mean reversion, half-lives, ADF and KPSS and their blind spots, test power by simulation, differencing vs. detrending. You find Daily PM2.5 forgets a shock with a half-life of about one day.
  4. 04 Baselines and Honest Evaluation Six baselines, MAE, RMSE and MASE (and why not MAPE), the rolling-origin backtest, leakage, and prediction intervals checked against outcomes. You find Naive wins for tomorrow and climatology from day 2: the bar every later model has to clear.
  5. 05 Exponential Smoothing SES from scratch, fitting objectives, Holt, damped trend, Holt-Winters, and ETS with AIC and interval coverage. You find Holt-Winters halves the best baseline's error on CO₂; on PM2.5 plain smoothing loses to the simple baselines.
  6. 06 Autocorrelation and ARMA Models ACF and PACF from scratch, AR, MA and ARMA fingerprints, AIC/BIC, Ljung–Box checks and mean-reverting forecasts. You find A seasonal profile plus AR(2) gives the best day-1 forecast so far, with 80% intervals that cover 78–80%.
  7. 07 ARIMA, SARIMA and SARIMAX Differencing inside the model, the airline model, residual diagnostics, auto_arima, cross-correlation, and weather as inputs: oracle vs. honest. You find Weather known today cuts the day-1 error to 40.9 µg/m³; perfect future weather would reach 34.2.
  8. 08 Prophet Fourier seasonality, changepoints, holiday effects, extra regressors, built-in cross-validation, logistic growth and a Prophet + AR hybrid. You find Spring Festival fireworks lift New Year's Day PM2.5 by about 55%.
  9. 09 Machine Learning for Forecasting Forecasting as a table, honest features and training cut-offs, direct vs. recursive, ridge vs. gradient boosting, three leakage traps measured. You find Ridge reaches 54.1 µg/m³ at day 2, the best so far; a leaky feature fakes 28.
  10. 10 Deep Learning Honest windows, training-only scaling, early stopping, MLP, SimpleRNN and LSTM, seed ensembles, and an hourly next-24-hours task. You find Identical networks differ by several µg/m³ between random seeds; on hourly data the LSTM beats ridge from 6 hours ahead.
  11. 11 Capstone: The Final Test Finalists fixed in advance, the locked 2015 year opened once, skill scores, a smog-alert forecast, and the same methods on temperature. You find Ridge is 36% better than climatology for tomorrow, and a day-ahead alert catches 65% of heavy-pollution days.

Running it yourself

Every chapter page shows the notebook with its outputs, so you can read the whole course here. To run and change the code, download the course (the notebooks, the shared tsutils.py helpers, the data and the requirements), then from its folder run:

pip install -r requirements.txt
jupyter lab                 # then open 01_time_in_pandas.ipynb

Run the chapters in order: from chapter 04 on, each one adds its scores to a shared scoreboard that later chapters compare against. Most chapters run in a minute or two on a laptop; the Prophet, deep-learning and capstone notebooks take a few minutes. Every chapter ends with exercises, with solutions folded away.

Data

Hourly PM2.5 and weather for Beijing, 2010–2015: Chen, Song (2016), PM2.5 Data of Five Chinese Cities, UCI Machine Learning Repository, doi:10.24432/C52K58, licensed CC BY 4.0. The Mauna Loa CO₂ record used for comparison is by Keeling & Whorf (Scripps Institution of Oceanography), public domain, and ships with statsmodels. For more depth on the statistical methods, Hyndman & Athanasopoulos's Forecasting: Principles and Practice is free online.

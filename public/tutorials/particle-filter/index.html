Part 1

The lost robot

Imagine you wake up somewhere in the hills, in thick fog. Your phone is dead. You have a paper map that shows how high the ground is everywhere, and a watch with an altimeter. The watch says 412 m. Where are you?

You can't say yet. Every point on the map that sits at 412 m is a candidate, and there are hundreds of them, strung along a contour line that winds over many hills. So you walk 100 m north and look again: 430 m. Now you only need the places that are at 412 m and have 430 m ground 100 m to their north. That is far fewer. Walk a bit more, read again, and soon only one place on the map fits everything you have seen.

One reading fits many places. A sequence of readings, taken while you move, fits only one.

That is the whole idea of this tutorial. Our robot is in exactly your situation. Finding out where you are on a map you already have is called localization, and it is one of the first problems every mobile robot has to solve.

The robot hasWhat it tells the robot
A mapThe height of the ground everywhere. The demo map is 240 m by 160 m; dark is low, light is high.
WheelsRoughly how far it just drove (about 4 m per step), but never exactly.
A compassWhich way it is facing, to within a couple of degrees.
An altimeterThe height of the ground under it, give or take about 1.5 m.
No GPSNothing tells it directly where it is.

Why this is harder than it sounds

Two things get in the way. First, as you just saw, one reading is ambiguous: it fits a whole contour line. Second, nothing is exact. The wheels slip, so "4 m" is really somewhere between about 3.5 and 4.5 m. The altimeter wobbles by a metre or two from one reading to the next. Engineers call these small random errors noise.

Because of the noise, the robot can't simply keep one best guess and update it. If that single guess is wrong, nothing would ever tell the robot to look somewhere else. It has to keep track of every place it might be, and how likely each one is. The rest of this tutorial shows a simple and surprisingly powerful way to do that: the particle filter.

This is not just a toy. Underwater vehicles find their position by comparing depth readings with maps of the seabed, because GPS signals do not reach under water. Many robot vacuums and warehouse robots run versions of the algorithm you are about to learn, with laser distance sensors in place of an altimeter.

Part 2

A cloud of guesses

How do you write down "I'm not sure where I am" in a way a computer can work with? The particle filter's answer is beautifully simple: keep lots of guesses.

Each guess is a complete answer, "the robot is here", a single point on the map. In the demo at the top there are 2,000 of them, the blue dots. Where the dots crowd together, the robot is probably there. Where there are no dots, it probably isn't. This crowd of guesses is the robot's belief: everything it currently thinks about its own position.

A cloud of guesses can describe any state of mind:

  • Spread evenly over the map: "I have no idea." That is how every run starts.
  • Split into a few clumps: "I'm in one of these three places, I just don't know which."
  • One tight clump: "I know where I am."

A single best guess could never say "one of these three places". A cloud can. The guesses are also called particles, which is where the name particle filter comes from.

What is a particle filter?

In plain words: a particle filter keeps track of something you cannot see directly by keeping many guesses about it, moving the guesses the way the thing moves, and favouring the guesses that agree with what you measure.

More precisely: a particle filter computes, step by step, the probability distribution of a hidden state given all the measurements so far. It represents that distribution by a set of random samples, the particles, which are moved with a model of the motion, weighted by how well they explain each new measurement, and resampled.

State
The numbers that describe what you want to know. Here, the robot's position (x, y), plus its heading when there is no compass. It is hidden: no sensor measures it directly.
Probability distribution
A description of how likely every possible value is. "70% in this valley, 30% in that one, almost certainly not anywhere else."
Sample
One value drawn at random from a distribution. Draw many, and they pile up where the distribution is high, like the bars of a histogram. The cloud of guesses is a set of samples.
Monte Carlo
Any method that answers a question by drawing many random samples instead of solving an equation.
Step by step
The filter never looks back at old readings. Everything it has learned so far is stored in the cloud, and each new reading only updates the cloud.
For the curious: why a cloud of samples works (optional)

Any question about the belief can be answered by counting guesses. "How likely is it that the robot is in this valley?" Count the fraction of guesses in the valley. "Where is the robot on average?" Average the guesses. In symbols, with N guesses \mathbf{s}_1, \dots, \mathbf{s}_N, any region R of the map and any quantity f:

P(\mathbf{s} \in R) \;\approx\; \frac{\#\{i : \mathbf{s}_i \in R\}}{N}, \qquad \mathbb{E}\big[f(\mathbf{s})\big] \;\approx\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(\mathbf{s}_i)

The law of large numbers says that both right-hand sides get closer and closer to the true values as N grows. How close? For a valley that holds a fraction p of the probability, the fraction of guesses inside it has a typical error (standard deviation) of

\sqrt{\frac{p\,(1-p)}{N}}

For p = 0.1 that is 0.10 \pm 0.03 with 100 guesses and 0.10 \pm 0.007 with 2,000. The error shrinks like 1/\sqrt{N}: four times more guesses halve it.

For localization there is a second, more practical limit: coverage. At the start, the guesses are spread over 38,400 m², so each one stands for 38{,}400 / N m²: 384 m² (a 20 m square) with 100 guesses, 19 m² (a 4.4 m square) with 2,000. If no guess starts within a few metres of the robot, the filter has nothing good to multiply. That is why 100 guesses often fail in Part 9, and why the filter without a compass, which must also cover every possible heading, needs many more.

Four steps, repeated forever

Every time the robot moves, the cloud goes through the same four steps:

  1. Predict. The robot drove 4 m north, so move every guess 4 m north, each with a little random error, because the wheels are never exact. Part 4
  2. Weigh. Read the altimeter. Give every guess a score: high if the map height at that guess matches the reading, low if it doesn't. Part 5
  3. Resample. Make a fresh set of guesses by copying the high-scoring ones and dropping the low-scoring ones. Part 6
  4. Jitter. Shake every guess by a tiny random amount so that copies spread out and keep exploring. Part 7

Good guesses multiply, bad guesses die out. After a few rounds, the survivors sit where the robot really is.

Why "filter"? In engineering, a filter is anything that takes noisy measurements and turns them into a cleaner estimate, as a coffee filter keeps the grounds out of your cup. This method is also called Monte Carlo localization, after the casino: it works by rolling dice, lots of them.

Part 3

Warm-up: a robot on a loop track

Before the full 2-D map, let's watch the four steps in the simplest possible world. A little robot runs on a circular track, 200 m around, that climbs over several hills. Its position is a single number: how far along the track it is. Below, the track is drawn unrolled, as if you had cut the loop and laid it flat. Leaving on the right brings you back on the left.

There are 60 guesses, drawn as blue sticks along the bottom. The taller a stick, the higher that guess's score. Press Next step and read the caption each time.

1 · weigh 2 · resample + jitter 3 · move (predict)

Three things are worth noticing.

  • The first reading leaves several clumps. The track crosses the same height in several places, so one reading can't tell them apart. The cloud honestly says "one of these".
  • Moving smears every clump a little. Each guess moved with its own small random error.
  • Wrong clumps die one by one. A wrong clump can match one reading by luck. After the robot moves, it predicts a different height from the one the robot actually reads, and its sticks shrink. Only the clump that agrees with every reading survives.

That last point is the heart of it. No single reading is decisive. It is the agreement with a whole sequence of readings, taken while moving, that singles out the right place.

Part 4 · Step 1

Moving makes you less sure

When the robot drives, it moves every guess the same way: "if I was here before driving 4 m north, I am 4 m further north now." The whole cloud shifts with the robot.

But the wheels slip. The robot asked for 4 m and might have done 3.7 m or 4.4 m. Its compass is off by a degree or two, so the direction is slightly uncertain too. The filter copies this uncertainty: each guess moves by its own slightly different, random amount. Most of these random errors are small and a few are larger, following the bell-shaped pattern you will meet properly in Part 5. Here the typical error is 10% of the distance, so about 0.4 m for a 4 m step.

Below, 200 guesses all start at exactly the same spot. Drive them and watch what happens.

The dashed line is where the robot would be if its wheels were perfect. Now untick compass, start over, and drive with a few turns: without a compass, small errors in the heading add up, and the cloud bends into a banana.

Moving alone only ever makes the cloud bigger. That is not a flaw. It is the honest truth: after driving with imprecise wheels, the robot really is less sure where it is. To become more sure, it needs to look at the world. That is the next step.

For the curious: the math of step 1 (optional)

Write a guess as a position (x, y). The robot commands a distance d and its compass reads a heading \theta. Each guess moves by

x \leftarrow x + (d + \varepsilon_d)\cos(\theta + \varepsilon_\theta), \qquad y \leftarrow y + (d + \varepsilon_d)\sin(\theta + \varepsilon_\theta)

where \varepsilon_d and \varepsilon_\theta are fresh random numbers for every guess, drawn from bell curves (normal distributions) centred on zero: \varepsilon_d \sim \mathcal{N}(0, (0.1\,d)^2) and \varepsilon_\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, (0.03 + 0.015\,d)^2). This rule is the motion model. In the textbooks it is written as a probability, p(\mathbf{s}_t \mid \mathbf{u}_t, \mathbf{s}_{t-1}): the chance of being at the new position \mathbf{s}_t, given the old position \mathbf{s}_{t-1} and the command \mathbf{u}_t. A particle filter never needs to write that probability down; it only needs to draw samples from it, which is exactly what the rule above does.

The map has y pointing down, as in an image. That is why a positive angle turns clockwise on screen.

Part 5 · Step 2

Sensing makes you more sure

After each move, the robot reads its altimeter, say 63.7 m. Now every guess gets a score. For each guess, look up the map height at that spot and compare:

  • The map says 63 m there? That guess explains the reading very well: high score.
  • The map says 20 m there? If the robot were really there, it would have read about 20 m, not 63.7 m. That guess is almost certainly wrong: score close to zero.

But we must be careful with guesses that are off by only a metre or two. The altimeter itself is off by about 1.5 m, so those guesses could easily be right. We need a soft score that falls off gently for small differences and steeply for big ones.

The bell curve

The tool for this is the bell curve, also called the normal or Gaussian distribution. It describes how random errors usually behave: small errors are common, big errors are rare, and very big errors almost never happen. Its width is set by one number, the standard deviation, written \sigma ("sigma"). You can read it as "the typical size of the error".

The score of a guess is the height of the bell curve at the difference between the reading and the map:

2.5 m10.61very plausible
5 m20.14possible, but suspicious
7.5 m30.011very unlikely
10 m40.0003practically impossible

The table uses \sigma = 2.5 m, the value our filter uses. In the widget below, click anywhere on the map to put the robot there. It takes a reading, and every point of the map is coloured by the score a guess standing there would get. Try different values of σ.

Blue = high score. The blue follows a contour line: every spot at roughly the same height as the reading gets a high score, wherever it is. This is the "one reading fits many places" problem, made visible.

Press read the altimeter again a few times: the reading wobbles by a metre or two, and the band shifts with it. That wobble is why σ can't be tiny. With σ = 0.5 m the band becomes a thread, and a guess standing right next to the robot could get a score of zero just because of one unlucky reading.

Why the filter assumes 2.5 m when the altimeter is better than that

The real altimeter error is 1.5 m, but the filter is told 2.5 m. This is a deliberate bit of humility. The map is stored in 1 m squares, the guesses are only a sample of all possible positions, and nothing in the model is perfect. A filter that trusts its sensor exactly tends to kill guesses that are close to the truth but not quite on it, and then it locks onto the wrong place. Giving it a little slack makes it far more reliable. You can test this yourself in Part 9.

For the curious: scores, Bayes' rule and normalizing (optional)

The bell-curve score of guess number i, at position (x_i, y_i), for the altimeter reading z is

w_i = \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{z - m(x_i, y_i)}{\sigma}\right)^{2}\right)

where m(x, y) is the map height. Up to a constant, this is the probability of reading z if the robot really were at guess i, written p(z \mid \mathbf{s}_i) and called the likelihood. It is the measurement model.

Why multiply a guess's importance by its likelihood? That is Bayes' rule, the basic law of learning from evidence:

\underbrace{p(\mathbf{s} \mid z)}_{\text{belief after the reading}} \;\propto\; \underbrace{p(z \mid \mathbf{s})}_{\text{likelihood}} \;\times\; \underbrace{p(\mathbf{s})}_{\text{belief before}}

Before the reading, the belief is the cloud of guesses. Multiplying each guess by its likelihood gives the belief after the reading. Finally, the scores are divided by their sum, \tilde w_i = w_i / \sum_j w_j, so that they add up to 1 and can be read as probabilities. The code also subtracts the smallest squared error before taking the exponential, so the best guess gets exactly 1 and the numbers never round down to zero.

Part 6 · Step 3

Survival of the fittest

After weighing, a few guesses have big scores and most have tiny ones. Carrying around 2,000 guesses of which 1,900 are almost certainly wrong wastes effort, and those few good ones are spread thin. Resampling fixes this. It builds a brand-new set of 2,000 guesses by picking from the old set, where each guess's chance of being picked is its score. Strong guesses are picked several times and become several copies. Weak guesses are usually not picked at all and disappear.

A neat way to do the picking is the comb. Lay the scores end to end, like segments on a ruler, each segment as long as that guess's share of the total score. Then drop a comb with one evenly spaced tooth per new guess on top of the ruler, shifted by a random amount. Each tooth picks the guess it lands on. A guess holding 25% of the total score gets about 25% of the teeth.

After resampling, every guess counts the same again. The information hasn't vanished, though: it now lives in where the guesses crowd. A place that scored well is now covered by many copies.

Notice what resampling cannot do: it never creates a new position. It only copies existing guesses. If no guess was near the robot to begin with, resampling cannot put one there. Keep this in mind for the next part.

For the curious: why the comb and not a lottery (optional)

The simplest way to resample is a lottery: draw 2,000 times at random, each time picking guess i with probability \tilde w_i. That works, but it is needlessly noisy: by bad luck a strong guess can get no copies at all. The comb, known as systematic resampling, uses a single random number u between 0 and 1/N and places teeth at u, u + 1/N, u + 2/N, \dots. A guess with weight \tilde w_i then gets either \lfloor N \tilde w_i \rfloor or \lceil N \tilde w_i \rceil copies, never wildly more or fewer. It is also fast: one pass through the list.

For the curious: how many guesses really count? (optional)

A cloud of 2,000 guesses in which one guess holds almost all the weight is really worth about one guess. A standard way to put a number on this is the effective sample size:

N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde w_i^{\,2}}

If all weights are equal, \tilde w_i = 1/N, then N_{\text{eff}} = N. If one guess has all the weight, N_{\text{eff}} = 1. In between, read it as "this weighted cloud is worth about N_{\text{eff}} equally good guesses". The comb widget above shows it for its eight guesses.

Our filter resamples after every reading, which keeps the code short. But every round of copying loses a little variety, even when the reading taught the filter nothing new. You can see this in exercise 4. A common refinement is to resample only when N_{\text{eff}} drops below, say, N/2. Until then, each guess keeps its weight and the next score is multiplied into it: w_i \leftarrow w_i \cdot p(z \mid \mathbf{s}_i).

Part 7 · Step 4

Keeping an open mind

Jitter: spreading the copies

After resampling, copies sit exactly on top of each other. Repeat that for many steps and the 2,000 guesses could end up as just a handful of distinct points stacked on top of each other, a problem called sample impoverishment. The fix is the fourth step: shake every guess by a small random amount, 0.5 m in the demo. Each stack of copies becomes a small local cloud that can explore the neighbourhood and find a slightly better spot.

The kidnapped robot

Now a harder case. Suppose the robot knows exactly where it is, and someone picks it up and puts it down somewhere else. All the guesses are still at the old place. None are near the new one, and as we just saw, resampling cannot create guesses where there are none. The filter is stuck. It keeps believing in the old place, because the old guesses, bad as they are, are the best it has.

Roboticists call this the kidnapped robot problem. It sounds contrived, but it is a good test of something real: can the filter recover after it has become confident about the wrong answer?

A simple fix is to keep a few explorers. At every step, a small fraction of the guesses (1% in the demo) is thrown to completely random places on the map. While the robot is where the filter thinks it is, the explorers score badly and die. But if the robot is somewhere unexpected, sooner or later an explorer lands near it, matches the readings better than anything else, and multiplies.

Each button first lets the filter find the robot, then kidnaps it and starts auto-drive. Watch the chart: the orange error line jumps up. Without explorers, the blue spread stays small, so the filter is confident and wrong, and it usually stays that way. With explorers, the cloud reappears at the new place after a while. In 30 simulated kidnappings, the filter without explorers recovered only twice in the next 60 steps; with explorers it recovered 27 times, typically within about 23 steps.

For the curious: what jitter and explorers do to the math (optional)

Jitter. Replacing each copy by a random point drawn from a small bell curve around it is the same as smoothing the cloud: every guess becomes a little blur of width 0.5 m instead of a sharp point. Gordon, Salmond and Smith called this roughening in the first particle-filter paper [GSS93], and smarter versions are known as regularized particle filters. The price is a little extra uncertainty that the real robot does not have, so the shake must stay small compared with the accuracy you want.

Explorers. Throwing a fraction \varepsilon of the guesses to random places before predicting turns the belief into a mix:

\operatorname{bel}(\mathbf{s}) \;\leftarrow\; (1-\varepsilon)\,\operatorname{bel}(\mathbf{s}) \;+\; \varepsilon\,\frac{1}{\text{area of the map}}

There is a neat way to read this: it is a motion model in which, at every step, the robot has a 1% chance of being picked up and dropped anywhere. In other words, explorers do not break the method; they model the kidnapping. The cost is that the model is a little wrong when nobody kidnaps the robot, and 1% of the guesses are wasted at every step. A cleverer scheme, augmented Monte Carlo localization [TBF05], adds explorers only when the scores of the guesses suddenly drop, which is the sign that the robot is no longer where the filter thinks it is.

Part 8

The whole algorithm

Here is everything together. At the start, scatter the guesses evenly over the map. Then, every time the robot moves:

  1. Predict: move every guess the way the robot moved, each with its own random error.
  2. Weigh: score every guess with the bell curve, by how well its map height matches the altimeter.
  3. Resample: pick a new set of guesses with the comb, copying in proportion to the scores.
  4. Jitter: shake every guess a little.

The robot's best single guess is the average position of all the guesses, the white ring in the demo. The spread, the typical distance of a guess from that average, says how sure the robot is. Be careful with the average while the cloud is split into several clumps: it can land in the empty space between them. It only becomes trustworthy once the cloud is a single clump.

For the curious: the estimate, the spread, and when the average lies (optional)

After resampling, all guesses count the same, so the estimate and the spread are

\hat{\mathbf{s}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{s}_i, \qquad r = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \big\lVert \mathbf{s}_i - \hat{\mathbf{s}} \big\rVert^2}

The spread r is the root-mean-square distance of a guess from the estimate; in Python it is np.sqrt(px.var() + py.var()). Before resampling, the same formulas use the weights: \hat{\mathbf{s}} = \sum_i \tilde w_i\, \mathbf{s}_i, as in the hand calculation below.

Now take a cloud with half its guesses at 40 m and half at 150 m. The average is 95 m, where there is no guess at all, and the spread is 55 m. The average answers "where is the robot on average?", which is not the same question as "where is the robot most likely to be?". The second question asks for the mode, the densest place of the cloud. Two common ways to find it are to pick the guess with the most neighbours within a few metres, or to split the cloud into clumps and average only the heaviest one. The demo keeps the plain average, and its spread warns you: a large spread means "don't trust the ring yet".

  3. Best guess. The weighted average is

    The plain average of the four positions would be 80.5 m, dragged away by D, which the reading has just ruled out.
  4. How many guesses count? The effective sample size of Part 6 is
    \begin{aligned} N_{\text{eff}} &= \frac{1}{0.554^2 + 0.364^2 + 0.081^2} \\ &= \frac{1}{0.446} \approx 2.2 \end{aligned}
    so the four weighted guesses are worth about two equally good ones.
  5. Resample. Lay the shares on a ruler: A covers 0 to 0.554, B 0.554 to 0.918, C 0.918 to 1. The comb has four teeth, 1/4 apart, starting at a random number between 0 and 1/4, say 0.10. The teeth fall at 0.10, 0.35, 0.60 and 0.85: two on A, two on B. The new set is A, A, B, B, each with weight 1/4, and its average is again 63.0 m.
  6. Look at C. C held 8% of the weight, so it "deserved" 4 \times 0.081 = 0.33 copies. The comb can give it only 0 or 1, and this time it gave 0. With 2,000 guesses instead of four, such rounding hardly matters.
  7. Jitter. Shake each copy by a little: the two copies of A might become 61.7 m and 62.4 m, and they are two different guesses again.

The code

The whole filter is four short functions in Python, using the NumPy library, which works on all 2,000 guesses at once. This is an excerpt from the complete program, which also makes the map, drives the robot and draws the plots.

N = 2000                 # number of guesses
ASSUMED_NOISE = 2.5      # the filter assumes a slightly worse altimeter than the real one
JITTER = 0.5             # metres of shake after resampling


def predict(px, py, compass, distance):
    """1. Move every guess the way the robot thinks it moved, plus random errors."""
    heading = compass + rng.normal(0, COMPASS_NOISE + DRIFT * distance, N)
    travelled = distance + rng.normal(0, STEP_NOISE * distance, N)
    px = np.clip(px + travelled * np.cos(heading), 0, WIDTH - 1)
    py = np.clip(py + travelled * np.sin(heading), 0, HEIGHT - 1)
    return px, py


def weigh(px, py, reading):
    """2. Bell-curve score: how well does each guess explain the reading?"""
    surprise = ((reading - height_at(px, py)) / ASSUMED_NOISE) ** 2
    weights = np.exp(-0.5 * (surprise - surprise.min()))   # best guess gets weight 1
    return weights / weights.sum()                          # weights now add up to 1


def resample(px, py, weights):
    """3. Copy guesses in proportion to their weight; unlikely ones disappear."""
    comb = (rng.random() + np.arange(N)) / N               # N evenly spaced teeth
    chosen = np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(weights), comb)
    chosen = np.minimum(chosen, N - 1)                      # guard against rounding
    return px[chosen], py[chosen]


def jitter(px, py):
    """4. A small shake, so that copies do not sit exactly on top of each other."""
    return px + rng.normal(0, JITTER, N), py + rng.normal(0, JITTER, N)

And the loop that runs it, once per step of the drive:

for step in range(1, STEPS + 1):
    distance, turn = 4.0, 0.2 * np.cos(step / 4)           # weave left and right

    rx, ry, rh = robot_step(rx, ry, rh, distance, turn)    # the real robot moves
    compass = read_compass(rh)                              # and reads its sensors
    reading = read_altimeter(rx, ry)

    px, py = predict(px, py, compass, distance)             # 1. predict
    weights = weigh(px, py, reading)                        # 2. weigh
    px, py = resample(px, py, weights)                      # 3. resample
    px, py = jitter(px, py)                                 # 4. jitter

For the curious: the Bayes filter behind it all (optional)

Call the robot's position at step t \mathbf{s}_t, its commands \mathbf{u}_{1:t} and its readings z_{1:t}. What we want is the belief, the probability distribution of the position given everything so far, \operatorname{bel}(\mathbf{s}_t) = p(\mathbf{s}_t \mid z_{1:t}, \mathbf{u}_{1:t}). It obeys a two-line recursion, the Bayes filter:

\overline{\operatorname{bel}}(\mathbf{s}_t) = \int p(\mathbf{s}_t \mid \mathbf{u}_t, \mathbf{s}_{t-1})\, \operatorname{bel}(\mathbf{s}_{t-1})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}_{t-1} \qquad \text{(predict)}
\operatorname{bel}(\mathbf{s}_t) = \eta\, p(z_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)\, \overline{\operatorname{bel}}(\mathbf{s}_t) \qquad \text{(update)}

where \eta makes everything add up to 1. For a hilly map these integrals have no formula. The particle filter replaces the belief by N samples, \operatorname{bel}(\mathbf{s}_t) \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \delta(\mathbf{s}_t - \mathbf{s}_t^{(i)}). Then the predict line becomes "move each sample with the motion model", the update line becomes "weigh each sample by the likelihood", and resampling turns the weighted samples back into equally weighted ones. With more and more samples, the approximation becomes exact.

For the curious: why the score is exactly the right weight (optional)

After predict, the guesses are samples from the belief before the reading, \overline{\operatorname{bel}}(\mathbf{s}). What we want are samples from the belief after the reading, \operatorname{bel}(\mathbf{s}) = \eta\, p(z \mid \mathbf{s})\, \overline{\operatorname{bel}}(\mathbf{s}), and we cannot draw from it directly. The trick that fixes this is called importance sampling.

Suppose you have samples from one distribution q (the proposal) but want averages under another distribution \pi (the target). Give each sample the weight w_i = \pi(\mathbf{s}_i) / q(\mathbf{s}_i), normalize, and average with the weights:

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\big[f(\mathbf{s})\big] = \int f(\mathbf{s})\, \frac{\pi(\mathbf{s})}{q(\mathbf{s})}\, q(\mathbf{s})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{s} \;\approx\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde w_i\, f(\mathbf{s}_i)

Samples that q produces too often get small weights, and samples it produces too rarely get large ones. In our filter the proposal is the predicted belief and the target is the belief after the reading, so

w_i = \frac{\eta\, p(z \mid \mathbf{s}_i)\, \overline{\operatorname{bel}}(\mathbf{s}_i)}{\overline{\operatorname{bel}}(\mathbf{s}_i)} = \eta\, p(z \mid \mathbf{s}_i)

The predicted belief cancels, and what is left is the likelihood: the bell-curve score of Part 5, nothing more. The unknown constant \eta disappears when the weights are normalized, which is also why the score can leave out the bell curve's usual factor 1/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}). This is why the filter is also called sequential importance resampling. If the guesses were moved some other way, for example towards places that match the reading, the weights would have to include the ratio \overline{\operatorname{bel}} / q.

Part 9

When it goes wrong

The best way to understand a method is to break it. Each button below changes one setting in the demo at the top, scatters the guesses and starts auto-drive. Use scatter guesses to rerun the same experiment, and keep an eye on the chart.

Too few guesses

With 100 guesses spread over 38,400 m², each guess has to cover almost 400 m². Often no guess starts anywhere near the robot. The filter then picks the best of a bad bunch and becomes confident about the wrong place. In the simulated drives, 100 guesses found the robot only 14 times out of 40, and in most of the failures the cloud was a tight clump in the wrong place.

Flat ground

On almost flat ground the heights differ by only 4 m across the whole map, just a few times the altimeter's own error. Every reading fits almost everywhere, and the cloud never shrinks. However clever the algorithm, it can only find the robot if what the sensor measures actually changes from place to place. The gentle terrain sits in between: big smooth hills give fewer clues per step, so finding the robot takes longer.

An overconfident filter

Tell the filter its altimeter is perfect to within 0.5 m. Now a guess that is 2 m off scores almost zero, even though the real altimeter is often 2 m off. Good guesses get killed by unlucky readings. The cloud collapses fast, often onto the wrong place: in the table it locks on quickest of all, but onto the right place only 16 times out of 40. At the other extreme, σ = 8 m never fails but needs almost twice as many steps as the default. Being a little humble about your sensors (Part 5) is the sweet spot.

No compass

Without a compass, each guess must also guess which way the robot is facing: three unknowns instead of two. A guess in the right place but facing the wrong way drives off in the wrong direction and dies. There are far more possibilities to cover, so the filter needs more guesses (5,000 here) and more driving. In 30 simulated drives it found the robot 25 times within 60 steps, typically after about 30 steps, and even then the cloud now and then wanders off for a few steps before settling again.

Hide and seek

Finally, a game. Hide the real robot, drive it yourself, and decide when you trust the guesses. Then reveal the robot and see whether the filter was right. When the cloud is a single tight clump, it almost always is.

Part 10

Why not just one bell curve?

The particle filter is not the only way to track a hidden state. The most famous one is the Kalman filter, from 1960 [Kal60]. Instead of a cloud of guesses, it keeps a single bell curve: one best guess and one uncertainty around it, which in two dimensions is an ellipse. Its predict and update steps are short formulas instead of rolling dice. When the motion and the sensor behave like straight lines and all the noise follows bell curves, it gives the exact answer, and it costs a handful of multiplications instead of 2,000 guesses. That is why it runs in phones, aircraft and cars. My TrustDrive course builds one step by step for a car keeping its lane.

So why not use it here? Because a bell curve has only one peak. Early in a run, the honest belief is "one of these three places". Below, the blue curve is such a belief, and the dashed curve is the single bell curve with the same average and the same spread:

There is a second problem. The Kalman filter's formulas need a sensor whose reading changes in a straight line with the position. Our altimeter reads the height of hills. The usual workaround, the extended Kalman filter, replaces the map by its slope at the current best guess. That works once the guess is close to the robot, but a hilly map changes slope every few metres. A bell curve that starts in the wrong valley follows the slope of that valley and stays there.

Once the particle filter has locked on, though, its cloud is a single tight clump, which looks very much like a bell curve. From then on a Kalman filter would track the robot almost as well, for a tiny fraction of the cost. A sensible design for a real robot is therefore to use a particle filter to find itself and a Kalman filter to keep track, switching back if the readings stop making sense.

For the curious: the Kalman filter in one dimension (optional)

Write the belief as a bell curve with mean \mu and variance P (the square of its standard deviation). Driving a distance d with a random error of variance Q moves the bell and widens it:

\mu \leftarrow \mu + d, \qquad P \leftarrow P + Q \qquad \text{(predict)}

Now suppose a sensor measured the position itself, z = s + v, with a noise v of variance R. Bayes' rule multiplies the bell curve of the belief by the bell curve of the sensor, and the product of two bell curves is again a bell curve. Its mean and variance are

K = \frac{P}{P + R}, \qquad \mu \leftarrow \mu + K\,(z - \mu), \qquad P \leftarrow (1 - K)\,P \qquad \text{(update)}

The Kalman gain K, between 0 and 1, decides how far to move towards the reading: close to 1 when the belief is vague and the sensor sharp, close to 0 the other way round. These are the same two steps as the particle filter, the Bayes filter of Part 8, done with formulas instead of samples. They are exact only because everything stays a bell curve. Our altimeter does not measure s but the map height m(s); the extended Kalman filter uses the straight-line approximation m(s) \approx m(\mu) + m'(\mu)\,(s - \mu), which is only good near \mu. Dan Simon's book [Sim06] covers all of this, from the Kalman filter to the particle filter.

Part 11

Exercises

The first four use only the demo. The others change a line or two of particle_filter.py; run each change with a few different seeds before drawing a conclusion. Every exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek. The numbers in the solutions come from running the exercises on many maps or seeds, so yours will differ a little.

  1. Count the steps. With the default settings, press new map, then auto-drive, five times. How many steps does it take until the spread drops below 5 m? Is it always about the same?
    Hint

    Watch the spread of the guesses readout, or move the mouse over the chart to read the step at which the blue line crosses 5 m.

    Solution

    Usually between 10 and 20 steps. On 40 simulated maps with the same auto-drive, the median was 14 steps, and 8 runs out of 10 took between 10 and 21. It is not always the same, because it depends on the ground the robot happens to drive over. Steep, varied ground gives a lot of information at every step. A long smooth slope or a plateau gives little, and the cloud has to wait for better ground.

  2. Watch the clumps. Drive by hand, one step at a time, from a fresh scatter. How many separate clumps are there after 3 steps? After 6? Can you explain from the map why the wrong ones died?
    Hint

    Look at the ground just in front of each clump, in the direction the robot is driving, and compare it with the ground in front of the robot.

    Solution

    The counts depend on the map and the drive, but the pattern is always the same: several clumps along pieces of the contour line after 3 steps, fewer and tighter ones after 6. Every clump sits on ground of the right height, but a wrong clump only survives a step if the ground in front of it rises or falls the way the ground in front of the robot does. Where the ground slopes the other way, or stays flat, the clump predicts the wrong height after the next move and dies. Only the right clump matches every reading.

  3. Read the chart. In a normal run, the blue spread line and the orange error line come down together. In the "kidnap without explorers" experiment they don't. Explain in one sentence why a filter's spread is not always a trustworthy measure of its error.
    Hint

    The spread is a distance between what and what? And the error?

    Solution

    The spread measures how much the guesses agree with each other, not whether they agree with the truth, so a filter that has locked onto the wrong place is confident and wrong: small spread, large error. No filter can measure its own error directly; if it could, it would simply correct it. What it can do is check whether the readings keep matching what it expects, which is the idea behind the consistency test in chapter 5 of my TrustDrive course.

  4. Turning in place. Turn left ten times without moving forward. The robot reads its altimeter each time, at the same spot. What happens to the cloud? Is that what you would expect?
    Hint

    When the robot only turns, how far do the guesses move? And what can ten readings taken at the same spot tell you that one reading cannot?

    Solution

    The cloud gathers onto the contour line at the robot's height and gets a little thinner across it, because ten independent readings average out the altimeter's noise. In simulated runs, about 70% of the guesses sat within 2.5 m of the robot's height after the first reading and about 90% after ten. But the cloud never shrinks along the contour line: the spread stayed between 75 and 88 m. That is exactly what you should expect. Every reading asks the same question, "how high is the ground here?", and asking it again only sharpens the answer to that question. It never says where along the contour line you are.

    You may also see some stretches of the contour line slowly empty out, although no reading tells them apart. That is resampling at every step losing variety by pure chance, the effect that the effective-sample-size refinement of Part 6 avoids.

  5. No jitter. Set JITTER = 0. Print len(np.unique(px)) at each step: the number of distinct guesses. What happens to it, and does the filter still find the robot?
    Hint

    Print it right after the resample line. Then read predict again: is the jitter the only thing that moves copies apart?

    Solution

    The number of distinct guesses drops sharply right after the most informative readings, to a few hundred and sometimes below 150, then climbs back up. Yet the filter still works: with JITTER = 0 it found the robot with 9 of the seeds 0 to 9, against 10 of 10 with jitter. The reason is predict. Every guess already moves with its own random error, so two copies of the same guess are pulled apart at the very next step. Jitter is a safety net for the moments when predict adds little or no randomness: a robot standing still or turning in place (exercise 4), or a very precise motion model.

  6. Humble or overconfident? Try ASSUMED_NOISE = 0.5, 1.5, 2.5 and 10 with seeds 0 to 9. For each value, count the runs that end with an error under 5 m, and note how many steps the successful runs took. What is the trade-off?
    Hint

    Forty runs by hand is tedious. Put everything from rng = ... to the end of the drive inside a function run(seed, assumed_noise) that returns the list of errors, drop the plots, and loop over both.

    Solution
    ASSUMED_NOISEEnds within 5 mTypical steps to lock on
    0.5 m5 of 106
    1.5 m8 of 109
    2.5 m10 of 1012.5
    10 m10 of 1027

    "Typical" is the median over the successful runs. The more the filter trusts its altimeter, the faster it makes up its mind, and the more often it makes it up wrongly. A humble filter is reliable but slow. The sweet spot is a little above the true noise of 1.5 m, the same pattern as the table in Part 9.

  7. Flatten the world. After terrain = make_terrain(...), add terrain = 50 + 0.04 * (terrain - 50). What happens to the spread, and why?
    Hint

    How much do the heights now differ from one place to another, compared with the altimeter's own error of 1.5 m?

    Solution

    The spread stays large. With seed 1 it is still about 70 m after 30 steps and 50 m after 40, and over seeds 0 to 9 not a single run ends within 5 m of the robot. The heights now span only 4 m, not much more than the altimeter's noise, so every reading fits most of the map. Nothing is wrong with the filter: the readings simply carry almost no information about the position. It is the "almost flat" experiment of Part 9.

  8. Kidnap the robot in Python. At step 25, move the robot: rx, ry = 200.0, 120.0. Then add explorers: at the start of every step, before predict, replace a random 1% of the guesses with new random positions. Does the filter recover? Why is it better to add them before weighing than after?
    Hint

    Set STEPS = 80 to give it time. For the last question, think about what 20 random guesses scattered over the whole map do to the average and the spread if they are still in the cloud when you compute them.

    Solution

    Inside the loop, right after the robot moves:

    if step == 25:
    rx, ry = 200.0, 120.0                    # kidnap
explorers = rng.random(N) < 0.01             # pick about 1% of the guesses
px[explorers] = rng.uniform(0, WIDTH, explorers.sum())
py[explorers] = rng.uniform(0, HEIGHT, explorers.sum())

    Without explorers, the filter recovered with only 3 of the seeds 0 to 9, and only after 36 steps or more; in the other runs it stayed confident and wrong. With explorers it recovered in 9 of 10 runs, typically within about 15 steps.

    Added before weighing, the explorers are judged by the very next reading, and the ones that do not fit are dropped straight away by resampling. Added at the end of the step instead, about 20 random points spread over the whole map are still in the cloud when you compute the estimate and the spread. Once the filter had locked on, the median spread was then 13.8 m instead of 5.9 m, so the spread said "unsure" when the filter was in fact sure. Even added before weighing, a few explorers land on ground of the right height and survive for a step or two, which is why the spread is larger than the 2.2 m without explorers. That is the price of staying open-minded.

  9. Take the compass away (harder). Give every guess its own heading ph, scattered at random at the start. In predict, turn each guess with ph = ph + turn + noise and drive it along ph instead of the compass. How many guesses and steps do you need now?
    Hint

    Start with ph = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, N). Resample ph with the same chosen indices as px and py, or the headings get shuffled between guesses, and give it a little jitter too (0.03 rad).

    Solution 
    def predict(px, py, ph, turn, distance):
    """1. Every guess turns and drives along its own heading."""
    ph = ph + turn + rng.normal(0, TURN_NOISE * abs(turn) + DRIFT * distance, N)
    travelled = distance + rng.normal(0, STEP_NOISE * distance, N)
    px = np.clip(px + travelled * np.cos(ph), 0, WIDTH - 1)
    py = np.clip(py + travelled * np.sin(ph), 0, HEIGHT - 1)
    return px, py, ph

    With 60 steps and seeds 0 to 9: 2,000 guesses found the robot in 8 runs out of 10, typically after about 43 steps. 5,000 guesses found it every time, but still needed about as long. 10,000 guesses found it every time, typically after about 21 steps. Compare this with about 12 steps for 2,000 guesses with a compass: one more unknown makes the problem much harder, because a guess now has to be right about its position and its heading at the same time.

Part 12

Going further

You now know the core of one of the most widely used algorithms in robotics. Real systems add refinements: better sensors (laser scanners and cameras instead of one altimeter), smarter ways to decide how many guesses to keep, and maps that the robot builds for itself while it explores, which is called SLAM. The sources below are good next steps, roughly from gentlest to most technical.

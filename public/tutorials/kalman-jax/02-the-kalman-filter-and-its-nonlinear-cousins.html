The Kalman filter and its nonlinear cousins

A sensor reports a position every tenth of a second, half a metre wrong on average. From that alone the Kalman filter recovers the position three times more accurately than the sensor and a velocity nobody measured, and it says how sure it is. This chapter builds the filter from its two equations, then its two nonlinear relatives, the extended and the unscented filter, in the form the companion repository implements them in JAX. Along the way it finds a real bug: the textbook unscented filter, run in JAX's default 32-bit arithmetic, destroys itself after 33 seconds of data. Below, all three filters run in your browser on the repository's own data.

Part 1

The model

State and dynamics. An object moves along a line at a velocity that drifts randomly. Its state is position and velocity, x_t = (p_t, v_t)^\top, sampled every \Delta t = 0.1 s. Between samples the velocity is constant up to a random acceleration, so

x_{t+1} = F x_t + w_t, \qquad F = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \Delta t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad w_t \sim \mathcal N(0, Q).

Where Q comes from. The repository's process noise is not two independent noises on position and velocity. It is a white acceleration of intensity q = \sigma_a^2 integrated over one sample: a random acceleration acting for a time \tau before the end of the interval moves the velocity by \tau times itself and the position by \tau^2/2 times itself, so

Q = q \int_0^{\Delta t} \begin{pmatrix} \tau^2 & \tau \\ \tau & 1 \end{pmatrix} d\tau = q \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\Delta t^3}{3} & \dfrac{\Delta t^2}{2} \\[0.6em] \dfrac{\Delta t^2}{2} & \Delta t \end{pmatrix}.

With \sigma_a = 0.1 and \Delta t = 0.1 this is Q = 10^{-2} \begin{pmatrix} 3.33 \times 10^{-4} & 5 \times 10^{-3} \\ 5 \times 10^{-3} & 0.1 \end{pmatrix}: the velocity wanders by \sqrt{10^{-3}} \approx 0.032 per step, the position by a mere 1.8 \times 10^{-3}, and the two are strongly correlated (correlation \sqrt 3 / 2 \approx 0.87), because the same acceleration causes both. This is the standard discretisation of the white-noise-acceleration model [4].

Measurement. Only the position is observed, with noise of standard deviation \sigma_z = 0.5:

z_t = H x_t + \nu_t, \qquad H = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \nu_t \sim \mathcal N(0, R), \quad R = \sigma_z^2 = 0.25.

The repository's generator starts the true state at (0, 1) and the filter at (0, 0) with P_0 = I: the filter does not know the object is moving, and has to find out. Over 1000 steps the position drifts about 100 units while the velocity wanders by about one; the sensor error, 0.5, is five times the total motion per step.

Part 2

The Kalman filter

The filter keeps a Gaussian belief about the state, mean \hat x and covariance P, and alternates two steps [1].

Predict. Push the belief through the dynamics. The mean follows F, the covariance grows by the process noise:

\hat x_{t|t-1} = F \hat x_{t-1}, \qquad P_{t|t-1} = F P_{t-1} F^\top + Q.

Update. Compare the measurement with what the belief predicted for it. The difference is the innovation e_t; its covariance S_t adds the belief's uncertainty about the measured quantity to the sensor's own noise; the gain weighs the two:

e_t = z_t - H \hat x_{t|t-1}, \qquad S_t = H P_{t|t-1} H^\top + R, \qquad K_t = P_{t|t-1} H^\top S_t^{-1}, \qquad \hat x_t = \hat x_{t|t-1} + K_t e_t .

The gain is a ratio of uncertainties: P H^\top is how much each state component co-varies with the measured quantity, S is how uncertain the measured quantity is in total. If the sensor is much better than the prediction, K \to H^+ and the measurement is believed outright; if the prediction is much better, K \to 0 and the measurement is ignored. Notice that the velocity gets corrected too, through the off-diagonal entry of P: the filter knows that a position surprise is partly a velocity surprise.

Covariance update, in Joseph form. The textbook formula P_t = (I - K_t H) P_{t|t-1} is correct only for the exact optimal gain and, computed in floating point, can lose symmetry and positive-definiteness. The repository uses the form due to Bucy and Joseph, valid for any gain and manifestly symmetric and non-negative [2]:

P_t = (I - K_t H)\, P_{t|t-1}\, (I - K_t H)^\top + K_t R K_t^\top .

It also avoids the explicit inverse: the gain is computed as the solution of S K^\top = H P^\top, one linear solve, as jnp.linalg.solve in kalman_jax/filters.py. The page does the same.

What is the Kalman filter?

In plain words: a running weighted average between what the model predicts and what the sensor says, with the weights set by how uncertain each is, and an honest bookkeeping of the remaining uncertainty.

More precisely: for a linear model with Gaussian noise it is the exact Bayesian posterior p(x_t \mid z_{1:t}), which stays Gaussian, so the mean and covariance are the whole story. For any linear model with any noise of the given covariances it is the linear estimator of minimum mean-square error. Its covariance recursion does not depend on the data, only on F, H, Q, R and P_0, which is why the gain can be computed, and inspected, before a single measurement arrives.

Innovation
e_t = z_t - H\hat x_{t|t-1}: the part of the measurement the model did not foresee. For a correct model it is white with covariance S_t, the basis of every consistency test on a filter.
Gain
K_t = P_{t|t-1} H^\top S_t^{-1}: how far each state component moves per unit of innovation.
Joseph form
The covariance update written as a sum of two positive terms, stable in floating point and correct for any gain.

A worked first step

Start from \hat x_0 = (0, 0), P_0 = I. Prediction: F P_0 F^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + \Delta t^2 & \Delta t \\ \Delta t & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.01 & 0.1 \\ 0.1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, plus the tiny Q: P_{1|0} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 1.0100 & 0.1001 \\ 0.1001 & 1.0010 \end{pmatrix}. The measured quantity is the position, so S_1 = 1.0100 + 0.25 = 1.2600, and

K_1 = \frac{1}{1.2600} \begin{pmatrix} 1.0100 \\ 0.1001 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.8016 \\ 0.0794 \end{pmatrix}.

The first measurement moves the position estimate 80% of the way to the reading (the prior sd 1 against the sensor's 0.5, variances 1 against 0.25, 1/1.25 = 0.8 up to the process noise) and nudges the velocity by 8% of the innovation, through the correlation that one time step created. The posterior position variance is P_{1|0,11} - K_1^2 S_1 = 1.0100 - 0.8016^2 \times 1.2600 = 0.2004, a standard deviation of 0.448: one reading of a 0.5-sd sensor combined with a prior of sd 1 gives (1^{-2} + 0.5^{-2})^{-1/2} = 0.447, as it should. The gain then falls step after step as the filter learns the velocity; Part 5 shows where it settles.

On the repository's data. With the true model, the linear filter achieves a position RMSE of 0.1554 against the sensor's 0.5 and a velocity RMSE of 0.1351 for a quantity it never sees. Its steady-state covariance predicts standard deviations of 0.1631 and 0.1315; the achieved values are within a few percent, the agreement one expects when the filter's model is the one that generated the data.

Part 3

The extended filter: linearise where you are

When the dynamics or the measurement are nonlinear, x_{t+1} = f(x_t) + w_t and z_t = h(x_t) + \nu_t, the posterior is no longer Gaussian and no finite recursion is exact. The extended Kalman filter (EKF) keeps the Kalman equations and replaces the matrices by the Jacobians of f and h at the current estimate [2]:

\hat x_{t|t-1} = f(\hat x_{t-1}), \qquad P_{t|t-1} = F_t P_{t-1} F_t^\top + Q, \qquad F_t = \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{\hat x_{t-1}}, \qquad e_t = z_t - h(\hat x_{t|t-1}), \qquad H_t = \left.\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\right|_{\hat x_{t|t-1}}.

The means go through the true nonlinear functions; only the covariances are propagated through the linearisation. The repository's extended_kf_predict and extended_kf_update take f, h and their Jacobians as arguments, and in JAX the Jacobians can be jax.jacfwd(f) rather than hand-derived.

Example. If the sensor measured the distance to a beacon at s rather than the position itself, h(x) = |p - s| and H_t = (\operatorname{sign}(\hat p - s),\ 0): the same update equations, with a matrix that changes sign when the object passes the beacon, and a filter that is briefly very wrong near it, where the linearisation is worst.

What the repository's script tests. Its EKF is run on the linear model, with f(x) = Fx, h(x) = Hx and constant Jacobians. The Jacobians are then the matrices themselves and the EKF is the linear filter: the two agree to the last bit on this page, and to 32-bit rounding in JAX. This is a sanity check of the implementation, not a test of the EKF's approximation; the script's identical RMSE lines (0.1554 and 0.1351) are the expected outcome, not a finding.

Part 4

The unscented filter: sigma points, weights, and a 32-bit catastrophe

The unscented Kalman filter (UKF) avoids Jacobians altogether [3]. It represents the belief by 2n+1 deterministic sample points, the sigma points, pushes each through the nonlinear function exactly, and recovers the mean and covariance of the results by weighted sums. For a belief with mean \hat x and covariance P = L L^\top (Cholesky factor L, columns L_i):

\mathcal X_0 = \hat x, \qquad \mathcal X_{i} = \hat x + \sqrt{n + \lambda}\, L_i, \qquad \mathcal X_{n+i} = \hat x - \sqrt{n + \lambda}\, L_i, \qquad \lambda = \alpha^2 (n + \kappa) - n .

With weights, in the parametrisation of Wan and van der Merwe [5],

W_0^{(m)} = \frac{\lambda}{n + \lambda}, \qquad W_0^{(c)} = \frac{\lambda}{n + \lambda} + 1 - \alpha^2 + \beta, \qquad W_i^{(m)} = W_i^{(c)} = \frac{1}{2(n + \lambda)} .

The predicted mean is \sum_i W_i^{(m)} f(\mathcal X_i), the predicted covariance \sum_i W_i^{(c)} (f(\mathcal X_i) - \hat x_{t|t-1})(\cdot)^\top + Q, and the update uses the same construction for h, with the cross-covariance P_{xz} and the innovation covariance P_{zz} giving the gain K = P_{xz} P_{zz}^{-1}. For a linear f the weighted sums reproduce F\hat x and FPF^\top exactly, since the weights sum to one and the points' spread is exactly P: on the repository's linear model the UKF should match the linear filter, and in double precision it does, to 10^{-7}.

The repository's default is \alpha = 10^{-3}, \beta = 2, \kappa = 0. These are the textbook values [5], and for n = 2 they give \lambda = 2 \times 10^{-6} - 2, so n + \lambda = 2 \times 10^{-6}, a centre weight W_0^{(m)} = -999\,999, four outer weights of 250\,000, and sigma points only \sqrt{2 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.0014 standard deviations from the mean. The covariance is then reconstructed as a difference of terms a million times larger than the result. In double precision (16 significant digits) ten digits survive; in JAX's default 32-bit floats (7 digits) none do. The repository's UKF loses the symmetry and positive-definiteness of P, the Cholesky factor becomes NaN, and from step 335 the script prints nan for the UKF's RMSE. Two of its own tests fail for the same reason.

Catastrophic cancellation

In plain words: subtracting two nearly equal numbers throws away their leading digits and keeps only their rounding errors.

More precisely: a floating-point number carries about 7 significant decimal digits in 32-bit and 16 in 64-bit [6]. If the UKF's covariance P \approx 0.03 is the difference of weighted terms of size 10^6 \times 10^{-6} \times \ldots that each carry an absolute rounding error of order 10^{-7} times their own magnitude, the result's error is of order 10^{-1} times the result itself: wrong in the first digit. A filter's covariance must stay positive-definite; once a rounding error pushes an eigenvalue below zero the Cholesky factor does not exist and every later step is NaN.

The fix
Spread the points: \alpha = 1, so \lambda = 0, W_0^{(m)} = 0, W_i = 1/4, points \sqrt 2 standard deviations out. All weights are of order one and both precisions track the linear filter; the hero demo uses this. Alternatively enable 64-bit in JAX (jax.config.update("jax_enable_x64", True)), which the repository does not.
Why the textbook value exists
A small \alpha keeps the sigma points close to the mean so that strongly nonlinear functions are sampled locally; with \beta = 2 this also captures the fourth moment of a Gaussian. It presumes double precision. The point of the parameters is a trade-off between local accuracy and numerical range, and 10^{-3} sits at the wrong end of it for single precision.

What the page emulates. Browsers compute in double precision. To reproduce the failure, the widget rounds every intermediate result to the nearest 32-bit float with Math.fround, including the Cholesky factor and every weighted sum. The emulated filter turns to NaN at step 168 against JAX's 335: the exact step depends on the order in which the sums are accumulated, which JAX's compiled code and the page's loops do differently, but the mechanism and the outcome are the same. The repository already fixed one UKF bug, the sigma points used rows of the Cholesky factor instead of columns; this one is next. The patch proposed to the repository sets the default \alpha = 1, documents why, and makes the two failing tests pass.

Part 5

Gain and covariance over time

Nothing in the covariance recursion involves a measurement. Eliminating the gain, the predicted covariance obeys the discrete Riccati recursion

P_{t+1|t} = F P_{t|t-1} F^\top + Q - F P_{t|t-1} H^\top S_t^{-1} H P_{t|t-1} F^\top,

a deterministic iteration from P_0 that, for an observable model with Q \succ 0, converges to a fixed point P_\infty: the steady-state covariance, with the steady-state gain K_\infty = P_\infty H^\top (H P_\infty H^\top + R)^{-1} [2]. A filter run with the constant gain K_\infty is a fixed linear filter, the \alpha–\beta tracker of radar practice, and is what the full filter becomes after its transient.

The numbers say what the filter is doing. The steady-state gain (0.106, 0.060) means each new reading moves the position estimate a tenth of the way to it, averaging over roughly ten readings, and corrects the velocity by six hundredths of the innovation per step, which over a second of readings adds up to a velocity correction of order the innovation's size divided by the window. The position sd settles at 0.163, a third of the sensor's 0.5; the velocity sd at 0.132, for a quantity that is never measured and wanders by 0.032 per step. The innovation sd is \sqrt{S_\infty} = 0.529: slightly more than the sensor noise, the excess being the filter's own position uncertainty. On a correct model the actual innovations should have exactly this spread; on the repository's run they do, which is the simplest check that the filter and the simulator agree.

The Kalman filter is two equations and a ratio of uncertainties, and everything about its behaviour, the gain, the convergence, the achievable accuracy, can be read from the covariance recursion before the first measurement arrives. Its nonlinear cousins keep the equations and change how the belief is pushed through the model; the unscented version buys exactness on linear maps at the price of a numerical range that single precision does not have.

Part 6

Check your understanding

1. Why is the process-noise covariance Q not diagonal?

Because one random acceleration changes both the velocity (by \tau times itself) and the position (by \tau^2/2 times itself) within the same step. The two perturbations share a cause, so they are correlated, here with correlation \sqrt 3 / 2 \approx 0.87.

2. The filter never sees a velocity. How does it estimate one?

Through the off-diagonal entry of the covariance. After a prediction step, position and velocity uncertainties are correlated (F P F^\top has an off-diagonal \Delta t\, P_{22}), so a position innovation carries information about the velocity and the gain's second component is non-zero: 0.079 on the first step, 0.060 in steady state.

3. What does the innovation covariance S_t add up?

The sensor's own noise R and the belief's uncertainty about the measured quantity H P_{t|t-1} H^\top. In steady state here S_\infty = 0.25 + 0.0298 = 0.280, sd 0.529.

4. Why use the Joseph form rather than (I - KH)P?

The short form is only correct for the exact optimal gain and, in floating point, produces a matrix that is not exactly symmetric and can acquire a negative eigenvalue after many steps. The Joseph form is a sum of two symmetric non-negative terms, so it stays a valid covariance for any gain and any rounding.

5. Does the EKF beat the linear filter on this problem?

No, and it cannot: the model is linear, so the Jacobians equal F and H and the EKF is the linear filter under another name. The script's identical RMSE lines confirm the implementation, not any advantage.

6. Why does the UKF with \alpha = 10^{-3} fail only in 32-bit arithmetic?

Its weights are \pm 10^6 and 2.5 \times 10^5, so the covariance is a difference of terms a million times larger than itself. That costs six of the available significant digits: ten remain in double precision, none in single, where the covariance loses positive-definiteness and the Cholesky factorisation returns NaN.

7. What does the steady-state gain (0.106, 0.060) mean operationally?

Each new reading moves the position estimate about a tenth of the way to it, an effective average over roughly ten readings, and nudges the velocity by six hundredths of the innovation. These two numbers define a fixed linear filter that the full filter becomes after about five seconds.

8. The achieved position RMSE is 0.155 and the filter's own sd is 0.163. Is the gap a problem?

No. The RMSE is estimated from 1000 correlated errors and has a sampling spread of a few percent; the two agree within it. A systematic gap, especially one in the other direction, would indicate that the filter's Q or R disagree with the data, which chapter 04 meets on a real series.

Exercises

  1. Reproduce the failure, then fix it. Run the repository's script and watch the UKF line print nan. Then run the UKF with \alpha = 1 and compare its RMSE with the linear filter's.
    Hint

    run_ukf takes alpha, beta, kappa as keyword arguments. Find the first step at which jnp.isnan is true.

    Solution

    With the defaults the estimates are NaN from step 335 onward and the RMSE is NaN. With \alpha = 1 the UKF returns 0.1554 and 0.1351, equal to the linear filter's to four decimals (the worst difference between the two state trajectories is 2.3 \times 10^{-5}, 32-bit rounding).

    import jax, jax.numpy as jnp
from kalman_jax.filters import generate_linear_synthetic_data, run_linear_kf, run_ukf, compute_rmse

F, H, Q, R, xs, zs = generate_linear_synthetic_data(jax.random.PRNGKey(42), n_steps=1000)
x0, P0 = jnp.zeros(2), jnp.eye(2)
f, h = (lambda x: F @ x), (lambda x: H @ x)
bad = run_ukf(zs, f, h, Q, R, x0, P0)                 # alpha = 1e-3 by default
print("first NaN at step", int(jnp.argmax(jnp.isnan(bad[:, 0]))))     # 335
good = run_ukf(zs, f, h, Q, R, x0, P0, alpha=1.0)
print(compute_rmse(xs, good), compute_rmse(xs, run_linear_kf(zs, F, H, Q, R, x0, P0)))
  2. Lie to the filter about the sensor. Keep the data fixed and run the linear filter with R four times too large, then four times too small. What happens to the RMSE and to the spread of the innovations relative to \sqrt{S}?
    Hint

    Compare the sample standard deviation of e_t with the filter's \sqrt{S_\infty}. A consistent filter has a ratio near one.

    Solution

    The two lies are not symmetric. Overstating R (1.0 instead of 0.25) makes the filter more sluggish, but on this slowly drifting track that costs almost nothing: the position RMSE on the repository's data moves from 0.1554 to 0.1562 (and by a few percent either way on fresh rollouts), while the innovations come out at 0.52 of the filter's own \sqrt S, the filter over-states its uncertainty by the factor 2 it was lied to by. Understating R (0.0625) makes the filter chase the sensor noise: the RMSE rises by 8 to 10%, to 0.168 on the repository's data and 0.18 to 0.20 on fresh rollouts, and the innovations are twice the claimed \sqrt S. With the true R the ratio is 1.03. The innovation ratio diagnoses the lie and its direction even when the RMSE barely moves, which is why it, and not the RMSE one cannot compute without ground truth, is the working engineer's check.

  3. Derive Q for a jerk model. Suppose the acceleration, not the velocity, is the integrated white noise, with state (p, v, a). Write F and Q for a sample time \Delta t.
    Hint

    The transition over \tau is \Phi(\tau) with entries 1, \tau, \tau^2/2 above the diagonal; the noise enters the last component. Integrate \Phi(\tau) G G^\top \Phi(\tau)^\top.

    Solution

    F = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \Delta t & \Delta t^2/2 \\ 0 & 1 & \Delta t \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} and, with G = (0, 0, 1)^\top and v(\tau) = (\tau^2/2,\ \tau,\ 1)^\top,

    Q = q \int_0^{\Delta t} v(\tau) v(\tau)^\top d\tau = q \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\Delta t^5}{20} & \dfrac{\Delta t^4}{8} & \dfrac{\Delta t^3}{6} \\[0.6em] \dfrac{\Delta t^4}{8} & \dfrac{\Delta t^3}{3} & \dfrac{\Delta t^2}{2} \\[0.6em] \dfrac{\Delta t^3}{6} & \dfrac{\Delta t^2}{2} & \Delta t \end{pmatrix},

    the discrete Wiener-process-acceleration model of the tracking literature [4].

  4. Write the steady state down without iterating. For the scalar model x_{t+1} = x_t + w_t, z_t = x_t + \nu_t, with variances q and r, solve the Riccati fixed point for the predicted variance and the gain.
    Hint

    The recursion is p^+ = p + q - p^2/(p + r); set p^+ = p.

    Solution

    The fixed point satisfies p^2 - qp - qr = 0, so p_\infty = \frac{q + \sqrt{q^2 + 4qr}}{2} and K_\infty = \frac{p_\infty}{p_\infty + r}. For q \ll r, p_\infty \approx \sqrt{qr} and K_\infty \approx \sqrt{q/r}: the gain is the ratio of the noise standard deviations, and the filter averages over about \sqrt{r/q} readings. With q = 0.05 and r = 0.25, the values chapter 04's forecaster uses, p_\infty = 0.140 and K_\infty = 0.36; chapter 04's filter has A = 0.9 instead of 1, which shrinks the predicted variance and gives K_\infty = 0.31.

The chapter on one page

Part 9

Scope and further reading

Scope. This chapter covers the repository's first script and its filter module: the linear filter in Joseph form, the EKF interface, and the UKF with its sigma-point construction, all on a linear tracking problem where the exact answer is known. It does not treat smoothing, square-root filters or the nonlinear cases where the EKF and UKF actually differ, which [2] and [7] do. Chapter 03 keeps the linear filter and puts a neural network inside its prediction step.

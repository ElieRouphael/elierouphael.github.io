Tracking a vehicle with a Kalman filter

A vehicle's position is measured a hundred times a second by a sensor whose error has a standard deviation of five metres. We want a cleaner position and, if possible, the velocity nobody measures. Part II opens with the smallest possible Kalman filter, the twenty-line NumPy class of the author's kalman_vehicle_tracking repository: a constant-velocity model, two equations, and two noise matrices to choose. The filter runs below on the repository's own simulation. It helps, but less than one might hope, and finding out why is Part II's first lesson: a Kalman filter is only as good as the model inside it.

Part 1

The problem and the data

The data. The repository ships a CSV of 1000 rows produced by data/simulate_data.py: ten seconds sampled at \Delta t = 0.01 s, a true position p(t) = 15 \sin t^2 in metres, and a measured position with Gaussian noise of standard deviation 5 m. The position is a chirp: a sine whose frequency grows with time, \omega(t) = 2t rad/s, from zero to 20 rad/s, about 3 Hz, by the end. The vehicle oscillates ever faster between -15 and +15 m, a demanding motion for any tracker, and the sensor's noise is a third of the amplitude.

Columns that do not belong. The file also carries true_velocity = 2t, noisy_velocity, and a constant acceleration of 2. None of these describes the motion in the file: the velocity of 15\sin t^2 is 30\,t\cos t^2, which reaches nearly \pm 300 m/s, not 2t. The generator mixes two scenarios, a constant-acceleration car and a chirp, and keeps the position of one and the velocity of the other. The tracker only reads the noisy position, so its estimates are unaffected, but anyone scoring the velocity estimate against that column would conclude the filter is broken. The hero demo draws the true velocity and the column side by side; the correction proposed for the repository derives the velocity from the position.

The task. From the noisy positions alone, estimate the position better than the sensor does, and estimate the velocity.

Part 2

A Kalman filter in twenty lines

The repository's whole filter is a class with two methods (kalman/kalman_filter.py):

class KalmanFilter:
    def __init__(self, F, H, Q, R, P, x0):
        self.F, self.H, self.Q, self.R, self.P, self.x = F, H, Q, R, P, x0

    def predict(self):
        self.x = self.F @ self.x
        self.P = self.F @ self.P @ self.F.T + self.Q
        return self.x

    def update(self, z):
        y = z - self.H @ self.x                      # innovation
        S = self.H @ self.P @ self.H.T + self.R      # its covariance
        K = self.P @ self.H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)     # Kalman gain
        self.x = self.x + K @ y
        self.P = self.P - K @ self.H @ self.P
        return self.x

and the tracker calls predict() then update(z) for each measurement. Read the two methods as a conversation between a model and a sensor.

Predict: what the model expects. If the vehicle kept its velocity for one step, the position would advance by v\,\Delta t: that is x \leftarrow Fx. The filter is less sure after moving than before, because the velocity may have changed: its uncertainty grows, P \leftarrow FPF^\top + Q. The matrix Q is how much the model admits the state can change in one step for reasons it does not describe [1].

Update: what the sensor says. The innovation y = z - Hx is the surprise: the measurement minus what the model expected to measure. Its covariance S is the model's uncertainty about the measured quantity plus the sensor's own noise R. The gain

K = P H^\top S^{-1}

is the ratio of how much the state co-varies with the measured quantity to the total uncertainty of the measurement, and it decides how far to move toward the measurement. When the sensor is precise compared with the model (R small against HPH^\top), HK \to 1 and the position estimate jumps to the reading; when the model is precise, K \to 0 and the measurement is ignored. In between, the velocity gets corrected too, by the second component of K, P_{pv}/(P_{pp} + R) of the predicted covariance, because a prediction step correlates position and velocity: a position surprise is partly a velocity surprise. Even with a perfect sensor that entry does not vanish: with the repository's Q the gain tends to (1, 1.72), not (1, 0) (question 9). Finally the uncertainty shrinks, P \leftarrow P - KHP, since a measurement has been absorbed.

What is a Kalman filter?

In plain words: a running compromise between a model's prediction and a sensor's reading, weighted by how much each is trusted, with the trust itself updated every step.

More precisely: for a linear model with Gaussian noise it is the exact Bayesian posterior over the state, and the minimum-variance linear estimator for any noise with the given covariances [2]. The twenty lines above are the complete algorithm; everything else in filtering is about the model, the noise matrices, and numerical care.

Innovation
Measurement minus predicted measurement. For a correct model it is unpredictable (white) with covariance S.
Gain
The step taken per unit of innovation; a 2-vector here, one entry for the position and one for the velocity.
Covariance update
The repository's P - KHP is the textbook form, exact for the optimal gain but able to lose symmetry in floating point over long runs. Chapter 02 uses the Joseph form; on this 1000-step run they agree to 15 digits.

The repository's numbers. With Q = \mathrm{diag}(1, 3), R = 25, P_0 = I and x_0 = 0, the filter's position RMSE over the run is 4.50 m against 5.00 m for the raw measurements: a 10% improvement, not the factor of three that chapter 02 gets on a well-modelled track. The gain settles at (0.195, 0.311): each measurement moves the position estimate a fifth of the way to the reading. The page reproduces the repository's estimates to 7 \times 10^{-5} m, the four-decimal rounding of the page's copy of the data.

Part 3

Choosing Q and R

R is the easy one: it is the sensor's noise variance, and the simulation says 5^2 = 25. On a real sensor it comes from the datasheet or from the spread of readings of a known position. Q is the hard one. It should describe how much the true state departs from the model's prediction in one step. For a vehicle whose velocity changes by an unknown acceleration a over \Delta t, the position departs by a\Delta t^2/2 and the velocity by a\Delta t, which gives the piecewise-constant Q of question 4 (chapter 02 uses its integrated white-noise cousin). The repository instead picks \mathrm{diag}(1, 3), a position wobble of 1 m and a velocity wobble of 1.7 m/s per hundredth of a second, with no correlation between them. In question 4's units, the velocity entry of 3 is an acceleration of \sqrt{3}/\Delta t \approx 173 m/s², and the position entry of 1 would alone need 20 000 m/s². The chirp's own acceleration has an RMS of 212, 1160 and 3082 m/s² over the three thirds of the run and peaks at 5936. The repository's Q is a guess, and the sweep below shows what the guess costs.

Two readings of the curve. First, the repository's Q is five to ten times too small for this motion. The minimum is flat: 2.89 m at seven times the scale and 2.93 m at ten, against 4.50 m at the repository's. Second, no scale makes the filter as good as it was on chapter 02's gentle track, because the model is wrong in a way that no noise level repairs. A larger Q buys agility at the price of noise: at the optimum the filter has stopped averaging very much, and at scale 1000 it is close to the raw sensor again (4.88 m against 5.00).

R is a property of the sensor; Q is a confession about the model. When tuning Q changes the result by a factor of two, the model is missing something, and the right fix is usually in F, not in Q. And when Q is tuned, its structure matters as much as its size: it decides what the filter blames a surprise on.

Part 4

When the model is wrong

Split the run into thirds. In the first, where the chirp is slow, the filter's error is 2.2 m against the sensor's 5.1: it works as advertised. In the second, 4.3 against 4.7. In the last, 6.1 against 5.1: the filter is now worse than the sensor it is filtering. The constant-velocity model predicts that the vehicle keeps going; a chirp at 3 Hz turns around every sixth of a second; the estimate lags behind the truth. A sine of amplitude 15 m lagged by an angle \varphi is off by \sqrt{2}\cdot 15 \sin(\varphi/2) m in RMS: as far from the truth as the sensor's 5 m at a lag of 27°, about a thirteenth of a period (0.024 s by the end of the run), and as far as the estimate 0 at a sixth of a period.

The velocity estimate tells the same story more bluntly. Over the run its RMSE against the true 30\,t\cos t^2 is 127 m/s, worse than the 122 m/s of answering 0 every time, and in the last two thirds it is anti-correlated with the truth (correlation −0.24, then −0.46). It is also an eighth of the truth's size: an RMS of 15 m/s against 122, a peak of 29 against 296. The estimate is too sluggish to follow the chirp and too late for what little it follows: by the time it has moved toward a velocity, the velocity has reversed. No scale of the diagonal Q helps; every scale from 0.01 to 1000 gives 121 to 128 m/s. A Q with the right structure, question 4's single acceleration noise that moves position and velocity together, does better: with \sigma_a \approx 5500 m/s² the velocity error falls to 90 m/s and the position error to 2.81 m. That is still far from the truth, so the constant-velocity model, F, remains the main fault. This is not a defect of the Kalman filter. It is what the filter correctly computes for the model it was given, and the lesson carries through the course: chapter 02 shows what the same filter achieves when the model is right, chapter 03 lets a network learn the part of the model that is missing, and chapter 04 meets a real series where the "model" is a guess again.

Corrections to the repository. (1) The true_velocity and acceleration columns are inconsistent with the position; derive them from 15\sin t^2. (2) The generator uses an unseeded random state, so the CSV cannot be regenerated; seed it. (3) Q = \mathrm{diag}(1, 3) is five to ten times too small for the chirp (RMSE 4.50 m; 2.93 m at ten times the scale, the value the repository now uses) and should be derived from an acceleration budget, or the model should carry an acceleration state. (4) np.linalg.inv(S) and P - KHP are harmless here but worth replacing by a solve and the Joseph form before the code grows. All four are now applied in the repository; this page keeps running on the CSV as first committed, so that the numbers above can be checked against the original script. The regenerated CSV in the download has consistent columns and a new, seeded noise draw, on which the corrected tracker prints 4.890 m for the measurements and 2.989 m for the filter (4.492 m at the original Q). The CSV as first committed is in the download too, as part2/data/vehicle_example_data_original.csv, and part2/ch01_numbers.py reproduces every number on this page: those above from that file, and the regenerated CSV's by re-running the seeded generator.

Part 5

Check your understanding

1. What are the two steps of the filter, in one sentence each?

Predict moves the estimate forward with the model and grows the uncertainty by Q; update corrects the estimate toward the measurement by the gain times the innovation and shrinks the uncertainty.

2. Why can the filter estimate a velocity it never measures?

Because the prediction step correlates position and velocity (it adds \Delta t\,P_{vv} to the off-diagonal of P), so a position surprise carries information about the velocity. The second entry of the gain, 0.31 here, applies it.

3. What is R for this data, and how would you know on a real sensor?

The noise variance of the position sensor, 5^2 = 25. On a real sensor: the datasheet, or the variance of readings taken at a known position.

4. The repository's Q is diagonal. What would a physically derived Q look like?

For an unknown acceleration, the position and velocity perturbations share a cause and are correlated: Q = \sigma_a^2 \begin{pmatrix} \Delta t^4/4 & \Delta t^3/2 \\ \Delta t^3/2 & \Delta t^2 \end{pmatrix} for a piecewise-constant acceleration, or chapter 02's integrated white-noise version.

5. Why does increasing Q help here, and why does it stop helping?

A larger Q makes the filter trust the model less and the measurements more, so it follows the fast chirp with less lag. Past the optimum it follows the sensor's noise too, and at very large Q the estimate is the raw measurement.

6. In the last third the filter is worse than the sensor. Is the filter wrong?

No, the model is. The filter computes the optimal estimate for a constant-velocity vehicle; the actual vehicle reverses every sixth of a second. The estimate lags, and a lagged sine can be further from the truth than a noisy one.

7. Would the Joseph-form covariance update change the estimates?

Not visibly: on this 1000-step run the two updates agree to 15 digits. The Joseph form matters for long runs, badly scaled problems and single precision, where P - KHP can lose symmetry or positive-definiteness.

8. What is wrong with scoring the velocity estimate against the CSV's true_velocity?

That column is 2t, the velocity of a different scenario; the position in the file has velocity 30\,t\cos t^2. Against the wrong column any estimator looks broken. Against the right one this filter's velocity is in fact poor, worse than answering 0 (127 against 122 m/s), but for a reason the column hides: the model is too sluggish and too late for this motion (Part 4).

9. As R \to 0, what does the gain tend to, and why is its velocity entry not 0?

To (1, P_{pv}/P_{pp}) of the predicted covariance, which is (1, 1.72) for the repository's Q. A perfect sensor pins the position, but each prediction step correlates position and velocity again (it adds \Delta t\,P_{vv} to the off-diagonal), so a surprise in the position still says something about the velocity.

Part 6

Exercises

  1. Fix the generator. Rewrite simulate_data.py so that velocity and acceleration are the derivatives of the position, and seed the random generator.
    Hint

    \frac{d}{dt} 15\sin t^2 = 30t\cos t^2; differentiate once more for the acceleration, or use np.gradient on the position.

    Solution 
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
time = np.arange(0, T, dt)
true_position = 15 * np.sin(time**2)
true_velocity = 30 * time * np.cos(time**2)
acceleration = 30 * np.cos(time**2) - 60 * time**2 * np.sin(time**2)
noisy_position = true_position + rng.normal(0, 5, size=time.shape)
noisy_velocity = true_velocity + rng.normal(0, 1, size=time.shape)

    The velocity now reaches nearly ±300 m/s and the acceleration nearly ±6000 m/s², which also says why a constant-velocity model struggles.

  2. Give the model an acceleration state. Use x = (p, v, a) with F from chapter 02's exercise 4 and a small Q on the acceleration only. Does the position RMSE improve on the chirp?
    Hint

    Keep H = (1, 0, 0) and R = 25; sweep the acceleration noise variance over several decades as the page does for Q.

    Solution

    With Q = \mathrm{diag}(0, 0, q_a), P_0 = I and R = 25, a small q_a does what intuition says: it improves in the slow part of the run, where the acceleration is nearly constant over many samples, and lags badly at the end, where the acceleration itself reverses every sixth of a second. The best q_a beats the repository's filter, but not a constant-velocity filter with a tuned Q:

    ModelRMSE [m]By thirds [m]
    constant velocity, the repository's Q4.502.20, 4.26, 6.14
    constant velocity, Q scaled by 7.22.892.53, 2.63, 3.44
    constant velocity, question 4's Q, \sigma_a \approx 55002.812.57, 2.64, 3.18

    A chirp is not well described by any low-order polynomial model; a model with the oscillation built in (a rotating state, as in a phase-locked loop) would do better. The honest answer to "which model" is "one that contains the motion".

  3. Check the innovations. Compute the innovations y_t and their predicted variance S_t for the repository's settings. Is the ratio of their sample variance to S near one? What does it say about Q?
    Hint

    Store y and S inside update. A consistent filter has \mathrm{var}(y)/S \approx 1; a ratio well above one means the filter is over-confident.

    Solution

    The ratio is well above one, 1.84 over the run, and it grows as the chirp accelerates: 1.09, 1.78 and 2.64 over the three thirds. The innovations are larger than the filter predicts because the model's lag feeds systematic error into them. With Q ten times larger the ratio is 0.86 (0.79, 0.78 and 1.01 by thirds), slightly under one, so that Q is if anything a little too large. This is the diagnostic that says "increase Q or fix the model" without needing the ground truth that only a simulation provides; J4 runs it over a thousand simulated filters and gives it a confidence band.

  4. Run it on your own sensor. Record any noisy scalar signal you can get (a phone's accelerometer, a potentiometer, a stock price) at a fixed rate and apply the class with a constant-velocity model. Tune Q by the innovation ratio of exercise 3, not by eye.
    Hint

    Estimate R first from a stretch where the signal is constant.

    Solution

    There is no single answer; the point is the procedure. Expect the same shape of result as here: the filter averages well where the signal is smooth and lags where it turns, and the innovation ratio tells you which regime you are in.

Part 8

Scope and further reading

Scope. This chapter is the gentlest start to Part II: one filter, one model, one simulation, and a reading of its result. It does not derive the filter, treat numerical stability or consider nonlinear models; chapter 02 does all three on a track where the model is right, so that the filter's behaviour can be separated from the model's errors.

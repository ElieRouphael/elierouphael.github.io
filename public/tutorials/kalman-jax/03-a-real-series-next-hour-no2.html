Kalman Filters with JAX · Chapter 03 of 03

A real series: next-hour NO2

Simulated plants are kind: the model is right, the noise is Gaussian, and ground truth is available. Real series are not. The repository's third experiment takes chapter 02's learned forecaster to a year of hourly nitrogen-dioxide readings from a polluted street and asks it to predict the next hour from the current sensor array and weather. This chapter rebuilds the pipeline and the baselines, re-runs the trained filter live with noise matrices you can change, and reaches the result the repository's script prints without comment: the learned filter loses to the forecast that says the next hour will look like this one. Why it loses, and what would have to change, is the point of the chapter.

Part 1

The data and the pipeline

The dataset. The UCI Air Quality dataset [1] [2] holds 9358 hourly rows from March 2004 to April 2005, recorded by a multi-sensor device at road level in a polluted Italian city: the responses of five metal-oxide sensors, temperature, relative and absolute humidity, and, as targets, the concentrations of CO, benzene, NOx and NO2 from a co-located reference analyser. Missing values are coded as −200. The repository's task is to forecast the reference NO2 one hour ahead from the current hour's eight sensor and weather readings.

The pipeline (kalman_jax/air_quality.py). Rows with any missing input or target are dropped (7393 of 9358 remain, since the NO2 analyser has long gaps); the target is the next row's NO2; the rows are split in time, the first 70% for training, the next 15% for validation, the last 15% for test; and inputs and target are standardised with the training split's mean and standard deviation only. The result: 5173 training hours (March to December 2004), 1107 validation hours (December to February) and 1110 test hours (16 February to 4 April 2005). The training NO2 has mean 100.2 µg/m³ and standard deviation 41.3; the standardised target is the series the models actually see.

Two things to know about the split. The test split is seven weeks of late winter and early spring, following a training set of spring to autumn; the diurnal cycle and the weather are not the same, and some loss from training to test is to be expected for any fitted model. And the "next row" is the next hour only when no row was dropped in between: the test split has 25 places where consecutive rows are more than an hour apart (the hero chart marks them), where the target is really two or more hours ahead. Both affect all models equally and neither changes the conclusions, but the second is a small correction to propose for the repository's pipeline.

Part 2

The baselines

Before any learned model, four forecasts that need no training or almost none, scored by RMSE and MAE in µg/m³ on the validation and test splits [3]:

  1. Climatology: always predict the training mean. Test RMSE 63.2, the scale of the series itself.
  2. Linear from the eight sensors, the repository's "linear regression baseline": least squares from the standardised inputs to the standardised target, with an intercept. Test RMSE 43.9. It is a poor baseline for a strange reason: the current NO2 is not among its inputs, so it must infer the pollutant's level from cross-sensitive sensors, including a sensor nominally for NO2 whose response is a weak proxy of the reference value.
  3. Persistence: predict that the next hour equals this one. Test RMSE 25.4. No parameters, and the bar every hourly forecaster must clear, since hourly NO2 is strongly autocorrelated.
  4. Linear from the eight sensors plus the current NO2, added for this course: the proper linear baseline, nine inputs and an intercept. Test RMSE 24.9, the best of the four and better than persistence by half a unit; the sensors add a little once the series' own level is in.

Persistence, and why it is hard to beat

In plain words: tomorrow's weather is today's. For a series that moves slowly compared with how often it is sampled, the last value is an excellent forecast of the next.

More precisely: the persistence error is the one-step change y_{t+1} - y_t, whose RMS is \sigma \sqrt{2(1 - \rho_1)} for a stationary series of standard deviation \sigma and lag-one autocorrelation \rho_1. Over the whole record the lag-one autocorrelation is 0.90; on the test split, a persistence RMSE of 25.4 against a standard deviation of about 41 corresponds to \rho_1 \approx 0.81. Four fifths or more of the next hour is already in this hour. A model beats persistence only by predicting the change, and hourly NO2 changes for reasons, traffic and sunlight, that a sensor array sees only indirectly and the hour of day sees directly.

Climatology
Predict the long-run mean. The error is \sigma itself; any forecaster worth running beats it.
Autoregressive baseline
A linear model that includes the series' own last value. If a learned model does not have access to the same information, the comparison is unfair in the learned model's favour or against it.
Time split
Train on the past, test on the future, never the reverse: random splits of an hourly series leak the neighbours of every test hour into training.
Part 3

The Kalman-MLP forecaster

The repository's model (Air Quality with JAX.py with kalman_jax/forecasters.py) is chapter 02's forecaster with a scalar state, the standardised NO2 level x_t, and the eight standardised readings as the input u_t:

\hat x_{t+1}^- = A \hat x_t + f_\theta(\hat x_t, u_t), \qquad \hat y_{t+1} = \hat x_{t+1}^-, \qquad P^-_{t+1} = A^2 P_t + Q, \qquad K_{t+1} = \frac{P^-_{t+1}}{P^-_{t+1} + R}, \qquad \hat x_{t+1} = \hat x_{t+1}^- + K_{t+1} (y_{t+1} - \hat x_{t+1}^-),

with A = 0.9, Q = 0.05, R = 0.25, P_0 = 1, the state initialised at the first current reading, and f_\theta a network 9 → 32 → 32 → 1 with tanh units (1409 parameters). It is trained by Adam (learning rate 10^{-3}, 1500 steps) on the one-step forecast loss, the mean squared standardised error of \hat y_{t+1} over the whole training split run as one 5173-step sequence; the repository, correctly, uses the forecast loss here and not chapter 02's leaky one. Standardised losses are read against persistence's training MSE of 0.236.

Read the recursion once more. The state after the update at t+1 is a weighted average of the model's forecast and the actual reading y_{t+1}, with weight K on the reading. In steady state (chapter 01, Part 5, with A = 0.9) K_\infty = 0.31: the state moves less than a third of the way to each new reading. The next forecast is A \hat x_{t+1} plus a residual computed from that state. So the one input that persistence uses, the current reading, enters the Kalman-MLP only through an exponential smoother with a time constant of about three hours, shrunk further by A. The model is built to lag.

The numbers confirm it. On the test split the Kalman-MLP forecast has RMSE 33.7 against the next hour's reading, and 28.8 against the current hour's: it is closer to the past than to the future. The network has learned to compensate as best it can, pushing the state toward where the series is, but it is fed a blurred version of the only strong predictor there is. Persistence, with no parameters, scores 25.4; the linear model with the current reading, 24.9.

Retuning the noise. The lag is a property of the filter's noise matrices, not of the network, and it can be tested: with A = 1, Q = 0.5, R = 0.05 the steady-state gain is 0.92 and the state is nearly the current reading. Retrained with those settings for 3000 steps the model reaches a test RMSE of 29.4 (validation 28.6): better by four units, still four behind persistence. Move the sliders in the hero demo for the untrained version of the same experiment: with the repository's weights unchanged, a gain near one already brings the test RMSE from 33.7 down to about 29.5. What remains is a network that fits its training year (loss 0.159 against persistence's 0.236) and does not carry over to the test weeks, the gap Part 4 shows in the loss curve.

Part 4

Where it loses

Three views, one story. By hour of day, every forecaster is worst in the morning rush, when NO2 climbs by 22 µg/m³ an hour on average, and the Kalman-MLP is worst by the widest margin exactly there, because a lagging state is most wrong when the series turns. Against the diagonal, its cloud is tilted toward the mean, slope 0.68 against persistence's 0.87: it under-predicts high hours and over-predicts low ones, as a smoother must. And in training, the repository's run ends barely below persistence's own training error (0.220 against 0.236 in standardised units) while the retuned run gets well below it (0.159); neither gain carries to the test weeks. The uncertainty band covers 87% of outcomes against a nominal 95%: the filter's Q + R were set by hand, in standardised units, and not fitted to anything.

A Kalman filter's state is a smoothed past. If the quantity you forecast is best predicted by its own last value, feeding that value through a filter with a gain of 0.3 throws away most of the information before the network sees it. Baselines first: persistence and a linear model with the right inputs tell you what the learned model has to explain, and whether it has the inputs to do so.

Corrections to the repository. (1) The air-quality test file fails on any pandas version: its ten-row fixture leaves seven usable rows, the 15% validation slice is then empty and build_no2_forecasting_dataset raises "Forecast horizon is too large"; a larger fixture or a larger validation fraction fixes the test. (2) The "linear regression baseline" omits the current NO2 and therefore understates what a linear model can do by a factor of 1.8 (43.9 against 24.9). (3) The script prints the three scores without noting that the learned model loses to persistence; the README should say so. (4) The next-row target crosses gaps in the record. Patches are proposed alongside this course.

Part 5

What would beat persistence

Each diagnosis suggests a change, and the cheap ones can be tested with least squares in a second. The results, computed for this course with the repository's pipeline:

Time of day is the missing input. Four numbers, the sine and cosine of the hour at one and two cycles a day, cut the linear model's test RMSE from 24.9 to 21.6, a 13% gain over persistence, with the sensors contributing another half unit on top of the current reading and the clock. At a three-hour horizon, where persistence degrades to 52, the same model scores 35: the further ahead, the more the daily cycle matters and the less the last value does. No network was needed for any of this.

Learning the noise helps the filter, not the forecast. Making \log Q and \log R trainable alongside the network (the gradient flows through the gains, chapter 02's reverse pass extended by two parameters) moves them from 0.05 and 0.25 toward 0.11 and 0.11 within 1500 steps and is still moving; the test RMSE improves to 28.4 but stays behind persistence. The filter learns to trust the readings more; it cannot learn an input it does not have.

What a fair learned model would look like. Give the network the current reading directly and the hour of day; keep the Kalman state for what a state is for, the slowly varying bias of a drifting sensor array; validate by early stopping on the validation split rather than a fixed step count; and report every score next to persistence. That model might beat 21.6. The one in the repository was never given the chance, and the honest summary of its experiment is a negative result with a clear cause.

Part 6

Check your understanding

1. Why must the split be in time rather than random?

Hourly NO2 is strongly autocorrelated, so a random split puts the neighbours of every test hour into the training set and any flexible model can interpolate them. A time split tests what a forecaster is for: the future from the past. It also exposes the model to a different season, which is realistic.

2. Why is persistence so hard to beat here?

Because the lag-one autocorrelation is high, 0.90 over the whole record and about 0.81 implied on the test split: four fifths or more of the next hour's value is the current one. The persistence RMSE \sigma\sqrt{2(1-\rho_1)} is 25 against a series standard deviation of 41. A model has to predict the hourly change, which depends mostly on the time of day.

3. Why is the repository's linear baseline (43.9) so much worse than persistence?

It does not include the current NO2 among its inputs, so it must reconstruct the level from cross-sensitive metal-oxide sensors. Adding the current reading as a ninth input gives 24.9. A baseline that lacks the strongest predictor understates what the model class can do.

4. In what sense is the Kalman-MLP's state "lagged"?

The update moves the state only a fraction K \approx 0.31 of the way to each reading, so the state is an exponentially weighted average of past readings with a time constant of about three hours. Its forecast is closer to the current reading (RMSE 28.8) than to the next one (33.7).

5. Why does raising Q or lowering R improve the forecast without retraining?

Either raises the gain toward one, so the state tracks the readings and the network's input is closer to the current value it was supposed to forecast from. With the repository's weights, a gain near one takes the test RMSE from 33.7 to about 29.5; retraining at that gain gives 29.4.

6. Why does the band cover 87% rather than 95%?

The forecast variance P^- + R comes from Q and R that were set by hand in standardised units, never fitted to the data, and the filter's state model ignores the diurnal cycle whose large morning errors fall outside the band. A calibrated band would need Q, R estimated from the innovations.

7. The retuned model's training loss (0.159) is well below persistence's (0.236), yet it loses on the test split. What happened?

Overfitting to one year, plus a season change: the training split is spring to autumn, the test split late winter to spring. A 1409-parameter network trained for a fixed 3000 steps with no validation-based stopping fits the training year's particulars. The validation score (28.6) was there to warn; the repository does not use it to stop.

8. What single input would help the most, and why does the Kalman-MLP not have it?

The hour of day. It explains the morning rise and evening fall that dominate the hourly changes; two harmonics in a linear model improve on persistence by 13%. The repository's inputs are the sensor array and weather only, and the pipeline never passes the timestamp to the model.

Part 7

Exercises

  1. Fix the failing test. Run pytest tests/test_air_quality.py, read the error, and make the test pass without changing the pipeline's defaults.
    Hint

    Count how many of the fixture's ten rows survive the missing-value filter, then how many land in a 15% validation slice.

    Solution

    Seven rows survive; \lfloor 7 \times 0.15 \rfloor = 1 row goes to validation and the horizon of 1 leaves it empty, so build_no2_forecasting_dataset raises "Forecast horizon is too large". Either give the fixture 20 to 30 rows or call the builder with val_fraction=0.3 in the test. The pipeline itself is fine; the test's data are too small for its own split.

  2. Add the clock. Append \sin and \cos of 2\pi h/24 and 4\pi h/24, with h the hour of the current reading, to the linear model with the current NO2. Report validation and test RMSE at horizons 1 and 3.
    Hint

    The timestamps are in each split's timestamps array; build the four columns and concatenate them to the standardised inputs before fit_linear_regression.

    Solution

    Horizon 1: 22.2 / 21.6 against 25.3 / 24.9 without the clock and 25.2 / 25.4 for persistence. Horizon 3: 36.2 / 34.9 against 46.8 / 46.4 and 51.4 / 52.1. The clock alone with the current reading, no sensors, gives 22.8 / 22.2 at horizon 1.

    import numpy as np
from kalman_jax.air_quality import *

def clock(split):
    h = np.array([int(str(t)[11:13]) for t in split["timestamps"]], dtype=np.float32)
    w = 2 * np.pi * h / 24
    return np.c_[np.sin(w), np.cos(w), np.sin(2 * w), np.cos(2 * w)]

frame = load_air_quality_frame("data/raw/AirQualityUCI.csv")
for horizon in (1, 3):
    ds = build_no2_forecasting_dataset(frame, input_columns=DEFAULT_INPUT_COLUMNS, horizon=horizon)
    tr, te = ds["splits"]["train"], ds["splits"]["test"]; inv = ds["target_scaler"].inverse_transform
    feats = lambda s: np.c_[s["controls"], s["current_target"], clock(s)]
    w = fit_linear_regression(feats(tr), tr["future_target"])
    print(horizon, rmse(inv(te["future_target"]), inv(predict_linear_regression(w, feats(te)))))
  3. Learn the noise. Make \log Q and \log R trainable parameters of the forecasting loss and train for 1500 steps with the repository's other settings. Where do they go, and does the test RMSE beat persistence?
    Hint

    Wrap forecasting_loss_fn in a function of the tuple (params, log_q, log_r) and pass jnp.exp(log_q)[None, None] as Q; Adam handles the tuple.

    Solution

    From 0.05 and 0.25 they drift to 0.11 and 0.11 by step 1500, still moving, and the gain rises from 0.31 toward 0.6. The test RMSE falls from 33.7 to 28.4 and the validation RMSE to 29.1; persistence (25.4) still wins. The filter learns to trust its readings more, which is the direction of Part 3's retuning, but the forecast is limited by the inputs, not by the noise matrices.

  4. Forecast three hours ahead. Rebuild the dataset with horizon=3 and score persistence, climatology and the two linear models. Which forecast degrades the most, and why?
    Hint

    Persistence's error at horizon k is \sigma\sqrt{2(1-\rho_k)}; the autocorrelation falls with the lag.

    Solution

    Persistence goes from 25.4 to 52.1, almost to climatology's 63; the linear model with the current reading to 46.4; the one with the clock to 34.9. The last value loses its predictive power fastest, while the daily cycle is as predictable three hours ahead as one. Any forecaster meant for longer horizons should carry the clock, and the Kalman-MLP as built would lose by more, not less.

Part 8

The chapter on one page

  1. The data: 7393 complete hours of five metal-oxide sensors, weather and reference NO2 from an Italian street; a 70/15/15 time split (5173 / 1107 / 1110 hours), standardised on the training split; test weeks 16 February to 4 April 2005.
  2. The baselines: climatology 63.2, linear from the sensors 43.9, persistence 25.4, linear with the current NO2 24.9 (test RMSE, µg/m³).
  3. The Kalman-MLP: a scalar state with A = 0.9, Q = 0.05, R = 0.25, gain 0.31, and a 1409-parameter residual; test RMSE 33.7, closer to the current reading (28.8) than to the next: a lagged state.
  4. Retuning: a gain near one gives 29.5 with the same weights and 29.4 retrained; learned Q, R give 28.4; all behind persistence, because of overfitting to one year and a missing input.
  5. The missing input: the hour of day. Two harmonics in a linear model give 21.6 at one hour and 34.9 at three hours, where persistence gives 52.1.

Part 9

Scope and further reading

Scope. This chapter covers the repository's air-quality pipeline and forecaster and the baselines needed to read its result. It does not build a competitive NO2 forecaster, treat the sensors' drift and cross-sensitivity, which the dataset was collected to study [1], or cover the structural time-series models in which a Kalman filter carries a daily cycle and a slowly varying level by design [4] [5]. Those are the natural next step for anyone who wants the filter to earn its place in this problem.

This ends the course. Three experiments, three lessons: a textbook constant can be a bug in single precision; a model that fits its training trajectory has not learned the dynamics until a fresh one says so; and on real data, the first comparison is with the forecast that costs nothing.

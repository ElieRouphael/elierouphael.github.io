Kalman Filters with JAX · Chapter 02 of 03

Learning what the model leaves out

Chapter 01's filter knew its model exactly. Real plants have a part we can write down, the linear dynamics, and a part we cannot, friction, aerodynamics, a sensor's drift. The repository's second experiment keeps the known part and puts a small neural network inside the filter's prediction step to learn the rest, training it by differentiating straight through the Kalman recursion. This chapter rebuilds that training in the browser, with the gradient derived by hand and checked against JAX's, and then asks the question the repository does not: does the network learn the dynamics, or just the one trajectory it was shown? The answer is the second, and the three changes that fix it are the real lesson.

Part 1

A grey-box model inside a filter

The plant. The repository's simulator (kalman_jax/learned_dynamics.py) is a two-state system driven by a scalar input u_t \sim \mathcal N(0, 1):

x_{t+1} = A x_t + B u_t + r(x_t)\, \mathbf 1, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0.1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad r(x) = 3 \sin x_0 + 2 \cos x_1, \qquad y_t = x_t + \nu_t, \quad \nu_t \sim \mathcal N(0, 0.05^2 I).

Two things about it matter later. The linear part is chapter 01's double integrator, A has both eigenvalues at 1, so without the residual the state would random-walk; with it, the state wanders over tens of units in 50 steps. And the residual is a scalar added to both components (the generator computes 3\sin x_0 + 2\cos x_1 and NumPy broadcasting adds it to each entry), with an RMS size of \sqrt{9/2 + 4/2} = 2.55 per component; it is the dominant term of the dynamics, not a small correction. There is no process noise in the simulator; the only noise is the sensor's, sd 0.05 on both components.

The learned model. The filter is told A, B and C = I, and learns the residual with a network f_\theta: \mathbb R^3 \to \mathbb R^2 of input (x_0, x_1, u), four hidden layers of 16 tanh units and a linear output (914 parameters). Its prediction step is

\hat x_{t}^- = A \hat x_{t-1} + B u_t + f_\theta(\hat x_{t-1}, u_t), \qquad P_t^- = A P_{t-1} A^\top + Q,

followed by chapter 01's update with C = I, filter noise Q = 0.1 I and R = 0.5 I, and P_0 = I. Note what the covariance does not know: it is propagated with A alone, as if the residual were a known function with no derivative. The gains therefore do not depend on \theta at all, and the page precomputes the whole sequence K_1, \ldots, K_T once. Note too the mismatch: the filter assumes process noise of sd 0.32 and sensor noise of sd 0.71 per component, while the simulator has none of the first and 0.05 of the second. The filter's steady-state gain is about 0.36 per component (chapter 01, exercise 4, gives the scalar formula), so it moves its state only a third of the way to each measurement, which it should trust almost completely.

Two losses. The repository trains \theta by Adam on

L_{\text{filt}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{2T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \lVert y_t - C \hat x_t \rVert^2 \ \ \text{(the repository's \texttt{loss\_fn})}, \qquad L_{\text{pred}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{2T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \lVert y_t - C \hat x_t^- \rVert^2 \ \ \text{(its \texttt{forecasters.py})},

the first comparing the measurement with the state after it has been used, the second with the prediction before. The first is leaky: since y_t - C\hat x_t = (I - CK_t)(y_t - C\hat x_t^-), it is the forecast error shrunk by the factor I - K_t, and a filter that trusted its sensor completely (K = I) would score zero whatever \theta. With the repository's gain of about 0.36 the loss still sees 64% of each forecast error, so training works, but the scores are not comparable: the repository's final loss 0.0120 against the forecast loss's 0.0947 does not mean the first model is eight times better. On the fresh-rollout tests below the two train to much the same thing.

What is a grey-box model?

In plain words: a model with a part written from physics and a part learned from data, where the learned part is only asked to explain what the physics missed.

More precisely: x_{t+1} = A x_t + B u_t + f_\theta(x_t, u_t) with A, B fixed and f_\theta a flexible function approximator. Placing it inside a Kalman filter and training on the filter's output is "backprop KF" [3]: the state estimator becomes a differentiable program and its parameters are fitted end to end. The division of labour is the point. The linear part carries the structure, the extrapolation and the covariance; the network carries only the residual, and is trusted exactly as far as the data it saw.

Residual
What remains of the dynamics after the known part: here r(x)\mathbf 1, a function of the state only.
Filtered vs forecast loss
Score the state after the measurement update, or the prediction before it. The second is what a forecaster is for, and it cannot be gamed by trusting the sensor.
Dynamics error
\lVert f_\theta(x, u) - r(x)\mathbf 1 \rVert at the states a trajectory visits: a direct test of whether the function, not just the trajectory, was learned. The repository's compute_dynamics_error computes it, but only on the training rollout.
Part 2

Differentiating through the filter

In JAX the training step is one line, value_and_grad(loss_fn) around a lax.scan over the recursion. To see what that line computes, the page does it by hand. Write the recursion as two maps per step, prediction and update,

\hat x_t^- = A \hat x_{t-1} + B u_t + f_\theta(\hat x_{t-1}, u_t), \qquad \hat x_t = (I - K_t C)\, \hat x_t^- + K_t y_t,

with the gains known in advance. The loss depends on \theta through every state, and each state through all earlier ones. Reverse-mode differentiation, backpropagation through time [1], runs the chain rule backwards with two adjoint vectors per step, \lambda_t = \partial L / \partial \hat x_t and \mu_t = \partial L / \partial \hat x_t^-:

\lambda_t = g_t + \Big(A + \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial x}\Big|_{t+1}\Big)^{\!\top} \mu_{t+1}, \qquad \mu_t = (I - K_t C)^\top \lambda_t + g_t^-, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \Big(\frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial \theta}\Big|_{t}\Big)^{\!\top} \mu_t,

where g_t = \frac{1}{T} C^\top (C\hat x_t - y_t) is the direct derivative of L_{\text{filt}} with respect to the filtered state (zero for the forecast loss), g_t^- = \frac{1}{T} C^\top (C\hat x_t^- - y_t) the same for L_{\text{pred}} (zero for the filtered loss), and \mu_{T+1} = 0. The step from \mu_t back to \lambda_{t-1} is the network's own backward pass: the vector \mu_t enters its output layer, the weight gradients accumulate layer by layer, and what comes out of the input layer, restricted to the two state inputs, is (\partial f_\theta/\partial x)^\top \mu_t. The page's filterGrad is exactly these three lines plus a 914-parameter Adam update [2] with the repository's settings (learning rate 10^{-3}, \beta_1 = 0.9, \beta_2 = 0.999, bias-corrected moments as in optax.adam).

Checked three ways. With JAX's trained parameters, the page's forward pass reproduces JAX's filtered states to 2 \times 10^{-6} and its posterior standard deviations to 4 \times 10^{-8}. The hand-written gradient agrees with central finite differences to a relative 6 \times 10^{-7}, for both losses. And the page's first twenty Adam steps reproduce JAX's loss trace to a relative 5 \times 10^{-7} from the same initial parameters: the repository's first loss is 0.887283 and so is the page's. After a few hundred steps the two runs drift apart, as any two chaotic optimisations in 32-bit and 64-bit arithmetic must, and end at 0.0113 (page) against 0.0120 (JAX), two equally good minima. Press "load JAX's result" in the demo to switch between them.

Why the gains need no gradient here deserves a sentence. Because the covariance is propagated with A and not with the network's Jacobian, P_t and K_t are the same numbers for every \theta; JAX differentiates through them anyway and finds zero. In an EKF-style version, where \partial f_\theta / \partial x enters the covariance, the gains would carry gradient too and the reverse pass would be longer, but the three lines above would still be its skeleton.

Part 3

What the network learned

On the training rollout the result looks like a success. The loss falls by a factor of 74, the filtered state follows the measurements inside its band, and the learned residual matches the true one along the trajectory to an RMS error of 0.5 against a residual of RMS 1.6 on those states. The repository's script stops here, with three figures of this rollout. The right question is what happens on a trajectory the network has not seen.

The hero demo answers it with a fresh rollout, the same plant driven by new random inputs (JAX key 7). The filtered estimate is fine, since the filter is still told the measurements; but the one-step forecast, the thing the network is for, misses the next measurement by an RMSE of 8 to 9, three times worse than a filter with no residual at all (2.93), and the residual error on the visited states is 4 to 5, worse than predicting zero. The network has made the forecasts worse than ignorance.

The map shows why. The 50 training states occupy a thin curve in the plane; the network is accurate on that curve and nowhere else. Two facts of the setup conspire. The plant's state is unbounded: with A a double integrator and a residual of size 2.5 per step, a fresh rollout reaches x_0 \approx 30, far outside anything the training rollout visited. And a tanh network cannot extrapolate a periodic function: outside its data its output saturates to a constant, while the true residual keeps oscillating between -5 and 5. The network learned a lookup table for one trajectory. Nothing in the training signal asked for more.

A learned residual is only as good as the region of state space it was shown. Judge it by its dynamics error on trajectories it never saw, not by the fit of the trajectory it was trained on; the repository's script does the second and calls it a result.

Corrections to the repository. (1) The experiment's claim, that the learned filter "learns the nonlinear component" of the dynamics, holds only on the training rollout; on fresh rollouts the learned model forecasts worse than the linear model alone (8.5 against 2.9). (2) The filter's noise matrices (Q = 0.1I, R = 0.5I) are unrelated to the simulator's (no process noise, sensor sd 0.05), so the filter under-trusts a nearly perfect sensor. (3) The residual is added to both state components by broadcasting, which the README's "f(x,u) includes sin and cos terms" does not say. Patches for the documentation and a fresh-rollout evaluation are proposed alongside this course.

Part 4

Making it transfer

Each hypothesis from Part 3 can be tested by changing one thing and training again in JAX, scoring every model on fresh rollouts by its one-step forecast RMSE against the measurements and its dynamics error on the visited states, next to the zero-residual filter's forecast RMSE as the bar to clear. The table collects the runs, all made with the repository's own training functions: the first block varies the data and the optimisation with everything else as in the repository, the second varies the plant and the filter's noise matrices with 2000 Adam steps; every score is the mean over three fresh rollouts (keys 7, 11 and 13). More data from the same plant does not help. A 500-step training rollout, or five times the Adam steps, leaves the fresh-rollout forecast at 8 to 11, no better than 50 steps: the longer rollout wanders further, and the fresh one goes somewhere else again. Capacity is not the problem and neither is optimisation; the training loss reaches 3 \times 10^{-3}.

A bounded plant keeps the data where the test will be. Replacing A by \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0 & 0.9 \end{pmatrix}, eigenvalues 0.9 instead of 1, makes the state return toward the origin; every rollout then lives in the same bounded region of the plane, within about ten units of the origin. The learned residual's error on fresh rollouts drops from 4.3 to 1.85, and the dynamics error map (third button above) is now accurate across the visited region. But the forecast RMSE, 2.8, still loses to the zero-residual filter's 2.18.

Honest noise matrices turn a residual into a forecast. The remaining loss comes from the filter, not the network. With Q = 0.1I, R = 0.5I the filter's state trails the measurements by a third of each innovation, and the network, fed a lagging state, learns a residual for the wrong input. Setting the filter's noise to the simulator's, Q = 0.01I (a small floor, since the simulator has none) and R = 0.05^2 I, with the bounded plant and a 500-step rollout, gives a fresh-rollout forecast RMSE of 1.80 against 2.18 for the zero residual, and a dynamics error of 1.74. The learned filter finally beats the one that ignores the nonlinearity. On the original, unbounded plant the same honest noise helps too (3.7 instead of 8.2) but still loses to 2.93: no amount of filter tuning fixes a network that is asked to extrapolate.

Three changes, one lesson each: keep the states where the training data were, tell the filter the truth about its noises, and score the learned model on data it did not see. The repository's experiment is sound as a demonstration of the mechanism, differentiating through a filter, and unsound as evidence that the mechanism learned the dynamics.

Part 5

Check your understanding

1. Why do the Kalman gains not depend on the network's parameters?

Because the covariance is propagated with the known matrix A only; the network enters the mean prediction, not P. The gain sequence is therefore fixed before training, and JAX's gradient through it is identically zero.

2. In what sense is the repository's loss "leaky"?

It scores the state after the measurement has been folded in, so each error is the forecast error multiplied by I - K_t. With K \to I the loss vanishes regardless of the network. With the repository's gain of about 0.36 it still carries 64% of the signal, which is why training works; but its values (0.012) cannot be compared with a forecast loss (0.095).

3. What are \lambda_t and \mu_t in the reverse pass?

The derivatives of the loss with respect to the filtered state and the predicted state at step t, accumulated from all later steps. The update map sends \lambda_t to \mu_t through (I - K_t C)^\top; the prediction map sends \mu_{t+1} back to \lambda_t through (A + \partial f_\theta/\partial x)^\top, which is the network's backward pass.

4. The page's and JAX's loss traces agree to 5 \times 10^{-7} for twenty steps and then separate. Is one of them wrong?

No. They are the same algorithm in 64-bit and 32-bit arithmetic; the rounding differences grow through a nonconvex optimisation until the two runs follow different paths to different, equally good minima (0.0113 and 0.0120). Agreement over the first steps, where rounding has not yet compounded, is the meaningful check.

5. Why is the filtered estimate still good on the fresh rollout when the forecast is terrible?

Because the filter is still given the measurements, and with C = I every state component is measured. The update pulls the state back toward the truth every step regardless of how bad the prediction was. The forecast, the prediction before the update, is where the learned model is actually tested.

6. Why can a tanh network not extrapolate 3\sin x_0 + 2\cos x_1?

Every tanh unit is constant outside a bounded interval of its input, so far from the data the network's output is a constant, while the true residual keeps oscillating. The network can only represent the periodic function where it saw samples of it. A Fourier feature or a sinusoidal activation could extrapolate, but only by building the right assumption in.

7. Why does a longer training rollout not improve generalisation here?

Because on an unbounded plant a longer rollout wanders further along its own path, not across the region a fresh rollout will visit. Coverage of state space, not length, is what the network needs; the bounded plant provides it with 50 steps.

8. Why did honest noise matrices help the learned forecast?

With over-stated sensor noise the filter's state lags the measurements, so the network is trained to map a lagging state to the next measurement and learns a distorted residual. With the sensor trusted as it deserves, the state is nearly the true one and the network learns the function of the true state, which is what transfers.

Exercises

  1. Score on a fresh rollout. Train the repository's model as its script does, then generate a rollout with a new key and compute the one-step forecast RMSE of the learned filter and of the zero-residual filter.
    Hint

    forecast_with_kalman in kalman_jax/forecasters.py returns the forecasts; the zero-residual forecast is A y_{t-1} + B u_t.

    Solution

    On key 7 the learned filter's forecast RMSE is about 8.5 against 2.93 for the zero residual; keys 11 and 13 give the same picture.

    import jax, jax.numpy as jnp, optax
from jax import random
from kalman_jax.learned_dynamics import generate_data, init_mlp_params, train_learned_kalman
from kalman_jax.forecasters import forecast_with_kalman

A, B, C = jnp.array([[1., .1], [0., 1.]]), jnp.array([[0.], [.1]]), jnp.eye(2)
Q, R, x0, P0 = 0.1 * jnp.eye(2), 0.5 * jnp.eye(2), jnp.zeros(2), jnp.eye(2)
xs, us, ys = generate_data(T=50)                                    # the script's rollout (key 0)
params = init_mlp_params(random.PRNGKey(42), [3, 16, 16, 16, 16, 2])
params, _, losses = train_learned_kalman(params, optax.adam(1e-3), A=A, B=B, C=C, Q=Q, R=R,
                                         x0=x0, P0=P0, us=us, ys=ys, num_steps=1000)
xs7, us7, ys7 = generate_data(T=50, key=random.PRNGKey(7))          # a rollout it never saw
y_pred, *_ = forecast_with_kalman(params, A, B, C, Q, R, x0, P0, us7, ys7)
zero = jnp.stack([A @ ys7[t - 1] + B @ us7[t] for t in range(1, 50)])
print(jnp.sqrt(jnp.mean((y_pred - ys7) ** 2)), jnp.sqrt(jnp.mean((zero - ys7[1:]) ** 2)))   # ~8.5 vs 2.93
  2. Check the gradient yourself. Perturb one weight of the initial parameters by \pm 10^{-4}, evaluate the loss twice, and compare the central difference with the corresponding entry of jax.grad(loss_fn).
    Hint

    In float32 a step of 10^{-4} balances truncation and rounding; expect agreement to three or four digits. Enable 64-bit for more.

    Solution

    The two agree to about 10^{-3} relative in float32 and to 10^{-7} with jax_enable_x64; the page's hand-written gradient agrees with finite differences in double precision to 6 \times 10^{-7}. Disagreement at that level would point to a bug in the forward pass rather than in JAX.

  3. Let the covariance see the network. Replace the covariance prediction by P^- = (A + J_t) P (A + J_t)^\top + Q with J_t = \partial f_\theta / \partial x from jax.jacfwd. Do the gains now depend on \theta? Does the fresh-rollout forecast improve?
    Hint

    Add the Jacobian inside the scanned step; jax.grad handles the rest. Compare the gains at step 50 before and after training.

    Solution

    Yes, the gains now vary with \theta and along the trajectory (the Jacobian of 3\sin x_0 swings between \pm 3, so the predicted covariance can grow sixteen-fold in one step). The forecast on fresh rollouts does not improve: a better covariance cannot rescue a mean prediction built on extrapolated tanh units. The fix is in the data and the plant, Part 4, not in the covariance.

  4. Reproduce the table's last line. Train on the bounded plant with Q = 0.01I, R = 0.0025I and a 500-step rollout, with either loss, and score on keys 7, 11 and 13.
    Hint

    generate_data accepts A_true; pass the same stable A to the filter. The table used 2000 Adam steps; on 500 samples that takes a minute or two on a CPU.

    Solution

    Forecast RMSE 1.80 (filtered loss) or 2.01 (forecast loss) against 2.18 for the zero residual, dynamics error 1.74 and 1.85, averaged over keys 7, 11 and 13. With the repository's noise matrices instead, the same plant and data give 2.98 and 3.23: the noise matrices are worth more than the extra data.

Part 7

The chapter on one page

  1. The model: known A, B, a 914-parameter tanh network for the residual, inside a Kalman filter whose gains do not depend on the network.
  2. Training: Adam on a loss of the filter's output, gradient by reverse mode through the recursion, two adjoints per step; the page's hand-written version matches JAX to 5 \times 10^{-7} over the first twenty steps and finite differences to 6 \times 10^{-7}.
  3. Two losses: the repository's filtered loss is the forecast loss shrunk by I - K; it trains, but its value (0.012 against 0.095) is not comparable.
  4. The failure: on fresh rollouts the learned filter forecasts with RMSE 8.5 against 2.9 for no residual; the states leave the training region and the tanh network saturates.
  5. The fix: a bounded plant (eigenvalues 0.9) and honest noise matrices (Q = 0.01I, R = 0.05^2 I) with 500 steps: forecast 1.80 against 2.18, dynamics error 1.74, down from 4.3 on the repository's plant.

Part 8

Scope and further reading

Scope. This chapter covers the repository's learned-dynamics module and forecaster: a residual network in the prediction step of a linear Kalman filter with fixed gains, trained end to end. It does not cover learned covariances, variational or sampling-based state-space models [4], or networks designed to extrapolate periodic structure. Chapter 03 takes the same forecaster to a real series, where the baseline to beat is simpler and harder.

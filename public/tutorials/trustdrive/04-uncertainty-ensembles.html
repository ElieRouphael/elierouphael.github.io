Uncertainty and ensembles

A perception network that answers every question with the same confidence is dangerous. We want it to say "I don't know" when it is out of its depth. This chapter separates the two kinds of uncertainty, builds an ensemble from scratch that can express them, and checks whether its uncertainty can be trusted. Below, 25 small models learn the same curve from data between the green lines; where they have seen no data, they disagree, and that disagreement is the uncertainty. The chapter ends with the failure mode the whole course is built to catch: uncertainty is not error.

Part 1

Two uncertainties, via the law of total variance

Let a model with parameters \theta define p(y \mid x, \theta), and let p(\theta \mid D) be the posterior after seeing the data D. The predictive variance splits exactly in two, by the law of total variance:

\underbrace{\mathrm{Var}(y \mid x)}_{\text{predictive}} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_\theta\big[\mathrm{Var}(y \mid x, \theta)\big]}_{\text{aleatoric}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}_\theta\big(\mathbb{E}[y \mid x, \theta]\big)}_{\text{epistemic}}.
  • Aleatoric uncertainty is the expected noise in the data. It is irreducible: it is there even with a perfect model, and more data does not shrink it [1], [2], [3].
  • Epistemic uncertainty is the spread of the model's predictions under the posterior: the model's ignorance. It does shrink with more data, and it is largest where the training set was sparse, that is, out of distribution.

For safety, the epistemic part is the interesting one: it is what should rise when the car enters an unfamiliar regime. In the demo at the top, the aleatoric part is the thin light band that never goes away; the epistemic part is the purple flare outside the training data.

Aleatoric and epistemic

In plain words: aleatoric uncertainty is the dice: the noise in the world itself, which no amount of learning removes. Epistemic uncertainty is ignorance: what the model does not know because it has not seen enough, which more data cures.

More precisely: the two terms of the law of total variance, the expected conditional variance and the variance of the conditional mean, with the expectation and variance taken over the posterior on the parameters.

For the curious: the law of total variance in three lines (optional)

Write m(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[y \mid x, \theta]. By the tower rule, \mathbb{E}[y \mid x] = \mathbb{E}_\theta[m(\theta)] and \mathbb{E}[y^2 \mid x] = \mathbb{E}_\theta\big[\mathrm{Var}(y \mid x, \theta) + m(\theta)^2\big]. Subtracting the square of the first from the second,

\mathrm{Var}(y \mid x) = \mathbb{E}_\theta\big[\mathrm{Var}(y \mid x, \theta)\big] + \Big(\mathbb{E}_\theta\big[m(\theta)^2\big] - \mathbb{E}_\theta\big[m(\theta)\big]^2\Big),

and the bracket is \mathrm{Var}_\theta(m(\theta)). No approximation is involved: the split is exact for any model and any posterior.

Part 2

The Bayesian predictive, and why ensembles approximate it

What we actually want is the posterior predictive, which averages over all plausible parameters:

p(y \mid x, D) = \int p(y \mid x, \theta)\,p(\theta \mid D)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

For a neural network, p(\theta \mid D) is intractable, so we approximate it: by a Laplace approximation [4], Monte Carlo dropout [5], variational inference [6], or a deep ensemble [7]. A deep ensemble trains M models from different random initialisations and/or resamples of the data, and treats the resulting parameters \{\theta_i\} as samples from the posterior:

p(y \mid x, D) \approx \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} p(y \mid x, \theta_i).

Empirically, ensembles are among the strongest uncertainty methods. Independent training runs land in different modes of the loss landscape, which gives genuine diversity between the functions they compute, rather than small wiggles around one mode [7], [8].

Part 3

The estimator

With M member predictions \hat y_i(x), each optionally also predicting a noise variance \hat\sigma_i^2(x) for the aleatoric part, the ensemble reports

\bar y(x) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_i \hat y_i(x), \qquad \Sigma^{\text{epi}}(x) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_i \big(\hat y_i(x) - \bar y(x)\big)\big(\hat y_i(x) - \bar y(x)\big)^\top,

and the total predictive variance follows the decomposition of Part 1:

\sigma^2_{\text{tot}}(x) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{M}\sum_i \hat\sigma_i^2(x)}_{\text{aleatoric}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}_i\big(\hat y_i(x)\big)}_{\text{epistemic}}.

In the driving system, \Sigma^{\text{epi}} is exactly the perception covariance \Sigma^{\text{AI}} whose trace feeds the integrity score of chapter 06. The notebook builds one from scratch, with random-feature ridge regressions as cheap "networks" [12]: the diversity comes from different random features for each member, plus a bootstrap resample of the data. It estimates the aleatoric part from the residuals on held-out data.

def features(D, seed, scale=1.5):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    Wf, bf = r.normal(0, scale, (D, 1)), r.uniform(0, 2*np.pi, D)
    return lambda x: np.cos(x.reshape(-1, 1) @ Wf.T + bf)

def fit_member(phi, X, Y, lam=1e-2):
    P = phi(X); w = np.linalg.solve(P.T@P + lam*np.eye(P.shape[1]), P.T@Y)
    return lambda x: phi(x) @ w

M = 25
members = []
for s in range(M):
    phi = features(200, seed=1000 + s)            # init diversity: different random features
    idx = rng.integers(0, len(Xtr), len(Xtr))     # data diversity: bootstrap resample
    members.append(fit_member(phi, Xtr[idx], Ytr[idx]))

epistemic std  in [-2,2]        = 0.017
epistemic std  in |x|>3 (OOD)   = 1.314

Part 4

Is the uncertainty calibrated?

An uncertainty is only useful if it is calibrated: a claimed 95% interval should contain the truth about 95% of the time [9], [10]. For regression, check it with the standardised residual z = \frac{y - \bar y}{\sigma_{\text{tot}}}. If the predictive Gaussian is calibrated, z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), so the fraction of points with |z| \le t should match the nominal 2\Phi(t) - 1 for every threshold t. Plotting one against the other gives a reliability curve; the diagonal is perfect. This one uses the ensemble trained at the top, with fresh points inside the data and far outside it:

In the data, the ensemble is well calibrated. Far from it, its intervals are too wide (coverage 1.00 instead of 0.95): it is conservative, which is the safe direction. The single model, which reports only the aleatoric noise, is badly over-confident wherever it is wrong, and the negative log-likelihood punishes that heavily.

For the curious: why the negative log-likelihood is a fair judge (optional)

For a Gaussian prediction with mean \mu and variance \sigma^2, the negative log-likelihood of an outcome y is

\text{NLL} = \frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) + \frac{(y - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}.

The first term punishes vagueness, the second over-confidence. If the outcome really has variance s^2, the expected score is minimised exactly at \sigma^2 = s^2: honesty is optimal. Scores with this property are called strictly proper [11]. A model cannot improve its NLL by claiming more or less certainty than it has, which is why it is used to compare uncertainty estimates.

Part 5

The failure mode: uncertainty is not error

The ensemble above widened where it was ignorant. That is the good case, and it is why the epistemic trace is a useful signal. But miscalibration can go either way, and only one direction is dangerous.

An ensemble only disagrees where its members can disagree. If they share a limiting assumption, too small a family of functions or the same blind spot, they agree and are all wrong: low variance, high error. The uncertainty then silently underestimates the error, and no reliability check on familiar data would reveal it.

Below, each member gets only a handful of random features. When all members share the same features, only their bootstrap resamples differ: they agree closely, the reported band is tight, and the fit is biased. Give each member its own features, with the same small number, and the disagreement returns.

actual error |\bar y - f| reported 2\sigma

With 3 shared features, the actual error exceeds the reported 2σ band over roughly 90% of the training range: the confidently-wrong regime. The notebook runs the same experiment:

phi_shared = features(3, seed=7)                  # too few features, shared by all members
weak = []
for _ in range(25):
    idx = rng.integers(0, len(Xtr), len(Xtr))     # bootstrap: inputs and labels resampled together
    weak.append(fit_member(phi_shared, Xtr[idx], Ytr[idx]))
pw = np.stack([m(xs) for m in weak]); muw, stdw = pw.mean(0), pw.std(0)
errw = np.abs(muw - f(xs))
mask = (xs > -2) & (xs < 2)
over = 100*np.mean(errw[mask] > 2*stdw[mask])
in-domain fraction where actual error exceeds the 2-sigma band: 89%

With 3 features drawn independently for each member, the members disagree, and the band covers the error almost everywhere. The size of the model family was the same; what changed was whether the members could fail differently.

Part 6

Consequences for the driving system

The trace of \Sigma^{\text{epi}} is a genuinely useful signal: it rises under blur, darkness and sparse data, and it feeds the integrity score. But Part 5 is the warning. On a truly novel regime, such as the out-of-distribution bend of chapter 03, which even looks like a valid image, an over-confident perception network can report a small covariance while being biased. No amount of introspective uncertainty fixes this, because the model is judging itself.

A model cannot be the only judge of its own errors.

The remedy is an external, model-based cross-check: compare the perception's output with a physics prediction and test whether the two are consistent. That is the Kalman innovation test of chapter 05, and it is precisely what catches the case where uncertainty alone stays quiet.

Part 7

The chapter on one page

  1. Two uncertainties: predictive variance = expected noise (aleatoric) + spread of the model's predictions (epistemic), exactly.
  2. Ensembles approximate the posterior predictive with M independently trained models.
  3. Estimator: the mean of the members, the spread around it (their covariance, divided by M), and the held-out residual for the noise.
  4. Calibration: a 95% interval should cover 95%; reliability curves and the negative log-likelihood check it.
  5. The failure mode: members with a shared blind spot agree and are all wrong; the uncertainty stays small while the error is large.
  6. The remedy: an external check against a physics model, chapter 05's innovation test.

Part 8

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

  1. The law of total variance, numerically. Estimate both terms from the ensemble and confirm that they add up to the empirical predictive variance inside the data.
    Hint

    To sample from the ensemble's predictive at a point x: pick a member at random, take its prediction, add Gaussian noise with the aleatoric variance. Then compare the variance of many such samples with the sum of the two terms.

    Solution

    With the notebook's ensemble and 200,000 samples, at x = 0 the empirical predictive variance is 0.01029, against 0.00993 (aleatoric) + 0.00022 (epistemic) = 0.01015; at x = -2, 0.01208 against 0.00993 + 0.00199 = 0.01192; at x = 2, 0.01098 against 0.01077. The agreement is within the Monte Carlo error. Inside the data, the aleatoric term dominates: the members agree far more closely than the labels vary. Towards the edges of the data, the epistemic term grows several times larger than in the middle: nine times at x = -2.

  2. How many members? Grow M from 2 to 50 and plot how the epistemic estimate stabilises. How many members are "enough" here?
    Hint

    Train 50 members once and use the first M. Look at the average epistemic standard deviation inside the data and far outside it separately. The M slider of the demo does the same.

    Solution

    Inside the data, the estimate is already about right with very few members (0.016 with 2) and is stable at 0.017 from about 10 members. Far from the data, it is much noisier: 1.70 with 2 members, 2.34 with 3, 1.82 with 10, then between 1.64 and 1.75 from 15 to 50. So 10 to 20 members are enough for the purpose here, flagging that the model is outside its data, but not for a precise value of the uncertainty far out, where each member extrapolates wildly. The notebook's own 25 members gave 1.31 far out, another reminder that this number depends heavily on the draw.

  3. Temperature scaling. Recalibrate the intervals with a single temperature \sigma \mapsto \tau\sigma, chosen on a validation set. Does one global \tau fix the coverage both inside and outside the data? Why not?
    Hint

    Choose \tau so that 95% of the validation points have |z| \le 1.96\tau. Then try the value that would fix the far region instead.

    Solution

    No. Validation data come from the training range, where the ensemble is already calibrated, so the chosen temperature is close to 1 (0.97): the coverage stays at 0.94 inside and 1.00 outside. To bring the far region to 95%, the intervals there must shrink to \tau = 0.71, but the same \tau drops the coverage inside the data to 0.84. The miscalibration depends on the input, too wide far away and right inside, so no single number can correct both. Input-dependent recalibration [10] or better uncertainty models are needed, and there are no labels for the regions where it matters most.

  4. Monte Carlo dropout. Replace the ensemble with Monte Carlo dropout on a small multilayer perceptron, if you have PyTorch, and compare the reliability curves.
    Hint

    Keep dropout active at test time and run, say, 50 forward passes per input; their mean and variance replace the ensemble's.

    Solution

    What to expect: inside the data the two give similar reliability curves. Away from the data, Monte Carlo dropout's samples all come from one trained network, perturbed, so they tend to disagree less than independently trained members, and the intervals widen less. Published comparisons generally find ensembles better calibrated, particularly out of distribution [7]. The exact curves depend on the architecture, the dropout rate and the training, so this solution doesn't quote any numbers.

LQR lane keeping

Chapter 01 built the car; this chapter steers it. The controller looks at how far the car is from the lane centre and how far its heading is off, and turns the wheels by a fixed combination of the two. Which combination? The linear-quadratic regulator gives the best one for a chosen balance between tracking the lane and steering effort, and this chapter derives it from scratch. Below, the car starts half a metre off-centre and then meets a bend. Change how much steering costs, the speed, and whether the controller knows about the bend in advance.

Part 1

The regulation problem and a quadratic cost

We reuse the linear lane-keeping error model of chapter 01 [9], x_{k+1} = A x_k + B\,\delta_k + E\,\kappa_k, with

A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & vT_s \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \frac{vT_s}{L} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad E = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -vT_s \end{bmatrix}.

We want to drive the state x = [e_y,\ e_\psi]^\top to zero with limited steering. Encode this as an infinite-horizon quadratic cost:

J = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \big(x_k^\top Q\,x_k + \delta_k^\top R\,\delta_k\big), \qquad Q = Q^\top \succeq 0, \quad R = R^\top \succ 0.

Q prices the tracking error, how quickly to return to the lane, and R prices the control, how hard to steer. The quadratic form is not arbitrary: for a linear plant it makes the problem convex and yields a linear optimal feedback with a closed-form solution [1], [2]. A larger R relative to Q gives gentler steering; a larger Q gives tighter tracking. Part 4 makes that trade-off quantitative.

What is a linear-quadratic regulator?

In plain words: the steering rule that keeps the car closest to the lane centre for the least steering effort, when "close" and "effort" are both measured by squares. It turns out to be very simple: steer by a fixed multiple of the offset plus a fixed multiple of the heading error.

More precisely: for a linear system and a quadratic cost summed over an infinite horizon, the optimal control is a linear state feedback \delta_k = -Kx_k, with the gain K obtained from the solution of a Riccati equation.

Regulator
A controller that drives the state back to zero.
Weights Q, R
How much each squared error, and each squared steering command, costs.
Gain K
The fixed row of numbers multiplying the state to give the steering.
Cost-to-go
The cost still to come from a given state, if you act optimally from now on.
Riccati equation
The matrix equation whose solution gives the cost-to-go, and from it the gain.
Part 2

Deriving LQR by dynamic programming

Take the finite-horizon version, with a terminal weight P_N = Q_f, and apply Bellman's principle of optimality: whatever the first step, the rest of an optimal plan must be optimal from where that step lands. Guess that the optimal cost-to-go from state x at step k is quadratic, V_k(x) = x^\top P_k x. The Bellman equation is

V_k(x) = \min_{\delta} \Big[\, x^\top Q x + \delta^\top R\,\delta + V_{k+1}(Ax + B\delta) \,\Big].

Substituting V_{k+1}(x') = x'^\top P_{k+1} x', the bracket is quadratic and convex in \delta (its Hessian R + B^\top P_{k+1} B is positive definite). Setting the gradient to zero gives a linear optimal control \delta_k = -K_k x_k, with

K_k = (R + B^\top P_{k+1} B)^{-1} B^\top P_{k+1} A.

Substituting back produces the backward Riccati recursion:

P_k = Q + A^\top P_{k+1} A - A^\top P_{k+1} B\,(R + B^\top P_{k+1} B)^{-1} B^\top P_{k+1} A.

As the horizon grows, the sequence P_k converges, under the conditions of Part 3, to a constant P. The control becomes the stationary feedback \delta_k = -Kx_k, and P solves the algebraic equation of Part 3. Full derivations are in [2], [3]. The widget at the end of Part 3 runs this recursion for any weights.

# backward Riccati recursion from a terminal cost P_N = Q
P = Q.copy(); gains = []
for _ in range(120):
    K = np.linalg.solve(R + B.T@P@B, B.T@P@A)
    gains.append(K.ravel().copy())
    P = Q + A.T@P@A - A.T@P@B@K
...
print("stationary gain K =", gains[-1])

After 120 backward steps the gain has settled, to every printed digit, on the fixed point that Part 3 computes directly.

For the curious: the minimisation, step by step (optional)

Write P for P_{k+1} and expand the bracket as a function of \delta:

f(\delta) = x^\top (Q + A^\top P A)\,x + 2\,\delta^\top B^\top P A\,x + \delta^\top (R + B^\top P B)\,\delta.

Its gradient is 2B^\top P A\,x + 2(R + B^\top P B)\,\delta. Setting it to zero gives \delta = -(R + B^\top P B)^{-1} B^\top P A\,x. Because R is positive definite and P positive semidefinite, R + B^\top P B is positive definite: f is strictly convex, this stationary point is its unique minimum, and the inverse exists. Putting the minimiser back into f leaves a quadratic form in x, whose matrix is the right-hand side of the Riccati recursion.

Part 3

The discrete algebraic Riccati equation

The stationary P is the symmetric, positive-semidefinite solution of the discrete algebraic Riccati equation (DARE):

\boxed{\;P = Q + A^\top P A - A^\top P B\,(R + B^\top P B)^{-1} B^\top P A\;} \qquad K = (R + B^\top P B)^{-1} B^\top P A.

When does a good solution exist? If (A, B) is stabilisable and (A, Q^{1/2}) is detectable, the DARE has a unique symmetric positive-semidefinite solution for which the closed loop A - BK is Schur stable: all its eigenvalues lie strictly inside the unit circle [2], [5]. Our (A, B) is controllable (chapter 01), and Q = \mathrm{diag}(1, 5) is positive definite, so (A, Q^{1/2}) is trivially detectable: both conditions hold.

How real solvers do it. Production code uses a numerically robust Schur method on the associated Hamiltonian pencil [8], for example scipy.linalg.solve_discrete_are. To keep the course free of dependencies, the notebook iterates the recursion to its fixed point and verifies the result by checking the residual of the DARE:

def dlqr(A, B, Q, R, iters=1000, tol=1e-14):
    P = Q.copy()
    for _ in range(iters):
        Pn = Q + A.T@P@A - A.T@P@B@np.linalg.solve(R + B.T@P@B, B.T@P@A)
        if np.max(np.abs(Pn - P)) < tol:
            P = Pn; break
        P = Pn
    K = np.linalg.solve(R + B.T@P@B, B.T@P@A)
    return K, P

K, P = dlqr(A, B, Q, R)
residual = Q + A.T@P@A - A.T@P@B@np.linalg.solve(R + B.T@P@B, B.T@P@A) - P

Part 4

Choosing Q and R

A practical starting point is Bryson's rule [4]: make each term of the cost of order one by dividing by the largest acceptable value of each variable,

Q_{ii} = \frac{1}{x_{i,\max}^2}, \qquad R_{jj} = \frac{1}{\delta_{\max}^2},

then tune by hand. Because only the ratio of Q to R matters, sweeping R traces a frontier between tracking accuracy and control effort: the fundamental trade-off of LQR. Each point below is a fully re-solved controller, simulated on the same manoeuvre: recover from a 0.5 m offset on a straight road, for 10 seconds.

Neither end is "right". Steering seven times harder buys about 40% less tracking error. The course picks R = 8, in the knee of the curve, where a little more effort no longer buys much accuracy.

Part 5

Feed-forward: a two-degree-of-freedom controller

Pure feedback leaves a steady offset on a bend. Holding a bend needs a non-zero steering angle, and a controller driven by the error only produces one once an error exists. For a constant curvature \kappa, the linear closed loop settles at

x_{ss} = \big(I - (A - BK)\big)^{-1} E\,\kappa,

which is not zero. We cancel it with a feed-forward term computed from the road geometry. Setting the curvature contribution to \dot e_\psi to zero,

\frac{v}{L}\tan\delta_{ff} = v\,\kappa \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \delta_{ff} = \arctan(L\kappa),

and the command becomes \delta = \delta_{ff} - Kx. This is a two-degree-of-freedom design: feedback shapes the transient and rejects disturbances, while feed-forward handles the known geometry. Because \delta_{ff} comes from the map, not the camera, it stays valid in the full system even when the perception cannot be trusted and authority shifts to the driver (chapter 07).

feedback only (dashed) feedback + feed-forward predicted offset (dotted) 

feedback only: predicted steady-state e_y = -0.286 m
For the curious: the offset in one line (optional)

In a steady state the heading error must be zero, or the offset would keep changing. Then the yaw rate must match the road's, so \tan\delta = L\kappa, and with feedback only \delta = -K_1 e_y. Hence

e_{y,ss} = -\frac{\arctan(L\kappa)}{K_1} \approx -\frac{L\kappa}{K_1} = -\frac{2.7 \times 0.03}{0.2834} \approx -0.286 \text{ m}.

The offset grows with the curvature and shrinks with the gain K_1. A stiffer controller (smaller R) holds the bend more tightly, but the offset never vanishes with feedback alone; feed-forward removes it entirely. The nonlinear simulation settles at -\frac{\arctan(L\kappa)}{K_1} = -0.285 m, a millimetre from the linear prediction.

Part 6

Gain scheduling: a gain for every speed

A and B depend on the speed v, so a single gain is optimal at one speed only. Since the integrity monitor will later slow the car down when trust is low (chapter 07), we recompute K(v) for each speed: a gain-scheduled, or linear-parameter-varying, controller [7], with the speed as the scheduling parameter.

The scheduled design keeps the closed loop comfortably stable over the whole range of speeds: the spectral radius stays between 0.69 and 0.93, well below 1. It is largest at low speed, where less happens in each 0.05 s step, so each step removes less of the error. Notice also how little the gains change, and how close the fixed gain stays to the scheduled one: for this plant, at these speeds, scheduling is a refinement rather than a necessity (exercise 3).

Part 7

Two classical facts worth knowing

Robustness. Full-state LQR is not only optimal, it is robust. In continuous time, the regulator has a guaranteed gain margin from \frac{1}{2} to infinity and a phase margin of at least 60° at each input, from the return-difference inequality [2], [6]. In plain terms: the steering could turn out to be as little as half as effective as the model assumes, or any amount more effective, and the loop would stay stable. The discrete-time picture is similar, but the clean margins are the continuous-time result. This is part of why LQR is such a strong baseline.

We don't actually measure the state. LQR assumes it knows x. In the full system, the controller sees only the noisy estimate from the perception, so we pair LQR with a state estimator: the Kalman filter of chapter 05.

The separation principle

In plain words: design the best controller as if you could see the state, design the best estimator as if nobody were controlling, then plug one into the other. For linear systems with Gaussian noise and quadratic costs, nothing better exists.

More precisely: the optimal linear-quadratic-Gaussian (LQG) controller applies the LQR gain to the Kalman filter's estimate, \delta_k = -K\hat x_k, and the two can be designed independently [2]. The guarantee is about optimality only: the robustness margins of full-state LQR do not automatically carry over once an estimator is in the loop.

Part 8

The chapter on one page

  1. Cost: penalise squared errors with Q and squared steering with R, summed over all future steps.
  2. Dynamic programming: a quadratic cost-to-go gives a linear optimal control, \delta = -Kx, and a backward Riccati recursion.
  3. DARE: its fixed point gives the stationary gain, and makes A - BK stable; the course's gain is K = [0.283,\ 1.500].
  4. Weights: start from Bryson's rule; sweeping R traces the frontier between tracking and effort.
  5. Feed-forward \arctan(L\kappa) removes the steady offset on a bend that feedback alone leaves.
  6. Gain scheduling re-solves K for each speed; LQR is robust, and pairs with a Kalman filter by the separation principle.

Part 9

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

  1. The gain from the gradient. Re-derive K_k by setting the gradient of the right-hand side of the Bellman equation to zero. Where is convexity, and hence a unique minimiser, used?
    Hint

    Expand (Ax + B\delta)^\top P_{k+1}(Ax + B\delta) and collect the terms in \delta. The optional box of Part 2 does the first step.

    Solution

    The bracket is f(\delta) = \text{const} + 2\delta^\top B^\top P A x + \delta^\top (R + B^\top P B)\delta, with gradient 2B^\top P A x + 2(R + B^\top P B)\delta. Setting it to zero gives \delta = -(R + B^\top P B)^{-1} B^\top P A\,x = -K_k x. Convexity is used twice: R positive definite and P_{k+1} positive semidefinite make R + B^\top P B positive definite, so the matrix can be inverted, and the zero of the gradient is the unique global minimum rather than a saddle or a maximum. If R were only semidefinite, steering could be free, and the minimiser could fail to exist or be unique.

  2. Check against scipy. If scipy is available, replace the Riccati iteration with scipy.linalg.solve_discrete_are and confirm that the gains match to machine precision.
    Hint

    P = solve_discrete_are(A, B, Q, R), then the same formula for K.

    Solution 
    from scipy.linalg import solve_discrete_are
Ps = solve_discrete_are(A, B, Q, R)
Ks = np.linalg.solve(R + B.T@Ps@B, B.T@Ps@A)

    The gains agree to 4e-15 and the matrices P to 2e-13: identical to machine precision. The iteration needs about 85 steps to reach the notebook's tolerance of 1e-14, so it is cheap here; the Schur method matters for large or badly conditioned problems, where the iteration converges slowly.

  3. The wrong speed. Apply the gain designed at v = 15 m/s at v = 6 m/s, without rescheduling. Does the loop stay stable? Compare its poles with those of the scheduled design.
    Hint

    Build A and B at 6 m/s, keep the old K, and compute the eigenvalues of A - BK. Then re-solve the DARE at 6 m/s and compare. The widget of Part 6 shows the spectral radius of both.

    Solution

    It stays stable. With the fixed gain, the poles at 6 m/s are 0.917 \pm 0.050j (spectral radius 0.918); with the gain re-solved for 6 m/s, K = [0.324,\ 1.556], they are 0.914 \pm 0.058j (0.915). The difference is small: the scheduled loop recovers from a 0.5 m offset to within 5 cm in 1.45 s, the fixed one in 1.60 s. Both are much slower than at 15 m/s (0.60 s), because at low speed a heading error turns into an offset slowly, whatever the gain. The fixed gain even stays stable up to 40 m/s (spectral radius 0.56). Scheduling here is a refinement; it would matter for a plant whose dynamics change more with the parameter.

  4. Bryson's rule. Use Bryson's rule with e_{y,\max} = 0.5 m, e_{\psi,\max} = 0.1 rad and \delta_{\max} = 0.2 rad to choose Q and R, and compare the resulting gain with the default.
    Hint

    One over each maximum squared. Only the ratios to R matter, so compare \frac{Q_{11}}{R} and \frac{Q_{22}}{R} with the default's.

    Solution

    Q = \mathrm{diag}(4, 100) and R = 25. Relative to R, the offset weight is about the same as the default (0.16 against 0.125), but the heading weight is more than six times larger (4 against 0.625). The gain becomes K = [0.284,\ 1.995]: the same response to the offset, a stronger one to the heading error. The poles move to 0.855 and 0.590, real numbers, so the response no longer overshoots. On the recovery manoeuvre the RMS offset rises slightly, from 0.081 to 0.086 m, and the steering effort falls from 0.0132 to 0.0117 rad: a gentler, better-damped controller, close to the default on the cost frontier.

Shared human-vehicle control

Chapter 06 produced one number, the integrity score I. This chapter turns it into action. Instead of a brittle all-or-nothing handover, the driver's and the automation's steering are blended, with an authority that follows integrity. Below, the two disagree: one of them is mistaken, and the integrity score is supposed to know which. Set the integrity and the driver's workload, swap who is wrong, and watch whose steering wins.

Part 1

Levels of automation and the handover problem

Partial automation, levels 2 and 3 of the SAE scale [1], keeps the human in a supervisory role. That is exactly where humans are worst. Asking a person who is out of the loop to resume control takes seconds and is error-prone [2], [3]. A hard handover, in which the automation drops out and the human must take over at once, inherits all of that risk. It also throws away the automation's contribution the moment it is flagged. The alternative is to keep both agents in the loop and shift authority smoothly.

Part 2

Haptic shared control

In haptic shared control, the driver and the automation act on the same control channel at the same time, and their commands are blended rather than switched [4], [5], [6]. The course uses an input-mixing form on the steering:

\boxed{\;\delta = \delta^{\text{ff}} + \lambda\,\delta_A + (1 - \lambda)\,\delta_H\;} \qquad \lambda \in [0, 1],

where \lambda is the automation authority: \lambda = 1 is full automation, \lambda = 0 is the driver alone, plus the map's feed-forward \delta^{\text{ff}} of chapter 02, which always applies. Blending means neither agent is ever fully out of the loop, the transition is continuous, and the driver feels the automation's intent through the shared actuator [4]. The design question is how to set \lambda. The course ties it to integrity.

What is shared control?

In plain words: the driver and the automation both hold the wheel. Each pushes the way it thinks is right, and the car follows a weighted mix of the two. The weights shift towards whichever is more trustworthy at the moment.

More precisely: a control architecture in which human and automatic controllers act continuously on the same actuator, with the authority between them set by a law rather than by a discrete switch [4], [6].

Authority \lambda
The automation's weight in the blend; the driver gets 1 - \lambda.

Part 3

A model of the driver: the crossover idea

To reason about the loop, we need a model of the human. The classic result is McRuer's crossover model [7]. A well-trained operator adapts their own dynamics so that, near the crossover frequency \omega_c, the open loop of human and vehicle together behaves like

Y_{\text{OL}}(j\omega) \approx \frac{\omega_c\,e^{-j\omega\tau}}{j\omega},

an integrator with an effective reaction delay \tau of roughly 0.2 to 0.4 s, from neuromuscular and processing lags. The delay is the crux. It erodes the phase margin, so a human with too much delay, or too much gain, goes unstable. The course's driver is a minimal realisation of this, a delayed, noisy proportional controller on the lane error:

\delta_H = -\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{2}W\big)\,K_h^\top x_{k-\tau} + w_k, \qquad w_k \sim \mathcal{N}\big(0, (\sigma_h(1 + 2W))^2\big),

with gains K_h = (0.12, 0.9), a delay of 4 steps (0.2 s), a noise of \sigma_h = 0.02 rad, and W the workload of Part 4. The automation is chapter 02's LQR, K_A = (0.2834, 1.5001), with no delay. The driver is meant to be a viable fallback, clearly worse than the automation. Is the delay really what limits it? The stability of a loop with a delay is analysed on an augmented state, the current state and its \tau predecessors, whose spectral radius must stay below 1. Change the delay and the driver's gain:

def blended_poles(lam, tau):
    # spectral radius of the delayed blended closed loop (augmented state)
    n, d = 2, tau + 1
    Z = np.zeros((n*d, n*d))
    Z[0:n, 0:n] = A - lam*np.outer(Bv, KA)                     # automation acts on x_k
    Z[0:n, n*tau:n*tau+n] += -(1 - lam)*np.outer(Bv, KH)       # human acts on x_{k-tau}
    for j in range(1, d):
        Z[n*j:n*j+n, n*(j-1):n*(j-1)+n] = np.eye(n)            # delay shift register
    return np.max(np.abs(np.linalg.eigvals(Z)))
human-only stable up to 0.20 s of delay, unstable from 0.25 s; our driver uses tau=4 (0.20 s), spectral radius 0.996

The driver alone is stable up to exactly the delay it is given, and only just. Its spectral radius there is 0.996, so its slowest mode takes 12 s to decay by a factor e. From a 0.5 m offset, it overshoots to 0.23 m on the other side and still has 5 cm to go after 20 s. The automation, at 0.80, is back within 1 cm in 0.75 s. So the course's driver is stabilising, but barely: a viable fallback, not a good one.

The blended loop, on the other hand, is stable for every authority, at the driver's 0.2 s delay. Its spectral radius is largest, 0.996, at \lambda = 0 (the driver alone), and falls to 0.77 near \lambda = 0.84.

For the curious: the crossover model predicts the critical delay (optional)

At the crossover frequency, where the open loop has gain 1, the crossover model's phase is -\frac{\pi}{2} - \omega_c\tau, so its phase margin is \frac{\pi}{2} - \omega_c\tau. The loop goes unstable when the delay eats the margin. More generally, a loop with phase margin \varphi_m at \omega_c tolerates an extra delay of about

\tau_{\text{crit}} \approx \frac{\varphi_m}{\omega_c}.

For the course's driver without delay, the open loop K_h^\top (zI - A)^{-1} B crosses over at \omega_c = 5.14 rad/s with a margin of 60.5°, which a delay of 0.205 s uses up: right between the last stable delay (0.20 s) and the first unstable one (0.25 s). A higher gain raises \omega_c and shrinks the tolerable delay (exercise 1): the gain-against-phase trade that a human operator manages by adapting [7].

Part 4

Workload and performance

Human performance is not fixed. It follows the Yerkes-Dodson inverted U [8]: too little or too much arousal degrades it. Under high workload, a driver's corrections get noisier and less forceful, and taking over is slower. The course folds a scalar workload W \in [0, 1] into the driver model (think of a normalised NASA-TLX score [10]). It inflates the steering noise, to \sigma_h(1 + 2W), and attenuates the gain, by 1 - \frac{1}{2}W. The model keeps only the high-workload side of the inverted U: performance only worsens as W grows.

Workload-adaptive haptic control hands more to the automation when the driver is loaded, provided the automation is trustworthy. It is an active research direction [9], and the authority law below does a version of it. Note one side effect of the weaker gain: it makes the delayed driver more stable. The blended loop's largest spectral radius falls from 0.996 at W = 0 to 0.97 at W = 0.5.

Part 5

The authority law and the operating envelope

The course sets the authority from the integrity I and the workload W:

\boxed{\;\lambda = \mathrm{clip}\big(I + \rho\,W\,(I - 0.5),\ \lambda_{\min},\ \lambda_{\max}\big)\;} \qquad \rho = 0.35, \quad [\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}] = [0.05, 0.95].

Three design choices are encoded here. First, authority rises with integrity: trust the automation when the monitor says it is trustworthy. Second, the workload term is signed about I = 0.5, so workload makes the arbitration more decisive. With decent integrity, a loaded driver hands a little more to the automation. With poor integrity, the automation, now the less trustworthy agent, hands a little more to the driver, loaded or not. Third, the clip keeps both agents partly in the loop at all times. That is not needed for stability, since Part 3 found the blended loop stable for every \lambda, ends included. It is about the people: a driver who never steers drifts out of the loop, and an automation reduced to nothing cannot help when the driver errs. The course's code adds a fourth rule: when the driver is not available, the authority is at least 0.85.

When integrity drops, the law also contracts the operating envelope, a form of graceful degradation towards a minimal-risk condition [1], by lowering the reference speed:

v_{\text{ref}} = v_{\text{nom}}\,(0.4 + 0.6\,I).

So when neither the perception nor the driver is ideal, the car slows down instead of gambling on one of them.

Authority rises with integrity

Speed contracts under low trust

with this workload no workload 

def authority(I, W, avail=True, rho=0.35, lo=0.05, hi=0.95):
    lam = I + rho*W*(I - 0.5)
    if not avail: lam = max(lam, 0.85)
    return np.clip(lam, lo, hi)
Part 6

Conflict: who should win, and why integrity decides

The hardest case is a conflict: the automation steers one way, the driver the other. A fixed authority cannot be right, because the correct winner depends on who is actually correct, and that is precisely what integrity estimates. When the perception is trustworthy (I high, so \lambda high), the automation should win against a mistaken driver. When the perception is out of its domain (I low, so \lambda low), the driver should win. The notebook simulates both, as the demo at the top does:

if case == "human_wrong":                       # perception good; driver steers to a wrong target
    dA = -KA @ x                                 # automation on the true state (correct)
    dH = -KH @ (xd - np.array([0.6, 0.0]))       # driver holds a wrong lane offset
else:                                            # "auto_wrong": perception biased; driver correct
    dA = -KA @ (x + np.array([0.6, 0.0]))        # automation on a biased estimate
    dH = -KH @ xd                                # driver on the true state (correct)
d = np.clip(lam*dA + (1 - lam)*dH, -0.6, 0.6)
x = A @ x + Bv*d

Where does the car settle? In a steady state the steering is zero and so is the heading error, so the two agents' pulls on the offset must cancel. That gives a simple, exact answer:

e_{y,ss} = (1 - w)\,b_H - w\,b_A, \qquad w = \frac{\lambda k_A}{\lambda k_A + (1 - \lambda)\,k_H},

where b_H is the offset the driver wrongly holds, b_A the perception's bias, and k_A = 0.2834 and k_H = 0.12 the two agents' gains on the offset. The car ends up at a weighted mix of the two agents' targets, and the weight w, the automation's effective share, is not \lambda. The automation's gain is more than twice the driver's, so it pulls harder: at \lambda = 0.3 it already has half the say.

perception good, driver mistaken perception mistaken, driver correct

The two curves cross: high authority is right when the perception is good, low authority is right when it is not, and no single \lambda minimises both. Because the law sets \lambda from I, which is high exactly when the perception is trustworthy (chapter 06) and low when it is out of its domain, the adaptive authority lands near the minimum of whichever curve is active. That is integrity-aware arbitration. Note the asymmetry the effective share creates. At high integrity (\lambda = 0.9), a mistaken driver moves the car by 2.7 cm. At low integrity (\lambda = 0.1), a mistaken perception still moves it by 12.5 cm, because a tenth of the authority is a fifth of the say.

The right winner of a conflict depends on who is right, and integrity is the system's estimate of exactly that.

It is only an estimate, though. In the demo at the top, choose "perception mistaken" and leave the integrity high: the monitor has been fooled, and the car follows the biased perception. The arbitration is only as good as the integrity score that drives it.

Part 7

Putting it together

This completes the loop. The vehicle (chapter 01) is driven by an LQR (02) reading a state estimate. When the state comes from the perception (03), with its uncertainty (04), a Kalman monitor (05) tests consistency, and an integrity score (06) fuses the evidence. That score sets the shared authority and the speed (07). Chapter 08 runs the whole thing in closed loop, and compares it with the naive and hard-handover baselines across four scenarios.

Part 8

The chapter on one page

  1. Handover: an out-of-the-loop human takes seconds to resume control; a hard switch inherits that risk.
  2. Shared control: \delta = \delta^{\text{ff}} + \lambda\,\delta_A + (1 - \lambda)\,\delta_H, both agents always in the loop.
  3. Driver model: a delayed, noisy proportional controller; the delay limits it, as the crossover model predicts (\tau_{\text{crit}} \approx \frac{\varphi_m}{\omega_c}).
  4. Authority law: \lambda = \mathrm{clip}(I + \rho W (I - 0.5), 0.05, 0.95), and v_{\text{ref}} = v_{\text{nom}}(0.4 + 0.6 I).
  5. Conflict: the car settles at a mix of the two agents' targets, weighted by the effective share, not by \lambda.
  6. Integrity decides: no fixed authority wins both conflicts; one that follows integrity lands on the right side of each.

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

    The course's driver is stable up to 0.20 s and unstable from 0.25 s. Tripling its gain cuts the tolerable delay to under 0.1 s. The margin changes little, between 50° and 64°, but the crossover frequency rises with the gain, and a given delay costs a phase of \omega_c\tau, more at higher frequency. The estimate \frac{\varphi_m}{\omega_c} always falls between the last stable and the first unstable delay of the discrete model. This is why a skilled human operator lowers their gain when their delay grows: an aggressive driver with slow reactions oscillates.

  2. Feed-forward on a bend. Add the map's feed-forward \delta^{\text{ff}} = \arctan(L\kappa) to the conflict simulation on a curve, and confirm that it removes the steady offset whatever \lambda is (chapter 02).
    Hint

    Add the curvature term E\kappa, with E = (0, -vT_s), to the dynamics, and the feed-forward outside the blend. In a steady state, the total steering must equal L\kappa.

    Solution

    On a bend with \kappa = 0.012 and both agents correct, the feedback alone must supply the steering L\kappa = 0.032 rad, which takes a standing offset of -\frac{L\kappa}{\lambda k_A + (1 - \lambda)\,k_H}. That is -0.25 m at \lambda = 0.05, -0.16 m at 0.5 and -0.12 m at 0.95: it depends on the authority, because the two agents have different gains. With the feed-forward, the offset is below 0.1 mm at every \lambda: the feed-forward supplies the steering, and the feedback has nothing left to do. (The tiny remainder is the difference between \arctan(L\kappa) and the linear model's L\kappa.) In the two conflicts, the feed-forward removes the bend's contribution and leaves exactly the straight-road errors: 2.7 cm and 57 cm at \lambda = 0.9, 48 cm and 12 cm at \lambda = 0.1. So the feed-forward makes the arbitration a matter of who is right, not of who steers harder on a bend.

  3. A smooth saturation. Replace the hard clip on \lambda with a smooth saturation, and check that the blended loop stays stable across \lambda.
    Hint

    Try \lambda = 0.5 + 0.45\tanh\big(\frac{u - 0.5}{0.45}\big), which has slope 1 in the middle. Then ask a harder question: the loop is stable for every fixed \lambda, but is it stable while \lambda changes?

    Solution

    For a fixed authority, the question is already settled: every \lambda in [0, 1] gives a stable loop (largest spectral radius 0.996, at \lambda = 0), so any saturation into that interval is stable. The smooth version is more conservative at the ends: it maps the law's raw value 1 to 0.86 instead of 0.95, and 0 to 0.14 instead of 0.05, keeping both agents further in the loop. It also has no kink, so the wheel's behaviour changes smoothly as integrity crosses the limits. While \lambda changes, stability for each frozen value does not guarantee stability in general. Here, though, even adversarial switching between 0.05 and 0.95 does not destabilise the loop: over every switching pattern that repeats within 12 steps, the worst growth per step is 0.991, and switching at random shrinks the state by a factor 0.87 per step.

  4. A loaded driver. Make the workload time-varying, and let it, not just integrity, drive \lambda. When should a loaded driver keep authority despite decent integrity?
    Hint

    Compute \lambda and the effective share before and during a burst of workload, at a decent integrity of 0.7. Then ask what integrity measures, and what it does not.

    Solution

    At I = 0.7, a burst of workload from 0.1 to 0.9 raises \lambda only from 0.71 to 0.76. But the automation's effective share goes from 86% to 93%, mostly because the loaded driver's own gain falls from 0.95 to 0.55 of its normal value. A loaded driver should keep authority when the workload comes from the driving itself: they are reacting to something the automation does not know about, such as an obstacle, a pedestrian or a pothole. Integrity measures whether the perception can be trusted, not whether the automation's goal is right. A sudden, deliberate steering input during a burst of workload is then the driver being right, and taking authority away fights an evasive manoeuvre. Production lane-keeping systems handle this with a driver override, such as a torque threshold on the wheel. A second reason is the out-of-the-loop problem of Part 1: if every burst of workload shifts authority away, the driver learns to disengage, and the next handover is slower [3].

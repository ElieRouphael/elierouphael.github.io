Perception and rendering

So far the controller was told the car's exact position in the lane. A real car has to see it, through a camera. This chapter builds a small synthetic camera from the pinhole model: a 64-by-64 image of the road, rendered from where the car is, then degraded by fog, darkness, occlusion and sensor noise. Drive it and degrade it below. Then look at what happens on a bend sharper than anything the perception was trained on: the image stays perfectly clean. That fact drives the rest of the course.

Part 1

The pinhole camera

Why synthetic images? Because we get the exact ground truth for free, we can dial any degradation continuously, and we need no dataset. The point of the course is not computer-vision accuracy but the trustworthy use of a perception estimate in a control loop, so a transparent renderer is the right tool. And, crucially, it lets us leave the perception's operating domain on demand.

A pinhole camera projects a point in space onto the image plane by a perspective division. Give the point coordinates (X_c, Y_c, Z_c) in the camera frame, with Z_c along the optical axis. By similar triangles, a point at depth Z_c lands on the image plane, at focal length f, at

u = f\,\frac{X_c}{Z_c} + c_x, \qquad v = f\,\frac{Y_c}{Z_c} + c_y,

where (c_x, c_y) is the principal point, where the optical axis meets the image. In homogeneous coordinates this is linear [1], [2]:

Z_c \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = K \begin{bmatrix} X_c \\ Y_c \\ Z_c \end{bmatrix}, \qquad K = \begin{bmatrix} f & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.

The nonlinearity that matters is the division by Z_c: near things are large, far things shrink, and parallel lines converge.

What is perception, here?

In plain words: turning camera pixels into the two numbers the controller needs, how far the car is from the lane centre and how far its heading is off. This chapter builds the camera; chapter 04 builds the network that reads it.

More precisely: a map from images to an estimate of the state (e_y, e_\psi), learned from examples. It is only trustworthy inside its operating domain, the conditions it was trained and tested for.

Part 2

The road is a plane: forward-camera geometry

For lane keeping, the scene that matters is the ground plane. Mount the camera at height h above the road, looking forward along it. Then a point of the road at distance s ahead and lateral offset y has depth Z_c = s, X_c = y (to the right) and Y_c = h (the road is h below the camera). Substituting into the pinhole equations:

\boxed{\;u = c_x + f\,\frac{y}{s}, \qquad v = c_y + f\,\frac{h}{s}\;}

The course's camera has f = 55 pixels, h = 1.25 m and its horizon at row c_y = 21.76. Two consequences are worth noting. As s grows, the projection tends to (c_x, c_y) whatever y is: all lane lines converge to a single vanishing point on the horizon. And because the road is planar, the whole road-to-image map is a 3×3 homography [1], which lane-detection systems use to "flatten" the road into a bird's-eye view (exercise 2). Move a point on the road and watch where it lands:

In the image (64 × 64 pixels)

The lane centreline, in the car's frame, is well approximated to second order in the look-ahead distance by a parabola: a clothoid segment, linearised in s [4], [5]. With the signs of chapter 01 (e_y and e_\psi positive to the left, \kappa > 0 for a left bend), and y measured to the right as above:

y_{\text{road}}(s) = e_y + e_\psi\,s - \frac{1}{2}\,\kappa\,s^2.

The three terms are the lateral offset, the drift due to the heading error, and the road's curvature: a car left of the centreline sees the centreline to its right, and a left bend curves away to the left. The lane markings sit at y_{\text{road}}(s) \pm \frac{w}{2}, with w = 3.5 m. The renderer draws these three lines from 3 m to 45 m ahead, the far ones dimmer, and projects them with the boxed formula: that is the demo at the top.

Himg = Wimg = 64
FOCAL, CAM_H, HORIZON = 55.0, 1.25, 0.34*Himg     # focal [px], camera height [m], horizon row

def project(s, y):
    return Wimg/2 + FOCAL*y/s, HORIZON + FOCAL*CAM_H/s
Part 3

Modelling the degradations

Each condition the operating-domain monitor watches corresponds to a physical or measurement process [2], [3]:

  • Defocus or atmospheric blur is a convolution with a point-spread function (PSF), the image of a single point of light. A Gaussian PSF of standard deviation \sigma is the standard approximation, I_{\text{blur}} = I * g_\sigma. It is separable, so we apply a one-dimensional kernel along the columns, then along the rows.
  • Illumination acts as a multiplicative gain, I \mapsto b\,I (ignoring gamma; exercise 4).
  • Occlusion removes a region of the image, here a band of the far road just below the horizon.
  • Sensor noise is additive. Read noise is well modelled as Gaussian, \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_n^2), and shot noise as Poisson [6]; we use additive Gaussian.

The four presets of the demo at the top are the four regimes used throughout the course: clear, fog with blur, partial occlusion, and an out-of-distribution bend. Here is the Gaussian PSF and what it does to the lane:

For the curious: why blur removes detail (optional)

By the convolution theorem, blurring multiplies the image's Fourier transform by the PSF's. The Fourier transform of a Gaussian of width \sigma is again a Gaussian,

\hat g_\sigma(\omega) = e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\omega^2},

so a spatial frequency \omega is attenuated by e^{-\sigma^2\omega^2/2}: the finest detail, the highest frequencies, goes first. Sharp edges are made of high frequencies, which is why a measure of gradient energy falls so quickly as \sigma grows (Part 4). Separability is the identity g_\sigma(x, y) = g_\sigma(x)\,g_\sigma(y): one two-dimensional convolution becomes two one-dimensional ones, 2(2r + 1) operations per pixel instead of (2r + 1)^2.

Part 4

Cheap pixel features for the monitor

The operating-domain monitor of chapter 06 does not know the true conditions; it has to estimate them from the image. Two very cheap statistics serve as proxies:

  • Brightness = the mean intensity, which tracks the illumination gain.
  • Sharpness = the mean squared image gradient, a Tenengrad-style focus measure. It falls as the blur rises, because convolution with the PSF attenuates high spatial frequencies [3].
def brightness_feat(img): return float(img.mean())
def sharpness_feat(img):
    gx = np.diff(img, axis=1); gy = np.diff(img, axis=0)
    return float(np.mean(gx**2) + np.mean(gy**2))

Both are monotone in their degradation, so a simple threshold on each detects "outside the domain" from the pixels alone. The dotted lines are the domain's limits used in chapter 06: blur up to 1.6 px, illumination down to 0.45.

Part 5

Why an out-of-distribution bend is invisible in the pixels

Blur, darkness and occlusion are visible degradations: they change the image statistics, so a monitor of pixel features can flag them. An out-of-distribution curvature is different. A bend sharper than anything in training still renders as a perfectly clean, well-exposed, in-focus lane, with essentially the brightness and sharpness of an ordinary bend. Nothing local in the pixels says "you have never seen this geometry".

Pixel statistics are nearly identical - the images look equally valid.

If anything, the sharp bend looks slightly better: its sharpness is 18% higher, because the slanted markings have more edge per pixel. Blur at the domain's limit, for comparison, divides the sharpness by 18.5.

The novelty lives in the regime, not in the image quality.

This is the crux of the whole course. No amount of pixel-level confidence or image-feature monitoring will catch an out-of-distribution bend. Detecting it requires comparing what the perception reports against a model of the dynamics, chapter 01's model: if the camera says the car drifts in a way the car physically cannot, something is wrong. That is the innovation test of chapter 05.

Part 6

The chapter on one page

  1. Pinhole camera: a point at depth Z_c lands at f\frac{X_c}{Z_c} + c_x; the division by depth is the whole of perspective.
  2. Road plane: u = c_x + f\frac{y}{s}, v = c_y + f\frac{h}{s}; lane lines meet at the vanishing point, and the map is a homography.
  3. Lane model: y_{\text{road}}(s) = e_y + e_\psi s - \frac{1}{2}\kappa s^2, rendered into a 64 × 64 image.
  4. Degradations: blur is a convolution with a PSF, darkness a gain, occlusion missing data, noise additive.
  5. Features: mean intensity and gradient energy detect darkness and blur from the pixels.
  6. The crux: an out-of-distribution bend produces a clean image; only a dynamics model can catch it.

Part 7

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

  1. The vanishing point. Derive the vanishing-point row v = c_y by letting s grow without bound in the projection. Where does the lateral offset e_y go in that limit, and why?
    Hint

    Substitute the lane model y = y_{\text{road}}(s) into u = c_x + f\frac{y}{s} and look at each term divided by s.

    Solution

    The row is v = c_y + f\frac{h}{s}, and \frac{h}{s} goes to zero, so every road point at great distance lands on the row c_y: the horizon. In the column, the lane model gives u = c_x + f\big(\frac{e_y}{s} + e_\psi - \frac{1}{2}\kappa s\big). The offset term \frac{e_y}{s} vanishes: a fixed sideways distance subtends a smaller and smaller angle as it recedes, so far away every lane position looks the same, and the information about e_y lives in the near, bottom rows of the image. The heading error does not vanish: on a straight road the vanishing point moves sideways to u = c_x + f e_\psi, 1.1 pixels to the right for e_\psi = 0.02: the car points to the left of the road, so the road's direction appears to its right. The curvature term grows instead: with a bend, the parabola has no vanishing point, and the lines curve off towards the side of the bend (the parabola is only valid for moderate s).

  2. The bird's-eye view. The bird's-eye, or inverse-perspective, view undoes the homography. Using u = c_x + f\frac{y}{s} and v = c_y + f\frac{h}{s}, solve for (s, y) given (u, v), and map the clear image back to a top-down road. What happens to noise near the horizon?
    Hint

    The second equation gives s alone. Then differentiate s with respect to v to see how a one-pixel error grows.

    Solution

    From the row, s = \frac{fh}{v - c_y}; then y = \frac{(u - c_x)\,s}{f} = \frac{(u - c_x)\,h}{v - c_y}. Every pixel below the horizon maps back to a patch of road, and pixels at or above it to nothing. Near the horizon the map explodes: \frac{\mathrm{d}s}{\mathrm{d}v} = -\frac{fh}{(v - c_y)^2} = -\frac{s^2}{fh}. In the course's camera (fh = 68.75), the bottom row is 1.7 m ahead and one pixel there is 4 cm of road; row 30 is 8.3 m ahead and one pixel is 1 m; row 23, just under the horizon, is 55 m ahead and one pixel is 45 m. So the far rows are stretched into long streaks in the bird's-eye view, and any noise, blur or one-pixel error in them is amplified quadratically with distance. In practice the top-down view is cut off well before the horizon.

  3. Defocus. Replace the Gaussian PSF with a uniform disc (defocus) kernel of the same radius. How does the curve of sharpness against blur change?
    Hint

    The Gaussian kernel is cut at radius r = 3\sigma. A disc of radius r weights all of it equally. Which of the two spreads light further?

    Solution

    The disc blurs much more for the same \sigma: most of a Gaussian's weight is within one \sigma of the centre, while a disc of radius 3\sigma spreads light evenly out to its edge. On the notebook's scene, the sharpness at \sigma = 1 (radius 3) is 0.0013 with the disc against 0.0036 with the Gaussian; at the domain's limit, \sigma = 1.6 (radius 5), 0.0004 against 0.0013: between two and a half and four times lower over the whole range. The curve keeps the same shape, monotone and steep, but is shifted down. A blur threshold calibrated on Gaussian blur would therefore fire too early on a defocused lens. A disc's frequency response, unlike a Gaussian's, also has zeros: some fine detail is erased completely and some comes back with its sign flipped, which a single focus number cannot tell apart.

  4. Gamma. Add a gamma nonlinearity I \mapsto I^\gamma to the illumination model, and check whether the mean intensity is still a good proxy for brightness.
    Hint

    Apply the gamma after the gain: (bI)^\gamma = b^\gamma I^\gamma. Is the mean still increasing in b? Is it still proportional?

    Solution

    The mean is still increasing in b, so it can still detect darkness, but it is no longer proportional to b, and the threshold has to be recalibrated. On the notebook's scene, with the linear model, the gain limit b = 0.45 corresponds to a mean intensity of 0.082; with \gamma = 0.45 (a typical encoding) to 0.314; with \gamma = 2.2 to 0.0077. The compression also changes the sensitivity: with \gamma = 0.45, going from full light to the limit lowers the mean by 30% instead of 55%, so the same sensor noise makes the threshold less reliable. Gamma also brightens the dark asphalt (0.15 becomes 0.43), which shrinks the contrast that the sharpness feature relies on.

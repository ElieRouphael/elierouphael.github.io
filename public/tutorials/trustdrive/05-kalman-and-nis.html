Kalman filter and NIS

Chapter 04 ended with a warning: a perception network can be confidently wrong, and its own uncertainty will not tell. This chapter builds the independent check that catches it. A Kalman filter predicts, from the car's physics, where the camera's next measurement should land. When the camera starts to lie, its reports stop matching that prediction, however confident it is. Below, the camera picks up a bias halfway through the drive while still claiming its usual small noise; watch the filter's surprise, the normalised innovation squared, jump through its alarm line. This is the mathematical core of the course.

Part 1

Recursive Bayesian estimation

Consider a linear-Gaussian state-space model:

x_k = A x_{k-1} + B u_{k-1} + w_{k-1}, \quad w \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q); \qquad y_k = C x_k + v_k, \quad v \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R).

For the car, x is the lane error (e_y, e_\psi) with chapter 01's matrices, u is the steering, and y is what the perception reports, both components (C = I). We want the posterior p(x_k \mid y_{1:k}), the belief about the state given every measurement so far. It obeys a two-step recursion [2], [4].

Predict (the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation): push the old belief through the dynamics,

p(x_k \mid y_{1:k-1}) = \int p(x_k \mid x_{k-1})\,p(x_{k-1} \mid y_{1:k-1})\,\mathrm{d}x_{k-1}.

Update (Bayes' theorem): weigh the prediction by the likelihood of the new measurement,

p(x_k \mid y_{1:k}) \propto p(y_k \mid x_k)\,p(x_k \mid y_{1:k-1}).

For a linear-Gaussian model, every distribution in this recursion stays Gaussian, so the whole thing collapses to propagating a mean and a covariance. That is exactly the Kalman filter [1].

Part 2

The Kalman filter

Writing each Gaussian as its mean and covariance (\hat x, P), the recursion becomes:

Time update (predict)

\hat x_{k \mid k-1} = A\hat x_{k-1} + Bu_{k-1}, \qquad P_{k \mid k-1} = A P_{k-1} A^\top + Q.

Measurement update

\nu_k = y_k - C\hat x_{k \mid k-1}, \quad S_k = C P_{k \mid k-1} C^\top + R, \quad K_k = P_{k \mid k-1} C^\top S_k^{-1}, \quad \hat x_k = \hat x_{k \mid k-1} + K_k\nu_k, \quad P_k = (I - K_k C)\,P_{k \mid k-1}.

The gain K_k can be derived in two ways that give the same answer [2], [3]: as the Bayesian posterior mean of a Gaussian or, without any Gaussian assumption, as the best linear unbiased estimator, the one that minimises \mathbb{E}\lVert x_k - \hat x_k\rVert^2. Either way, K_k = P_{k \mid k-1} C^\top S_k^{-1}. In one dimension, the update is a weighted average of the prediction and the measurement, weighted by how much each is trusted:

prediction measurement updated estimate

What is a Kalman filter?

In plain words: a loop that, at every step, predicts where the car should be from the physics, then corrects that prediction with the camera's measurement, trusting each in proportion to how precise it is. It also keeps track of how uncertain its own estimate is.

More precisely: the exact recursive Bayesian estimator for a linear system with Gaussian noise, propagating the posterior's mean and covariance. Without Gaussian noise, it is still the best linear unbiased estimator.

State estimate \hat x
The filter's best guess of the state.
Covariance P
How uncertain that guess is.
Innovation \nu
The measurement minus its prediction: what the model did not see coming.
Innovation covariance S
How large the innovation should be, if the model is right.
Kalman gain K
How much of the innovation to believe.
Part 3

The innovation, and why it is white

The innovation \nu_k = y_k - C\hat x_{k \mid k-1} is the part of the new measurement that the model did not predict. For the correct model with the optimal gain, it has three properties [5], [6]:

\mathbb{E}[\nu_k] = 0, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}[\nu_k] = S_k, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\nu_k\,\nu_j^\top] = 0 \ \text{ for different steps } k \text{ and } j.

The last one, whiteness, is the powerful one. An optimal filter extracts all the predictable information, so what remains is noise uncorrelated in time: the filter turns the correlated measurements into a white sequence of innovations, the "innovations representation" [5]. If the innovations are not white, or not zero-mean, or do not match S_k, the model is wrong. That is the hook for both consistency testing and fault detection. Give the filter a wrong model and see:

Part 4

The NIS statistic and the consistency test

Since \nu_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, S_k), whiten it: e_k = S_k^{-1/2}\nu_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_m). Then the normalised innovation squared is a sum of m squared standard normals:

\boxed{\;\mathrm{NIS}_k = \nu_k^\top S_k^{-1}\nu_k = e_k^\top e_k\;} \sim \chi^2_m, \qquad m = \dim(y), \quad \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{NIS}_k] = m.

Single-sample gate. A sample with \mathrm{NIS}_k above \chi^2_{m, 1-\alpha} is flagged as inconsistent; for m = 2, \chi^2_{2, 0.99} = 9.21. For two dimensions the tail is simply P(\chi^2_2 > t) = e^{-t/2}, so 9.21 is -2\ln 0.01.

Time-averaged test. One sample is noisy. Averaged over a window of N approximately independent steps, \sum_k \mathrm{NIS}_k \sim \chi^2_{Nm}, so the mean should fall inside a tight interval around m: the filter consistency test of Bar-Shalom [6]. Too high means the filter is overconfident (the real errors exceed the claimed S); too low means it is conservative.

NEES, a cousin for simulation. When the true state is known, as in a simulator, the normalised estimation error squared, \mathrm{NEES}_k = (x_k - \hat x_k)^\top P_k^{-1}(x_k - \hat x_k) \sim \chi^2_n, tests whether the reported covariance P_k is honest [6]. The notebook uses it to validate its filter:

def run_filter(N=500, bias_after=None, bias=(0.0, 0.0), R_scale=1.0, seed=0):
    ...
    for k in range(N):
        u = 0.05*np.sin(2*np.pi*k*Ts/3.0)                 # known steering input
        x = A@x + B*u + r.multivariate_normal([0, 0], Qt) # true dynamics
        b = np.array(bias) if (bias_after is not None and k >= bias_after) else np.zeros(2)
        y = C@x + b + r.multivariate_normal([0, 0], Rt)   # measurement (+ optional OOD bias)
        # --- Kalman filter ---
        xp = A@xhat + B*u; Pp = A@P@A.T + Qt
        nu = y - C@xp; S = C@Pp@C.T + Rc
        nis = float(nu @ np.linalg.solve(S, nu))
        K = Pp@C.T@np.linalg.inv(S); xhat = xp + K@nu; P = (np.eye(2) - K@C)@Pp
        nees = float((x - xhat) @ np.linalg.solve(P, x - xhat))
mean NIS  = 2.08  (expected m = 2)
mean NEES = 2.01  (expected n = 2)
fraction of NIS above the 99% gate: 0.02  (expected ~0.01)
time-averaged NIS  = 2.09   95% acceptance interval [1.82, 2.19]
time-averaged NEES = 1.99   -> filter covariance P is honest

Everything checks out: the average NIS sits inside its 95% interval, and the reported covariance is honest. But the test only means something if the noise the filter is told is the truth. Change what the camera claims:

NIS samples \chi^2_2 density, if the filter is consistent 

claimed-R scale   mean NIS   interpretation
    0.25            4.06    overconfident R -> false alarms
    0.50            2.89    overconfident R -> false alarms
    1.00            1.93    honest R -> mean ~ 2
    2.00            1.24    conservative R -> misses faults
    4.00            0.79    conservative R -> misses faults

The test compares the innovation with the claimed S. A perception that overstates its noise hides its faults; one that understates it raises false alarms. An honest R is part of the contract.

Part 5

From consistency to fault detection

Because NIS tests the innovation against the model, anything that breaks the model raises it: a bias, an unmodelled manoeuvre, a wrong noise covariance, or an out-of-distribution regime that the perception was never trained on. The classical theory of fault detection from innovations formalises this [7], [8], and a gate on NIS also rejects outliers when associating measurements with tracks [6].

The case that matters for us is a perception network that becomes confidently wrong. Its measurement acquires a systematic bias, but it keeps reporting a small R. The innovation then grows while S_k stays small, so \mathrm{NIS} = \nu^\top S^{-1}\nu explodes: the failure is detected even though the perception's own uncertainty never rose. That is the demo at the top, with the notebook's fault:

mean NIS  before bias: 1.9    after bias: 10.5
detection rate after bias (NIS>9.21): 47%

The average NIS jumps five-fold, and about half of the samples during the fault cross the 99% line, against 1% normally. Notice also that the alarm does not fade, although the filter adjusts its estimate.

For the curious: why the filter cannot absorb this bias (optional)

A Kalman filter fed a biased measurement shifts its estimate towards it, and a bias the dynamics could explain would soon look like an ordinary state. This one cannot be explained. The heading reading is 0.06 rad off, which by chapter 01's model means a sideways drift of v\,e_\psi = 15 \times 0.06 = 0.9 m/s; but the offset reading, biased by a constant 0.22 m, shows no such drift. No state of the car fits both readings, so the filter settles on a compromise that leaves a persistent innovation: in the notebook's run, the lateral innovation averages -0.08 m during the fault, almost three times its claimed standard deviation (0.03 m), and the NIS stays near 10. The more physically impossible the measurements, the louder the alarm.

Part 6

Where this plugs in

Using a filter's residuals to decide whether its output can be trusted is what satellite navigation calls integrity monitoring [9]. Here the NIS is the second of the three signals fused into the integrity score of chapter 06:

s_{\text{NIS}} = \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\big(\mathrm{NIS} - \chi^2\big)^+}{c}\Big),

which stays near 1 while the filter is consistent and decays once the NIS crosses the threshold ((\cdot)^+ keeps only the positive part). Unlike the perception's own uncertainty (chapter 04) and the pixel features of the operating-domain monitor (chapter 03), the NIS is an external check grounded in the car's dynamics.

The physics is the witness the perception cannot argue with.

That is why it catches the out-of-distribution bend that the perception's own uncertainty and the pixel features miss.

Part 7

The chapter on one page

  1. Recursive Bayes: predict through the dynamics, update with the measurement; for linear-Gaussian models, only means and covariances are needed.
  2. Kalman filter: K = P C^\top S^{-1} weighs the innovation by the relative trust in prediction and measurement.
  3. Innovations: zero mean, covariance S, white, if the model is right.
  4. NIS = \nu^\top S^{-1}\nu \sim \chi^2_m: a 99% gate at 9.21 for m = 2, and a time-averaged consistency test.
  5. Honest noise: the test compares with the claimed S; overstating the noise hides faults.
  6. Fault detection: a confidently biased perception makes the NIS explode, because physics contradicts it.

Part 8

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

  1. The moments of NIS and NEES. Confirm empirically that \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{NIS}] = m and \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{NEES}] = n by averaging over many independent runs, and check that the variances match 2m and 2n.
    Hint

    Run the filter with 200 different seeds and pool the samples after a warm-up of 50 steps. A \chi^2_k variable has mean k and variance 2k.

    Solution

    Over 200 runs of 300 steps (50,000 samples after warm-up), the NIS has mean 2.000 and variance 4.02, and the NEES mean 2.018 and variance 4.03, against the theoretical 2 and 4 for both (m = n = 2). The match of the variance matters as much as the mean: it says the samples are not just right on average but have the whole \chi^2_2 shape, which is what makes a gate at 9.21 mean "1% false alarms".

  2. An unmodelled manoeuvre. Make the true process noise larger than the filter's Q. Which statistic reacts: NIS, NEES, or both?
    Hint

    Multiply the true process noise by 4, 10 and 25 while the filter keeps the original Q.

    Solution

    Both. With the true process noise 4 times the filter's, the mean NIS rises from about 1.9 to 4.0 and the NEES from 2.1 to 4.0; at 10 times, 8.3 and 7.6; at 25 times, 19.2 and 16.5. The filter believes its predictions too much, so both its estimate errors and its innovations exceed what it claims. The practical difference: the NEES needs the true state, which only a simulator has; the NIS is computed from the measurements alone, so it is the one that works in a real car.

  3. A slow drift. Replace the step bias with a slow ramp, and compare single-sample gating with a windowed average. Which detects the gradual drift sooner? (This foreshadows the CUSUM test of chapter 09.)
    Hint

    Make the comparison fair: tune each test's threshold so that both raise false alarms at the same rate when there is no fault. A 99% gate checked every step raises one about every 5 seconds, which would make its "detections" meaningless.

    Solution

    The windowed average, once both tests are tuned to raise false alarms no more often than about once per 100 s when there is no fault. For a bias ramping to the notebook's size over 10 s, the median delay is 7.1 s for the single-sample gate and 4.9 to 5.1 s for averages over 1 or 2 s. For a ramp to 30% of that size, the averages catch 98 to 100% of runs, against 75% for the gate. Averaging accumulates many small excesses that no single sample could distinguish from noise. Without that calibration, a naive comparison misleads: a 99% gate on every step fires after about 3 s even with no fault at all. Chapter 09's CUSUM test makes the accumulation optimal.

  4. Whiteness under a bias. Compute the lag-1 autocorrelation of the innovation without a fault and under the bias. Does a constant bias break whiteness, or only the zero mean?
    Hint

    Compare with \pm\frac{1.96}{\sqrt{N}}, the band within which a white sequence's sample autocorrelation falls 95% of the time. Look at the steady part of the fault separately from its first few steps.

    Solution

    Only the zero mean, once the filter settles. Without a fault, the lag-1 autocorrelation of the lateral innovation is -0.04, well inside \pm 0.09. In the steady part of the fault (from 15 s on), it is -0.09, still inside its band (\pm 0.14 for 200 samples), while the mean innovation is -0.08 m instead of 0. Including the transient right after the onset, while the filter is still adjusting, the autocorrelation rises to 0.14. So a constant bias is caught by the NIS through its mean, not by a whiteness test; a wrong model of the dynamics, as in the widget of Part 3, is what breaks whiteness.

Introduction and overview

A neural network that reads the road from a camera works well inside the conditions it was trained on, and can fail silently outside them: confidently wrong. Inside a control loop, a wrong estimate is not a bad number on a screen; the car steers on it. This course builds, from first principles, the machinery to decide when such a perception can be trusted. Below is the phenomenon at the heart of it. Halfway through, the perception's estimate becomes biased. Watch which signal notices: the network's own uncertainty, or a check against the car's physics.

Part 1

Trustworthy AI perception in a control loop

Modern driver-assistance and automated-driving systems increasingly replace hand-engineered perception with learned components: a neural network maps camera pixels to the quantities a controller needs, such as the lane offset, obstacles and other road users. This works well inside the conditions the network was trained on, its operational design domain (ODD) [1], and can fail silently outside it. A blurred lens, a low sun, an unusual road geometry, or simply an input unlike anything in the training set can make the network confidently wrong.

The danger is specific to a closed loop. A wrong estimate is fed straight into a controller that acts on it, and the vehicle moves. So the question this course studies is not "how accurate is the perception?" but:

Given an imperfect, learned perception, when should we trust it enough to close the control loop on it, and what should happen when we should not?

The course answers it with three ingredients: a way to quantify the perception's uncertainty (chapters 04 and 05), a way to decide whether the system is inside its competence (chapter 06), and a way to act on that decision by sharing authority with a human driver (chapter 07). The same theme runs through recent research: fault detection for perception systems [9], integrity monitoring of learned object detectors [10], and monitoring the operating domain from uncertain measurements [11].

What is an integrity monitor?

In plain words: a watchdog that sits between the perception and the controller, and decides, at every step, how much the perception's output can be trusted, using evidence the network cannot fake.

More precisely: a function of the perception's outputs, the vehicle's dynamics and the operating conditions that returns an integrity score I_k \in [0, 1], from which the controller's authority and the vehicle's speed are set. The term comes from satellite navigation, where deciding when a position estimate must not be trusted is safety-critical [16].

Closed loop
The controller acts on the estimate, and the action changes what is measured next.
Operational design domain
The conditions a system was designed and validated for.
Confidently wrong
A wrong estimate that comes with a small reported uncertainty.
Integrity score
The monitor's verdict, between 0 (do not trust) and 1 (trust).
Part 2

The system as equations

The course studies the smallest closed loop that still contains the real difficulty. The symbols are collected in Part 7.

Plant. A vehicle tracking a lane, written in error coordinates x = [e_y,\ e_\psi]^\top (the lateral offset in metres and the heading error in radians), driven by the steering angle u = \delta, with the road's curvature \kappa as a measured disturbance:

x_{k+1} = f(x_k,\ u_k,\ \kappa_k). \tag{1}

Perception. A camera produces an image \mathcal{I}_k = g(x_k, c_k) that also depends on the conditions c_k: brightness, blur, occlusion, curvature. A learned estimator h_\theta inverts it into an estimate of the state, together with a self-reported covariance R_k^{\text{AI}}, how uncertain the network believes it is:

\hat y_k = h_\theta(\mathcal{I}_k) \approx C x_k, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}[\hat y_k] \approx R_k^{\text{AI}}. \tag{2}

Controller. A feedback law acts on an estimate of the state,

u_k = \pi(\hat x_k), \tag{3}

and the naive choice is \hat x_k = \hat y_k: trust the network completely.

The missing layer. Between (2) and (3), the course inserts a monitor that outputs an integrity score I_k \in [0, 1] and an authority \lambda_k \in [0, 1]: how much of the automation's command to apply, against the human's. Constructing I_k and \lambda_k well is what the rest of the course is about.

Part 3

Why "the network is confident" is not enough

A prediction can be uncertain for two very different reasons [7]:

  • Aleatoric uncertainty is irreducible sensor noise. It is what R_k^{\text{AI}} is meant to capture, and more data does not remove it.
  • Epistemic uncertainty is the model's ignorance, largest where it saw little training data, that is, out of distribution. A deep ensemble [6] estimates it from the disagreement between independently trained models (chapter 04).

Both can be reported as a covariance, and both share one failure mode: in a genuinely novel regime, a network can be confidently wrong. Every ensemble member agrees, R_k^{\text{AI}} stays small, yet the estimate is biased. Uncertainty reported by the model alone cannot detect this, because the model is the thing that is wrong.

The classical remedy, from estimation and fault-detection theory [2], [3], [4], [5], is an independent, model-based check. Run a Kalman predictor from the physics (1) to predict what the measurement should be, then test the innovation \nu_k = \hat y_k - C\hat x_{k \mid k-1} against its own covariance S_k = C P_{k \mid k-1} C^\top + R_k^{\text{AI}}, through the normalised innovation squared:

\boxed{\;\mathrm{NIS}_k = \nu_k^\top S_k^{-1}\,\nu_k\;} \tag{4}

If the model and the measurement are consistent, S_k^{-1/2}\nu_k is standard normal, so \mathrm{NIS}_k \sim \chi^2_m, with m = \dim(\hat y) degrees of freedom [3]. A value above the chi-squared threshold flags a mismatch even when R_k^{\text{AI}} is small. That clause is the entire point, and the demo at the top shows it.

Finally, the course fuses three complementary signals into a single score, so that any one of them can veto trust (chapter 06):

I_k = s_{\text{unc}}\big(\Sigma^{\text{AI}}_k\big) \cdot s_{\text{NIS}}\big(\mathrm{NIS}_k\big) \cdot s_{\text{ODD}}(c_k). \tag{5}
Part 4

A first look: the phenomenon in one experiment

The demo at the top is the notebook's experiment. A monitor receives a stream of perception measurements of a car following a winding lane. For the first half they are consistent. Then an out-of-distribution bias appears: the measurement becomes systematically wrong, but its reported uncertainty R^{\text{AI}} does not grow. This is the mechanism of chapter 05, shown here only to motivate the course.

srep = np.array([0.02, 0.01])                      # reported std - CONSTANT throughout
for k in range(N):
    kappa = 0.012*np.sin(2*np.pi*k*Ts/6)
    true = np.array([true[0] + V*np.sin(true[1])*Ts, true[1] - V*kappa*Ts])
    bias = np.zeros(2) if k < 150 else np.array([0.22, 0.06])   # OOD bias for k >= 150
    y = true + bias + rng.normal(0, srep)
    x = A @ x + E*kappa; P = A @ P @ A.T + Q                     # KF predict (delta = 0)
    S = P + np.diag(srep**2); nu = y - x
    nis.append(float(nu @ np.linalg.solve(S, nu)))
mean NIS  in-distribution: 1.3   |   out-of-distribution: 13.6

The reported uncertainty never moves, so a rule that watches it stays silent through the entire failure. The model-based NIS crosses the chi-squared line on 70% of the samples once the measurement stops agreeing with the physics. A monitor built only on the network's self-reported confidence is blind here; a monitor that also checks consistency with a model is not. Everything else in the course, the controller, the operating domain, the integrity score and the shared control, exists to turn this detection into safe action.

Two details are worth a second look. First, the mean NIS before the fault is 1.3, not the 2 of a perfectly consistent filter: the filter assumes a process noise this simulated car does not have, so it overstates its predicted spread. Chapter 05 gives the car exactly the noise its filter assumes, and recovers 2. Second, switch the demo to the network that admits its error, by reporting a noise large enough to cover it. The roles reverse: the reported uncertainty jumps above the handover's threshold, while the NIS falls quiet (a mean of 0.9 during the fault), because the error is now within the claimed noise. Each monitor catches a failure the other misses, which is why chapter 06 fuses them.

Part 5

The three architectures, precisely

The capstone (chapter 08) compares three ways of using the same perception, stated here as decision rules.

Naive AI: trust completely,

\hat x_k = \hat y_k, \qquad u_k = \pi(\hat x_k), \qquad \lambda_k = 1.

Hard handover: switch fully to the driver when the reported uncertainty crosses a threshold \tau,

\lambda_k = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \mathrm{tr}\,R_k^{\text{AI}} \le \tau, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}

As the experiment shows, this never triggers on the confident-but-wrong case. And when it does trigger, the switch is abrupt, with the driver suddenly in charge; how drivers keep track of who is driving, and how much they trust a partially automated car, is a field of its own [14].

Proposed, integrity-aware: filter the estimate, fuse the integrity score (5), then share authority smoothly [13] and contract the operating envelope,

u_k = \delta^{\text{ff}}_k + \lambda_k\,\mathrm{fb}_A(\hat x_k) + (1 - \lambda_k)\,\delta^H_k, \qquad \lambda_k = \Phi(I_k, W_k), \qquad v^{\text{ref}}_k = v_{\text{nom}}\,(0.4 + 0.6\,I_k),

where W_k is the driver's workload [12] and \delta^H_k the driver's command (chapter 07). The map-based feed-forward \delta^{\text{ff}}_k always applies; only the feedback authority is arbitrated (chapter 02 explains why). Here are the three rules deciding the authority on the demo's stream:

naive hard handover proposed

The proposed rule here uses only the uncertainty and NIS scores, with chapter 07's authority law and a workload of 0.2; the demo has no operating-domain conditions to score. With the confident network, its authority drops only while the NIS crosses its line, so it flickers. That weakness is exactly what the domain monitor (chapter 06) and the cumulative CUSUM test (chapter 09) address.

Roadmap

The first chapters build the plant and its controller, then the perception and its uncertainty. The middle chapters build the monitor: the Kalman consistency test, the operating domain, the fused integrity score and the shared control. The capstone assembles everything and tests it, and the last chapter shows how to make each piece rigorous.

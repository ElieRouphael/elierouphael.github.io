Part 1

Sequential change detection: CUSUM

The capstone found that a single-sample gate on the NIS is intermittent. Under a bias the filter partly tracks, the NIS crosses the gate on only about 30% of the samples, so on its own the gate is a weak trigger. The fix is to accumulate evidence over time.

From the SPRT to CUSUM. Detecting a change from an in-control density p_0 to an out-of-control density p_1 as quickly as possible, within a false-alarm budget, is a sequential testing problem. Wald's sequential probability ratio test [2] accumulates the log-likelihood ratio of each observation,

s_k = \log \frac{p_1(z_k)}{p_0(z_k)},

which is negative on average before the change and positive after it. Page's CUSUM [1] applies it with a reset at zero:

\boxed{\;g_k = \max\big(0,\ g_{k-1} + s_k\big), \qquad \text{alarm when } g_k > h\;}

To detect an increase in the mean, the increment becomes z_k - k_{\text{ref}}, with a reference halfway between the two means, k_{\text{ref}} \approx \frac{\mu_0 + \mu_1}{2}. This is exact for Gaussian data (exercise 1). Before the change the increments are negative, and g_k stays pinned near 0: few false alarms. After it they turn positive, and g_k ramps up: fast detection. CUSUM is optimal in a precise sense: among all tests with the same false-alarm rate, it minimises the worst-case expected detection delay. Lorden defined that criterion and proved CUSUM asymptotically optimal for it [3]; Moustakides later proved it exactly optimal [14], [4].

What is a CUSUM?

In plain words: a running total of how surprising each new sample is, which never goes below zero. Ordinary samples drain it; a run of slightly surprising ones fills it up, and the alarm sounds when it overflows.

More precisely: the cumulative sum of log-likelihood ratios between the after-change and before-change distributions, reset at zero, compared with a threshold. For a given false-alarm rate, it is the optimal detector of an abrupt change, in Lorden's worst-case sense [3], [14].

One run says little. Here the gate happened to fire one step after the change: after it, a sample crosses 9.21 with probability 0.35, so an early crossing is likely. In this run, it also raised no false alarm in the first 200 steps, although it fires on 1% of in-control samples. The fair comparison fixes the false-alarm rate, and measures the delay.

For the curious: the exact likelihood ratio for the NIS (optional)

The NIS is not Gaussian. In control it is \chi^2_2, with density \frac{1}{2}e^{-z/2}; under a bias it becomes a noncentral \chi^2_2 with noncentrality \lambda, with density \frac{1}{2}e^{-(z + \lambda)/2}\,I_0\big(\sqrt{\lambda z}\big), where I_0 is a modified Bessel function. The exact log-likelihood ratio is therefore

s(z) = -\frac{\lambda}{2} + \log I_0\big(\sqrt{\lambda z}\big),

increasing in z but not linear. The CUSUM on this exact s is the optimal one. Is the simple linear version far behind? Not much. At about one false alarm per 780 steps, the exact CUSUM detects the notebook's change in 3.7 steps on average, the linear one with k_{\text{ref}} = 5 in 4.0.

Part 2

The operating characteristic: average run length

A detector's quality is summarised by its average run lengths (ARL) [4]. \mathrm{ARL}_0 is the mean time between false alarms while in control (want it large); \mathrm{ARL}_1 is the mean detection delay once the change has happened (want it small). Sweeping the threshold traces a trade-off curve. For the single-sample gate, both are exact: with a gate t, \mathrm{ARL}_0 = \frac{1}{P(\chi^2_2 > t)} = e^{t/2}, and similarly after the change. The CUSUM's are simulated:

Simulating…

At any given false-alarm interval, CUSUM detects sooner, and its advantage grows as false alarms are made rarer. At one false alarm per 100 steps, the delay is 2.6 steps against 2.9 for the gate; at one per 300, 3.4 against 4.4; at one per 1,000, 4.2 against 7.4. That is exactly the robustness the capstone was missing: a gate strict enough to rarely cry wolf becomes slow, while the sum keeps adding up small excesses that no single sample could separate from noise.

The simulation lets every run go on until its first false alarm. At the largest threshold that takes about 4,600 steps on average, and a run cut short would understate \mathrm{ARL}_0.

Part 3

Conformal prediction

Ensembles (chapter 04) give a spread, but no guarantee. Conformal prediction [5] wraps any model to produce prediction sets with a finite-sample, distribution-free coverage guarantee.

Split conformal. Hold out a calibration set of n points, compute their nonconformity scores R_i = |y_i - \hat f(x_i)|, and let q be the \lceil (n + 1)(1 - \alpha)\rceil-th smallest. Then, for an exchangeable test point,

\boxed{\;\mathbb{P}\big(y_{n+1} \in \hat f(x_{n+1}) \pm q\big) \ \ge\ 1 - \alpha\;}

Why it holds. If the calibration points and the test point are exchangeable, the rank of the test score R_{n+1} among R_1, \ldots, R_{n+1} is uniform on \{1, \ldots, n + 1\}. So \mathbb{P}(R_{n+1} \le q) \ge \frac{\lceil (n + 1)(1 - \alpha)\rceil}{n + 1} \ge 1 - \alpha, with no assumption on the distribution [6], [7]. The catch is the exchangeability premise, which is exactly what a distribution shift breaks. A drop in realised coverage is therefore itself an out-of-distribution signal. Move the test inputs away from where the model was trained and calibrated:

For the curious: what the guarantee does and does not promise (optional)

The guarantee is marginal: it averages over the draw of the calibration set as well as of the test point. For one fixed calibration set of n = 800 points, the coverage is itself random, distributed as \mathrm{Beta}(721, 80): mean 0.900, standard deviation 0.011 [7]. Half of all calibration sets give a coverage below 0.90, and one in twenty below 0.882. The notebook's calibration set happens to give 0.909 (measured on two million test points); the printed 0.92 adds the noise of its 5,000 test points. Another run could just as well print 0.89, without breaking anything. A guarantee that holds for most calibration sets, not just on average, needs a slightly larger q: the "training-conditional" versions of conformal prediction.

Part 4

Keeping coverage under drift: adaptive conformal inference

If the world drifts, fixed conformal loses coverage. Adaptive conformal inference (ACI) [8] restores it online, with a tiny feedback rule on the miscoverage level:

\alpha_{t+1} = \alpha_t + \gamma\,\big(\alpha - \mathbb{1}[\text{miss}_t]\big).

Each miss lowers \alpha_t, which widens the next interval; each hit raises it a little. This provably drives the long-run empirical coverage to 1 - \alpha for any sequence of data, even an adversarial one. Below, the input drifts from the training domain [-2, 2] to 5, and each interval uses the scores seen so far:

Two remarks keep this honest. First, ACI restores coverage by widening the intervals: once the input has drifted out of the training domain, the fixed-level predictor covers only 51% of the points, ACI 90%, but by the end ACI's half-width is about 2.1, against 0.17 inside the domain, for a function of amplitude 1. That is not a better model, it is an honest admission of ignorance. And the widening, or the fall of \alpha_t, is itself the out-of-distribution signal. Second, the guarantee assumes that when \alpha_t drops to zero or below, the interval becomes the whole line. The notebook instead caps it at the largest past score, which happens on 16% of the steps here; with the textbook rule, the coverage is the same, 0.899.

Part 5

A research roadmap

Where a serious version of this system would go, with the mathematics named and the literature to start from:

  1. Chance-constrained, stochastic MPC instead of LQR. Replace the regulator with a predictive controller that keeps the lane with high probability, \mathbb{P}(|e_y| \le e_{\max}) \ge 1 - \varepsilon over a horizon, propagating the perception covariance \Sigma^{\text{AI}} into a tightening of the constraints. This turns the integrity signal into a rigorous safety margin, rather than a speed heuristic [9].
  2. A learned driver state. Replace the scalar workload W with an estimate from gaze, reaction time or steering entropy, so that the authority \lambda = \Phi(I, W) reflects the real driver: the workload-adaptive shared control of chapter 07.
  3. Perception with built-in, calibrated uncertainty. Swap the synthetic sensor and the ensemble for evidential or prior-network heads that output a distribution directly [10], [11], trained on real images and a photorealistic simulator such as CARLA, with the NIS and domain monitors wrapped around the real network.
  4. Runtime assurance and formal guarantees. Make the contracted-envelope mode a verified safety controller, and switch to it under low integrity: a Simplex, or runtime-assurance, architecture [12]. Certify its safe set with control barrier functions or reachability analysis [13], moving from a heuristic score towards an assured envelope.
  5. Integrity with a bound, not a score. Borrow the aviation notion of a protection level: instead of I \in [0, 1], compute a statistical bound on the state error that holds with a target integrity risk, using the conformal or change-detection machinery above. This is the path from a monitor to a safety case.
  6. A higher-fidelity plant. A dynamic bicycle model with tyre slip and actuator lag (chapter 01), longitudinal control, and closed-loop evaluation in a realistic simulator, where the monitored perception feeds the controller. Chapter 08 showed why: the sharp bend's 1.08 g is invisible to a kinematic model.

Part 7

The chapter on one page

  1. CUSUM: g_k = \max(0, g_{k-1} + s_k), alarm above h; for a mean shift, s_k = z_k - k_{\text{ref}}, with k_{\text{ref}} halfway between the means.
  2. Average run lengths: \mathrm{ARL}_0 between false alarms, \mathrm{ARL}_1 to detection; at equal \mathrm{ARL}_0, CUSUM detects sooner, the more so as false alarms get rarer.
  3. Split conformal: \hat f(x) \pm q, with q the \lceil (n + 1)(1 - \alpha)\rceil-th smallest calibration score, covers with probability at least 1 - \alpha under exchangeability.
  4. Shift: breaking exchangeability voids the guarantee; the fall in coverage is an out-of-distribution signal.
  5. ACI: \alpha_{t+1} = \alpha_t + \gamma(\alpha - \mathbb{1}[\text{miss}_t]) restores long-run coverage, by widening the intervals.
  6. Roadmap: stochastic MPC, driver-state estimation, calibrated perception, runtime assurance, protection levels, higher-fidelity plants.

Part 8

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

  1. The Gaussian CUSUM. Derive the CUSUM increment z_k - k_{\text{ref}} from the log-likelihood ratio of a Gaussian mean shift, and identify k_{\text{ref}} in terms of \mu_0, \mu_1 and \sigma.
    Hint

    Write \log \frac{\mathcal{N}(z; \mu_1, \sigma^2)}{\mathcal{N}(z; \mu_0, \sigma^2)}; the quadratic terms in z cancel.

    Solution
    s(z) = \frac{(z - \mu_0)^2 - (z - \mu_1)^2}{2\sigma^2} = \frac{\mu_1 - \mu_0}{\sigma^2}\Big(z - \frac{\mu_0 + \mu_1}{2}\Big).

    The factor \frac{\mu_1 - \mu_0}{\sigma^2} is positive and constant, so dividing the whole recursion by it changes nothing but the scale of the threshold. The increment is z_k - k_{\text{ref}} with k_{\text{ref}} = \frac{\mu_0 + \mu_1}{2}, and the threshold becomes h' = \frac{h\sigma^2}{\mu_1 - \mu_0}. The reference does not depend on \sigma; only the threshold does. For the NIS, \mu_0 = 2 and \mu_1 = 2 + \lambda = 8, which gives the notebook's k_{\text{ref}} = 5. The NIS is not Gaussian, though, so there this is a heuristic, a very good one (Part 1's box).

  2. CUSUM in the capstone. Add the CUSUM to the integrity monitor of chapter 08, feeding it the NIS, and re-run the sharp-bend ablation with the NIS alone. Does the cumulative test now hold the integrity down where the single-sample score could not?
    Hint

    Keep k_{\text{ref}} = 5 and h = 10. Feed the integrity monitor nothing while g_k \le h, and the excess g_k - h above the gate once it crosses. Check the other scenarios for false alarms.

    Solution

    Yes, mostly. With the NIS as the only external check, the single-sample score holds the integrity below one half on only 13% of the bend on average over ten seeds; the CUSUM, on 94%. The RMSE falls from 0.172 to 0.063 m, and the unsafe usage from 29% to 2%: almost the full system's 0.052 m and 0.2%. On the notebook's own seed the gain is smaller (0.170 to 0.095 m; 10% to 62% of the bend), a reminder to look at more than one run. And the CUSUM costs nothing elsewhere: in nominal, fog and conflict, g_k never reaches h, and every result is unchanged. With the domain monitor also on, the CUSUM is redundant here (0.053 against 0.052 m), as the bend is a monitored condition, but it makes the NIS a standalone trigger for the failures no map can announce.

  3. A self-starting CUSUM. Implement a normalised, self-starting CUSUM that estimates \mu_0 and \sigma online.
    Hint

    Standardise each sample with a running mean and standard deviation (Welford's update), learned only while the statistic sits at zero, and run the CUSUM on the standardised values with k = 1.5 and h = 4.5, which is the notebook's test in units of \sigma = 2.

    Solution

    It works, but it pays in false alarms. With the true \mu_0 = 2 and \sigma = 2, the standardised CUSUM has a mean time between false alarms of about 720 steps. Self-starting after a burn-in of 30 samples, about 150; after 100 samples, about 310; after 200, about 460. Two effects compound. Estimating a mean and a spread from a few dozen samples of a skewed \chi^2_2 is noisy, and an underestimated spread inflates every increment. And learning only while g_k = 0 discards the in-control samples that happened to run high, which biases both estimates low. The remedies are a longer burn-in, a larger threshold, or a robust estimate (a median and a scaled interquartile range). The general lesson: a detector's false-alarm rate depends on knowing the in-control behaviour, and that knowledge has a price.

  4. Normalised conformal scores. Use a normalised nonconformity score, \frac{|y - \hat f(x)|}{\hat\sigma(x)}, with an ensemble's \hat\sigma, and compare the sharpness of the intervals with the constant-width version.
    Hint

    Fit five random-feature ridge models on bootstrap resamples of the training set, take their standard deviation (plus a small floor) as \hat\sigma, calibrate q on the normalised scores, and predict \hat f(x) \pm q\,\hat\sigma(x). Look inside and outside the training range.

    Solution

    Inside the training range, the normalised intervals are not sharper: they cover the same 91%, with a mean half-width of 0.184 against 0.172. The noise here is the same everywhere, while the ensemble's spread, an epistemic quantity, varies from 0.013 to 0.035 and makes the widths vary where they should not. Outside the range, the picture flips. The constant-width intervals cover 36% of points on [2, 3] and 9% on [3, 5]; the normalised ones widen to mean half-widths of 4.4 and 9.6 and cover every point. They become honest out of distribution, though uselessly wide, and without a guarantee, since exchangeability is broken there too. Normalising pays off in sharpness when the score's scale genuinely varies, as with heteroscedastic noise and a \hat\sigma that tracks it.

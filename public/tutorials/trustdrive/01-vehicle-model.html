The vehicle model

Everything in this course controls one thing: a car keeping its lane. Before we can control it, or check what its camera tells us, we need a model of how it moves. This chapter builds one from geometry alone: two wheels that roll without sliding and one turning centre. Steer the car below and watch the turning centre, the circle it drives and the sideways acceleration that circle needs. Then follow the derivation to the two-equation model that the rest of the course uses, and find out where it stops being true.

Part 1

The kinematic bicycle

Why a model at all? The controller of chapter 02 and the Kalman monitor of chapter 05 are both model-based: they use a mathematical prediction of how the car moves. The quality of that model sets the quality of both, so we take it seriously, and we measure where it stops being valid.

At low sideways acceleration, the two wheels on each axle can be lumped into one, giving a bicycle: one rear wheel, one steered front wheel, a wheelbase L between them. We take the rear axle as the reference point, and the state (X, Y, \psi) is its position and the car's heading on the ground.

The key modelling assumption is no side-slip: each wheel rolls in the direction it points, a so-called nonholonomic constraint [4]. Then:

  • the rear wheel moves along the heading, so \dot X = v\cos\psi and \dot Y = v\sin\psi;
  • the front wheel points at \psi + \delta. Both wheels must turn about one instantaneous centre of rotation (ICR), which fixes the turning radius R = \frac{L}{\tan\delta} and hence the yaw rate:
\dot\psi = \frac{v}{R} = \frac{v}{L}\tan\delta.

Collecting these gives the standard kinematic bicycle [1], [2]:

\dot X = v\cos\psi, \qquad \dot Y = v\sin\psi, \qquad \dot\psi = \frac{v}{L}\tan\delta. \tag{1}

The ICR sits on the line through the rear axle, perpendicular to the heading, at distance R. Move the steering angle to see why R = \frac{L}{\tan\delta}:

What is a kinematic model?

In plain words: a model of motion from geometry alone. It says where the car goes when the wheels roll the way they point, and ignores forces, mass and tyres entirely.

More precisely: a kinematic model relates velocities to positions through constraints, here that neither wheel slides sideways. A dynamic model adds Newton's laws and tyre forces. The kinematic one is simpler and needs fewer parameters, and it is accurate while the tyres are far from their grip limit (Part 6).

Steering angle \delta
The angle of the front wheel relative to the car's heading.
Heading \psi
The direction the car points, measured on the ground.
Yaw rate \dot\psi
How fast the heading turns, in radians per second.
Turning centre (ICR)
The point the whole car is turning about at this instant.

A constraint on velocities, not positions: the car can reach any place, but cannot move sideways directly.

A sanity check: with constant steering, the model should drive a circle of radius \frac{L}{\tan\delta}. The notebook integrates (1) with small steps and measures the circle:

# Sanity check: a constant steering angle should trace a circle of radius L/tan(delta)
v, L, Ts = 15.0, 2.7, 0.02
def global_step(s, delta):
    X, Y, psi = s
    return np.array([X + v*np.cos(psi)*Ts, Y + v*np.sin(psi)*Ts, psi + v/L*np.tan(delta)*Ts])

delta = 0.15; s = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 0.0]); H = [s]
for _ in range(int(2*np.pi*(L/np.tan(delta))/(v*Ts)) + 3):
    s = global_step(s, delta); H.append(s)
H = np.array(H)
R_true = L/np.tan(delta); R_est = (H[:,0].max() - H[:,0].min())/2
radius from geometry L/tan(delta) = 17.86 m   from simulation = 17.86 m

The model passes. But look at that circle in the demo at the top: at 15 m/s it needs 1.28 g of sideways acceleration, which no tyre on any road can provide. The model drives it anyway, because it knows nothing about tyres. Part 6 makes that limit precise.

Part 2

Errors relative to the road

For lane keeping we don't care about the car's absolute position, only its error relative to the lane centreline. Attach a Frenet frame to the centreline, measured by arc length s, with curvature \kappa(s) = \frac{\mathrm{d}\psi_{\text{ref}}}{\mathrm{d}s} (one over the radius of the bend), and define

e_y = \text{signed lateral offset from the centreline}, \qquad e_\psi = \psi - \psi_{\text{ref}}.

Differentiating the geometric relations gives the exact error dynamics [1], [8]:

\dot s = \frac{v\cos e_\psi}{1 - \kappa\,e_y}, \qquad \dot e_y = v\sin e_\psi, \qquad \dot e_\psi = \frac{v}{L}\tan\delta - \kappa\,\dot s.
For the curious: where these come from (optional)

Write the car's position as the closest centreline point plus the offset along the centreline's left normal: p = r(s) + e_y\,n(s). Along the centreline the unit tangent t and normal n turn with the curvature, t' = \kappa\,n and n' = -\kappa\,t. Differentiating in time,

\dot p = \dot s\,t + \dot e_y\,n - e_y\,\kappa\,\dot s\,t = \dot s\,(1 - \kappa e_y)\,t + \dot e_y\,n.

The car moves at speed v at angle e_\psi to the tangent, so \dot p = v\cos e_\psi\,t + v\sin e_\psi\,n. Matching the two components gives the first two equations. The road's direction turns at \dot\psi_{\text{ref}} = \kappa\,\dot s, and \dot e_\psi = \dot\psi - \dot\psi_{\text{ref}} with \dot\psi from (1) gives the third.

For a small offset (\kappa e_y much smaller than 1), the speed along the road is \dot s \approx v, and we get the model used throughout the course:

\boxed{\;\dot e_y = v\sin e_\psi, \qquad \dot e_\psi = \frac{v}{L}\tan\delta - v\,\kappa\;} \tag{2}

So the curvature enters as a measured disturbance: on a bend, the lane itself turns at rate v\kappa, and the steering must supply that yaw rate just to stay parallel to the lane. Setting \dot e_\psi = 0 gives the steering that does exactly that, \delta = \arctan(L\kappa): the feed-forward of chapter 02. Try it on a bend below.

Part 3

Discretisation and its error

A computer steps through time. We integrate (2) with forward Euler at sample time T_s: take the current slope and follow it for one step,

x_{k+1} = x_k + T_s\,f(x_k, \delta_k, \kappa_k), \qquad x = \begin{bmatrix} e_y \\ e_\psi \end{bmatrix}.

Euler makes a local error of order T_s^2 per step, and therefore a global error of order T_s over a fixed horizon: it is a first-order method [7]. Halve the step and the error halves. That is adequate here because T_s = 0.05 s is short compared with how fast the car's errors change, but it is worth seeing the order rather than asserting it. The notebook starts from e_y = 0.2 m and e_\psi = 0.05 rad with \delta = 0.1, and compares Euler at the end of 2 seconds with a very accurate Runge-Kutta solution:

Lateral offset over 2 seconds

Error at 2 s against step size (log-log)

ref = integrate(rk4, 1e-4)
hs = np.array([0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 0.025, 0.0125])
err = np.array([np.linalg.norm(integrate(euler, h) - ref) for h in hs])
slope = np.polyfit(np.log(hs), np.log(err), 1)[0]
print("fitted convergence order = %.2f  (theory: 1)" % slope)
fitted convergence order = 1.00  (theory: 1)
For the curious: why a local error of order two gives a global error of order one (optional)

Taylor's theorem gives the exact step x(t + T_s) = x(t) + T_s\,\dot x(t) + \frac{T_s^2}{2}\,\ddot x(\xi). Euler keeps the first two terms, so each step is off by about \frac{T_s^2}{2}\max\lvert\ddot x\rvert. Reaching a fixed time T takes \frac{T}{T_s} steps, and for a stable enough problem these errors add up rather than explode:

\frac{T}{T_s} \cdot \frac{T_s^2}{2}\max\lvert\ddot x\rvert = \frac{T}{2}\max\lvert\ddot x\rvert\;T_s.

One power of T_s is lost to the number of steps. The same argument gives order 2 for the midpoint method and order 4 for Runge-Kutta (exercise 2).

Part 4

Linearisation

Controllers and observers are easiest to design on a linear model. Linearise the one-step map x_{k+1} = F(x_k, \delta_k, \kappa_k) about the centred, aligned operating point x = 0, \delta = 0, by taking its Jacobians:

A = \left.\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\right|_0, \qquad B = \left.\frac{\partial F}{\partial \delta}\right|_0, \qquad E = \left.\frac{\partial F}{\partial \kappa}\right|_0.

Term by term, using \cos 0 = 1 and \frac{1}{\cos^2 0} = 1,

\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial e_y} = 1, \quad \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial e_\psi} = vT_s, \quad \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial e_\psi} = 1, \quad \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial \delta} = \frac{vT_s}{L}, \quad \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial \kappa} = -vT_s,

so

A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & vT_s \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \frac{vT_s}{L} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad E = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -vT_s \end{bmatrix}. \tag{3}

With the course's numbers (v = 15 m/s, T_s = 0.05 s, L = 2.7 m), vT_s = 0.75 m and \frac{vT_s}{L} \approx 0.278.

The approximation comes from \sin e_\psi \approx e_\psi and \tan\delta \approx \delta. Their first neglected terms are cubic, \sin e = e - \frac{e^3}{6} + \dots and \tan\delta = \delta + \frac{\delta^3}{3} + \dots, so the linear model is excellent for small errors and degrades smoothly as they grow. Part 5

Structural properties

Three questions decide whether model-based control and estimation are even possible.

Open-loop behaviour. Both eigenvalues of A are 1: the error dynamics are a chain of two integrators, the heading error integrating into the lateral error. Errors do not grow explosively, but they do not decay either. Hence the need for feedback.

Controllability. Can steering drive any error to zero? Yes, if and only if the matrix [\,B \ \ AB\,] has full rank. Chapter 02 relies on this.

Observability. In the full system we don't measure the state directly; perception estimates it. If perception gave both components, C = I, reconstructing the state would be immediate. More interestingly, even measuring only the lateral offset, C = [\,1 \ \ 0\,], the pair (A, C) is observable: the heading error can be inferred over time from how the offset changes [5], [6]. That is what makes the Kalman monitor of chapter 05 well posed.

Controllable and observable

In plain words: controllable means the steering can bring the car to any lateral offset and heading; observable means the measurements, watched for a while, reveal the whole state.

More precisely: for x_{k+1} = Ax_k + Bu_k and y_k = Cx_k with n states, the system is controllable if [\,B \ \ AB \ \cdots \ A^{n-1}B\,] has rank n, and observable if the matrix stacking C, CA, \dots, CA^{n-1} has rank n. Here n = 2.

For the curious: both tests by hand (optional)

Write b = \frac{vT_s}{L}. Then AB = \begin{bmatrix} vT_s\,b \\ b \end{bmatrix}, and

\det\,[\,B \ \ AB\,] = \det\begin{bmatrix} 0 & vT_s\,b \\ b & b \end{bmatrix} = -vT_s\,b^2 = -\frac{v^3T_s^3}{L^2}.

For observability from the offset alone, CA = [\,1 \ \ vT_s\,], and

\det\begin{bmatrix} C \\ CA \end{bmatrix} = \det\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & vT_s \end{bmatrix} = vT_s.

Both determinants are non-zero exactly when the car moves. A parked car cannot be steered sideways, and its heading cannot be read from an offset that does not change: both properties hold only for v above zero. Concretely, the heading error is the change of offset over one step divided by vT_s.

A = np.array([[1, v*Ts], [0, 1.]]); B = np.array([[0.], [v*Ts/L]])
ctrb = np.hstack([B, A@B])
C = np.array([[1.0, 0.0]])                     # measure only lateral offset
obsv = np.vstack([C, C@A])
print("eigenvalues of A            :", np.linalg.eigvals(A), " (integrator chain)")
print("rank[ B  AB ]  (controllab.):", np.linalg.matrix_rank(ctrb), " / 2")
print("rank[ C; CA ]  (observab.)  :", np.linalg.matrix_rank(obsv), " / 2  from e_y alone")
eigenvalues of A            : [1.+0.j 1.+0.j]  (integrator chain)
rank[ B  AB ]  (controllab.): 2  / 2

Part 6

When the model stops being valid

The kinematic model assumes the tyres do not slip, which holds only at modest sideways acceleration. On a bend of radius R, the sideways (lateral) acceleration is

a_y = \frac{v^2}{R} = v^2\,\kappa.

A common rule of thumb is that the kinematic model can be trusted while a_y stays below about 0.5\,\mu g, half of what the tyre-road friction \mu can give. Beyond that, side-slip matters, and one should switch to a dynamic bicycle model with tyre forces [1], [2], [3]. Kong et al. [2] compare the two models for control design, and Polack et al. [3] give conditions under which the kinematic model is consistent for planning.

The course drives at 15 m/s. Its nominal road curves gently, with a curvature of at most 0.012 per metre; the integrity monitor of chapter 06 treats 0.022 as the edge of its operating domain; and the out-of-distribution scenario adds a sharp bend of 0.04. Here they are against the two limits:

CurvatureSideways accelerationKinematic model

v = 15.0
for kappa in (0.012, 0.022, 0.04):
    print("kappa = %.3f 1/m  ->  R = %5.1f m,  a_y = %.2f m/s^2  = %.2f g"
          % (kappa, 1/kappa, v**2*kappa, v**2*kappa/9.81))
kappa = 0.012 1/m  ->  R =  83.3 m,  a_y = 2.70 m/s^2  = 0.28 g
kappa = 0.022 1/m  ->  R =  45.5 m,  a_y = 4.95 m/s^2  = 0.50 g
kappa = 0.040 1/m  ->  R =  25.0 m,  a_y = 9.00 m/s^2  = 0.92 g

So the nominal road stays well inside the valid region, the edge of the operating domain sits right at the rule of thumb, and the sharp bend of the out-of-distribution scenario goes far past it, deliberately: 0.92 g is more than a dry road's full grip. That is part of why this scenario is "out of distribution" for the perception and stressful for the plant: the model the controller relies on is no longer accurate exactly when the camera is also wrong.

The chapter on one page

  1. Kinematic bicycle (1): wheels roll without sliding, so the car turns about one centre at radius R = \frac{L}{\tan\delta}, with yaw rate \frac{v}{L}\tan\delta.
  2. Error coordinates (2): offset e_y and heading error e_\psi relative to the lane; the road curvature is a measured disturbance, cancelled by the feed-forward \arctan(L\kappa).
  3. Forward Euler at T_s = 0.05 s: global error proportional to T_s.
  4. Linear model (3) with A, B, E: accurate while the angles stay small; the neglected terms are cubic.
  5. Structure: a double integrator, controllable by steering and observable from the offset alone, as long as the car moves.
  6. Validity: trust the model while v^2\kappa stays below about half the grip, 0.5\,\mu g.

Part 8

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

  1. The yaw rate from geometry. Re-derive \dot\psi = \frac{v}{L}\tan\delta from the turning-centre geometry.
    Hint

    Each wheel moves perpendicular to the line joining it to the turning centre. Draw both lines; the widget of Part 1 shows them. Where does the angle \delta appear a second time?

    Solution

    The rear wheel points along the heading, so the turning centre lies on the line through the rear axle, perpendicular to the heading, at some distance R. The front wheel, a distance L ahead, points at angle \delta, so the line from it to the turning centre is perpendicular to it. The two lines from the turning centre, to the rear wheel and to the front wheel, make the angle \delta between them (two angles whose sides are perpendicular are equal). In the right triangle formed by the turning centre and the two wheels, the side opposite that angle is L and the adjacent side is R, so \tan\delta = \frac{L}{R}, that is R = \frac{L}{\tan\delta}. The rear axle moves at speed v on a circle of radius R, so the heading turns at \dot\psi = \frac{v}{R} = \frac{v}{L}\tan\delta. (The front wheel is further from the centre, at \frac{L}{\sin\delta}, so it moves faster, at \frac{v}{\cos\delta}.)

  2. A second-order method. In the discretisation study, replace Euler by the midpoint method and confirm that the fitted order becomes 2.
    Hint

    The midpoint method takes a half Euler step to estimate the slope at the middle of the step, then uses that slope for the full step.

    Solution 
    def midpoint(x, h): return x + h*f(x + 0.5*h*f(x))

    With the same five step sizes, from 0.2 to 0.0125 s, the errors at 2 s are 8.4e-3, 2.1e-3, 5.3e-4, 1.3e-4 and 3.3e-5: each halving of the step divides the error by 4, and the fitted order is 2.00. The midpoint method needs two evaluations of f per step instead of one, but at T_s = 0.05 s its error is about 600 times smaller than Euler's (5.3e-4 against 0.33). For comparison, the four-stage Runge-Kutta used as the reference fits an order of about 4.

  3. Exact against simplified. Drive the global model (1) along a winding road, compute e_y and e_\psi from the geometry, and compare with the error model (2). Where do they start to differ, and why? Look at the 1 - \kappa e_y term.
    Hint

    Use the course's nominal road, \kappa(s) = 0.012\sin\frac{2\pi s}{300}. Build its centreline by integrating the heading along arc length, steer with the feed-forward \arctan(L\kappa), start a little off the centreline, and project the car onto the centreline to get its offset.

    Solution

    Starting aligned with the road but 0.5 m off the centreline, with feed-forward steering and no feedback, model (2) says the offset stays at 0.5 m forever. The real geometry disagrees by up to 0.30 m within 10 seconds. Starting 2 m off, the gap reaches 1.18 m in 30 seconds; starting 5 m off, 2.94 m. The gap grows in proportion to the offset, which points at the product \kappa e_y. At an offset e_y, the car covers the road's length at \dot s = \frac{v\cos e_\psi}{1 - \kappa e_y}, not v: on the inside of a bend it gets around faster. Model (2) therefore feels the road's curvature at the wrong place and at the wrong rate, and the small heading errors this creates integrate twice into the offset. Integrating the exact equations, with \dot s, instead reproduces the global model to within 5 mm. In closed loop, feedback keeps e_y small, so \kappa e_y stays tiny (0.004 at an offset of 0.3 m on the nominal road) and (2) is accurate where it is used.

  4. The limit on a dry road. Compute the sideways acceleration at which the kinematic assumption breaks on a dry road (\mu \approx 0.9). Which of the course's scenarios approach it?
    Hint

    Use the rule of thumb a_y \lesssim 0.5\,\mu g, with g = 9.81 m/s², and a_y = v^2\kappa at 15 m/s. The widget of Part 6 does the same.

    Solution

    The limit is 0.5 \times 0.9 \times 9.81 \approx 4.41 m/s², or 0.45 g. The nominal road, at most 2.70 m/s² (0.28 g), is well inside. The edge of the operating domain, \kappa = 0.022, gives 4.95 m/s² (0.50 g): just past the limit. The out-of-distribution bend, \kappa = 0.04, gives 9.00 m/s² (0.92 g): twice the limit, and more than the full grip of a dry road, \mu g \approx 8.83 m/s². A real car would slide there, which the kinematic model cannot represent. To take that bend within the rule of thumb, it would have to slow to \sqrt{\frac{4.41}{0.04}} \approx 10.5 m/s.

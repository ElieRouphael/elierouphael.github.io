Operational design domain and the integrity score

Three chapters built three ways of doubting the perception: its own uncertainty (chapter 04), the physics check of the Kalman filter (chapter 05), and the conditions it was designed for (chapter 03). This chapter fuses them into one number, the integrity score I between 0 and 1, that the controller can act on. Below, the drive meets two faults: a heavy blur, which the perception notices, and a camera bias, which it does not. Each detector sees only one of them. Switch detectors off, change how they are combined, and watch which faults the integrity score still catches.

Part 1

The operating domain as a set

The operational design domain (ODD) is the set of conditions a system was designed and validated for [1], [2]: road types, weather, light, speed, and so on. For the course's perception, four conditions matter, each with a limit:

\mathcal{D} = \{\, c : |\kappa| < \kappa_{\max},\ b > b_{\min},\ \sigma_{\text{blur}} < \sigma_{\max},\ o < o_{\max} \,\}.

The limits are \kappa_{\max} = 0.022 per metre for the road's curvature, b_{\min} = 0.45 for the illumination gain, \sigma_{\max} = 1.6 pixels for the blur and o_{\max} = 0.35 for the occluded fraction of the road. Monitoring is then a membership test, c_k \in \mathcal{D}, at every step. Leaving \mathcal{D} is a form of novelty detection [9]: the input no longer resembles what the system was validated on.

What is an operational design domain?

In plain words: the list of conditions in which the system has been designed and tested to work. Here: bends up to a certain sharpness, enough light, a reasonably sharp image, a mostly visible road. Outside it, the system makes no promise.

More precisely: a set \mathcal{D} in the space of operating conditions, fixed at design time. A runtime monitor tests whether the current conditions c_k, as measured, lie inside it [3].

Condition c
A measurable property of the situation: curvature, light, blur, occlusion.
Guard
One inequality of the domain, such as \sigma_{\text{blur}} < 1.6.
Margin m_j
How far inside guard j the condition is: 1 for perfect conditions, 0 on the boundary, negative outside.
Domain score s_{\text{ODD}}
A soft version of "inside": the worst margin, squashed into [0, 1].

Two subtleties matter. First, the conditions are not known exactly. Blur and illumination are estimated from the pixels (chapter 03's features), so the test must cope with uncertain measurements [3]. The curvature needs a source outside the image, such as a map, since a sharp bend looks perfectly ordinary in the pixels (chapter 03, Part 5). Second, a hard indicator \mathbb{1}[c \in \mathcal{D}] chatters at the boundary: measured with noise, a condition close to its limit flips in and out at almost every step. The course therefore uses a soft margin. Each guard gets a normalised margin, positive inside:

m_\kappa = 1 - \frac{|\kappa|}{\kappa_{\max}}, \qquad m_b = \frac{b - b_{\min}}{1 - b_{\min}}, \qquad m_\sigma = 1 - \frac{\sigma_{\text{blur}}}{\sigma_{\max}}, \qquad m_o = 1 - \frac{o}{o_{\max}},

and the worst one goes through a logistic function:

\boxed{\;s_{\text{ODD}} = \sigma_L\big(a \min_j m_j\big), \qquad \sigma_L(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}, \qquad a = 6\;}

Here a is the logistic's gain, which sets how soft the margin is (\beta is kept for the smoothing of Part 3). On the boundary, s_{\text{ODD}} = 0.5; in perfect conditions, with every margin at 1, it is 0.998. Move the true conditions towards the edge of the domain. The widget measures them as the notebook does, from the pixels, with noise of 0.15 pixels on the blur and 0.05 on the illumination:

For the curious: how soft should the margin be? (optional)

Suppose the measured margin is the true margin plus Gaussian noise of standard deviation s_m. Then the probability that the true condition is inside, given a measured margin m, is

P(\text{inside} \mid m) = \Phi\Big(\frac{m}{s_m}\Big) \approx \sigma_L\Big(\frac{1.70\,m}{s_m}\Big),

using the classical approximation of the normal distribution function by a logistic. For the blur guard, s_m = \frac{0.15}{1.6} = 0.094, so the probability of being inside is a logistic with a gain of about 18, and for the illumination guard about 19. The course's gain of 6 is three times softer than the measurement noise alone justifies. At a margin of 0.2 (a blur of 1.28 pixels), the probability of being inside is 0.98, yet s_{\text{ODD}} is only 0.77. The softer gain buys an early warning: trust starts to fall well before the boundary, which leaves the smoothing and the controller time to react. Its cost is that it double-counts with the uncertainty score, which also falls with the blur (exercise 3).

Part 2

Three detectors, three soft scores

Each of the three signals is a detector of untrustworthiness. Each is mapped monotonically to a trust score between 0 and 1, where 1 means trustworthy:

s_{\text{unc}} = \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma^{\text{AI}}}{\sigma_{\text{ref}}}\Big), \qquad s_{\text{NIS}} = \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\big(\mathrm{NIS} - \chi^2_{2,0.99}\big)^+}{c}\Big), \qquad s_{\text{ODD}} = \sigma_L\big(a \min_j m_j\big),

with a reference trace \sigma_{\text{ref}} = 0.02, the gate \chi^2_{2,0.99} = 9.21, a scale c = 6 and the gain a = 6. The shapes are deliberate. The two exponentials behave like soft likelihoods that fall off as their statistic worsens. And s_{\text{NIS}} reacts only to the excess above the chi-squared gate ((\cdot)^+ keeps the positive part), so a consistent filter scores exactly 1 on 99% of the steps.

Each detector is sensitive to a different failure. The trace \mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma^{\text{AI}} rises when the perception is unsure of itself (chapter 04). The NIS rises when the measurements contradict the car's physics, however confident they are (chapter 05). The margin falls when the conditions leave the validated domain (chapter 03). That is the whole reason to fuse them.

CHI2 = 9.210
sigmaL = lambda z: 1/(1 + np.exp(-z))

def s_unc(trace, ref=0.02): return np.exp(-trace/ref)
def s_nis(nis, scale=6.0):   return np.exp(-np.maximum(0, nis - CHI2)/scale)
def s_odd(margin):           return sigmaL(6*margin)

In the notebook's scenario, the blur comes with fog that also dims the image. The perception reports its noise honestly, growing with both: \sigma_{\text{rep}} = \sigma_0\,(1 + 2.5\,\sigma_{\text{blur}})\,\big(1 + 1.5\,(1 - b)\big), from \sigma_0 = (0.02 \text{ m}, 0.01 \text{ rad}) in clear weather. Part 3

Fusing the detectors

How should three trust scores combine into one? Combining complementary detectors is a recurring theme of perception monitoring [8]. The course multiplies them:

\boxed{\;I_{\text{raw}} = s_{\text{unc}} \cdot s_{\text{NIS}} \cdot s_{\text{ODD}}\;}

The product is a logical AND, an independent veto: every detector must be satisfied for I to be high, and any one of them collapsing to 0 vetoes trust. That is the safe choice. A fail-operational system should distrust the perception as soon as any credible signal says so. The average \frac{1}{3}\sum_i s_i, by contrast, lets two satisfied detectors mask one that has detected a real fault. Drag the point, or use the sliders:

At (s1, s2) = (0.1, 0.9):  product = 0.09 (veto) ,  average = 0.50 (would pass)
For the curious: why a product, and why not Bayes' rule (optional)

A product of probabilities is easily mistaken for a naive-Bayes combination [4], [5], but the two answer different questions, and the difference is worth getting right. Suppose each detector watches a different failure mode, and s_i is the probability that failure mode i is absent. If the failure modes occur independently, the perception is trustworthy only when all of them are absent, so

P(\text{trustworthy}) = \prod_i P(\text{no failure of kind } i) = s_{\text{unc}}\,s_{\text{NIS}}\,s_{\text{ODD}}.

This is the reliability of a series system, in which any failing component fails the whole: precisely the veto. Naive Bayes answers a different question. It combines several pieces of evidence about one and the same hypothesis T, "trustworthy". With conditionally independent signals, it is the odds O(p) = \frac{p}{1 - p} that multiply:

O(T \mid \text{all}) = O(T)^{1-n} \prod_{i=1}^{n} O(T \mid \text{signal}_i).

This is the product rule of classifier combination [4], which multiplies each class's posteriors and then normalises across the classes. With a prior of one half and two detectors at 0.1 and 0.9, the odds are \frac{1}{9} and 9. They cancel, and the posterior is 0.5: the same masking as the average. Two detectors at 0.9 each give 0.988, more trust than either alone, while the product gives 0.81. So the product is not a posterior. It is a deliberately conservative AND: exact for independent failure modes, and pessimistic when two detectors watch the same failure, as s_{\text{unc}} and s_{\text{ODD}} both do under blur (exercise 3).

Finally, the score is smoothed to avoid chatter, by a first-order recursion, an exponential moving average, with weight \beta:

I_k = (1 - \beta)\,I_{k-1} + \beta\,I_{\text{raw},k}, \qquad \beta = 0.4.

With \beta = 0.4, the score averages over roughly the last four samples, 0.2 s. A single inconsistent sample cannot pull it below one half; two in a row can.

Part 4

Does fusion actually help? ROC and detection delay

To evaluate detectors we need ground truth. The notebook builds a 40-second drive with two different faults, and labels each instant "untrustworthy" while a fault is present:

  • a blur episode, heavy defocus with fog dimming the image, labelled while the blur exceeds the domain's 1.6 pixels, from 9.85 s to 16.77 s. It raises the perception's uncertainty and shows in the pixel features, so s_{\text{unc}} and s_{\text{ODD}} should catch it. But the filter's R grows honestly with the noise, so the NIS stays quiet: its mean during the blur is 2.2, as for a consistent filter;
  • an unmonitored perception bias, 0.20 m and 0.05 rad from 24 to 32 s, while the conditions look perfectly normal: a sensor glitch, or a novel object. It is invisible to the uncertainty and the domain monitors, but it makes the innovation inconsistent, so only the NIS catches it.

No single detector covers both. They are compared in two ways. The first is a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve: the true-positive rate against the false-positive rate as the alarm threshold sweeps [6], summarised by the area under it (AUC: 1 is perfect, 0.5 is chance). The second is the detection delay, the time from a fault's onset to the first alarm [7].

for k in range(N):
    t = k*Ts
    blur = ramp(t, 8, 11, 0, 2.6) if t < 14 else ramp(t, 16, 18, 2.6, 0)   # blur fault 8..18
    bright = 1 - 0.4*blur/2.6
    bias_on = 24 <= t < 32                                                  # unmonitored bias fault
    srep = base*(1 + 2.5*blur)*(1 + 1.5*max(0, 1 - bright))                 # honest noise level
    ...                                                                     # Kalman filter with R = diag(srep^2)
    blur_m = max(0, blur + r.normal(0, 0.15)); bright_m = np.clip(bright + r.normal(0, 0.05), 0, 1)
    margin = min((bright_m - 0.45)/0.55, 1 - blur_m/1.6)                    # pixel-only ODD guards
    su, sn, so = s_unc((srep**2).sum()), s_nis(nis), s_odd(margin)
    Iraw = su*sn*so; Iprev = 0.6*Iprev + 0.4*Iraw
    S["label"].append((blur > 1.6) or bias_on)                             # ground-truth "untrustworthy"

For the detection delays, the notebook sets each detector's threshold so that it gives 10% false positives on the clean samples. It also requires an alarm to last 5 consecutive samples, 0.25 s, so that a lone spike does not count:

detector            blur-fault delay    bias-fault delay
uncertainty                 0.00 s            MISSED
NIS                         MISSED            1.75 s
ODD                         0.00 s            MISSED
fused                       0.00 s            0.30 s

The uncertainty and domain monitors catch the blur but miss the bias. The NIS catches the bias but is, correctly, quiet during the honest-noise blur. The fused score catches both, with the highest AUC: 0.96, against 0.70, 0.66 and 0.70. It does so robustly: over 50 runs with different noise, its AUC stays between 0.948 and 0.964, and it is the highest every time.

Two effects are mixed in that comparison, though, because the fused score is the only one that is smoothed. Fusion itself, the raw product without smoothing, reaches an AUC of 0.86. It covers both faults, while a detector that sees only one fault ranks the other's samples no better than chance, which caps it near 0.75 here. The smoothing adds the rest, up to 0.96, and cuts the bias delay from 1.75 s to 0.30 s. During the bias, the NIS crosses its gate on only 59% of the samples: the filter partly absorbs the bias, and the alarm flickers. A single-sample detector with a 5-sample hold must wait for five crossings in a row; the smoothed score accumulates the flicker. The second button of the widget shows the two steps.

No single detector covers every failure; the product inherits all their coverage, and lets any one of them veto.

This is why the deployed system of chapter 08 fuses all three signals rather than trusting any single one, and why the model-based NIS is indispensable for the failures the condition monitors cannot see.

Part 5

Where this goes next

The integrity score is the single number the controller acts on. In chapter 07 it sets the authority shared between the automation and the driver, \lambda = \Phi(I, W), where W is the driver's workload. It also shrinks the operating envelope:

v_{\text{ref}} = v_{\text{nom}}\,(0.4 + 0.6\,I),

full speed when I = 1, 70% of it at I = 0.5, and 40% when trust is gone. Because I is smooth and vetoed by the weakest detector, that hand-over is graceful rather than a hard switch.

Part 6

The chapter on one page

  1. Operating domain: a set \mathcal{D} of conditions; monitoring is a membership test on measured conditions.
  2. Soft margin: a normalised margin per guard, the worst one through a logistic, s_{\text{ODD}} = \sigma_L(a \min_j m_j); no chatter at the boundary.
  3. Three detectors: uncertainty (the trace), consistency (the NIS's excess over its gate), domain (the margin), each mapped to a score in [0, 1].
  4. Fusion: I = s_{\text{unc}}\,s_{\text{NIS}}\,s_{\text{ODD}}, an AND in which any detector can veto; the average lets a fault be masked.
  5. Smoothing: an exponential moving average with \beta = 0.4, about the last four samples.
  6. Evaluation: ROC curves and detection delays. Fusion covers both faults; smoothing accumulates flickering evidence.

Part 7

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

  1. Noisy-OR. Replace the product with a noisy-OR on the alarms, 1 - \prod_i (1 - a_i), and compare its ROC with the product of trust scores. When would you prefer each?
    Hint

    Write each detector's alarm as a_i = 1 - s_i and simplify. Then try an OR of binary alarms, each detector thresholded on its own.

    Solution

    With a_i = 1 - s_i, the noisy-OR alarm is 1 - \prod_i (1 - a_i) = 1 - \prod_i s_i = 1 - I_{\text{raw}}: the same rule, so the ROC is identical (an AUC of 0.86 for both on the notebook's run, before smoothing). The real alternative is an OR of binary alarms: threshold each detector at its own 10% false-positive rate, and raise an alarm if any fires. On the notebook's run, that gives a single operating point, 78% true positives at 12% false positives, against 92% at 10% for the smoothed product. Prefer the soft product when the scores are calibrated and a graded number is useful: the controller of chapter 07 scales speed and authority with it. Prefer a binary OR when the detectors are heterogeneous or uncalibrated, each with its own validated threshold, and the logic must be simple to certify. Either way, false alarms add up: these three detectors, at 10%, 1% and 10%, would reach 20% if they fired independently. Here the uncertainty and domain alarms fire together, while the blur builds up and clears, so the union is only 12%.

  2. Night. Add a third fault type, low light at night, and check which detectors cover it. Does the fused AUC stay the highest?
    Hint

    Dim the illumination to 0.3 from 34 to 39.5 s (over 1.5 s each way), label the samples below the domain's 0.45, and keep the notebook's honest noise model, which grows with darkness.

    Solution

    Only the domain monitor catches it, through its illumination guard: it flags every sample of the night, labelled from 35.2 to 38.3 s. The uncertainty barely moves. The camera honestly reports its noise doubling at that light level (×2.05), so the trace goes from 0.0005 to 0.0021, and s_{\text{unc}} only from 0.975 to 0.90. That is less of a drop than at a blur of 0.5 pixels (0.85), which the domain still accepts. The NIS is quiet, since the reported noise is honest. The fused score inherits the domain monitor's coverage: it also flags the whole night, and its AUC (0.95) stays the highest, in 30 of 30 runs with different noise. This is the pattern of the chapter: each fault is seen by some subset of the detectors, and the product needs only one of them.

  3. Double counting. The independence assumption is false when blur lowers both s_{\text{unc}} and s_{\text{ODD}}. Estimate their correlation on the scenario. Does the double counting make the product too conservative?
    Hint

    Compare the product of the two with the smaller of the two, at a few blur levels inside the domain.

    Solution

    Their correlation over the run is 0.986: they are nearly the same signal. As the blur grows towards the limit, both fall. At 1.0 pixel, well inside the domain, s_{\text{unc}} = 0.63 and s_{\text{ODD}} = 0.90, product 0.57. At 1.3 pixels, 0.47 and 0.75, product 0.35, already below one half. At the limit, 1.6 pixels, 0.31 and 0.50, product 0.15, half of the smaller one. So yes: against a fixed alarm line, the product is too conservative inside the domain. With the smoothed product and an alarm below one half, the alarm starts at 9.10 s, 0.75 s before the blur crosses its limit, and lasts until 17.20 s, 0.43 s after it is back. Replacing the two correlated scores by their minimum, then multiplying by the independent NIS score, lowers the share of samples outside the faults with a raw score below one half from 4.4% to 3.2%. The ranking barely changes (an AUC of 0.863 against 0.860, before smoothing). A rule of thumb: multiply across independent failure modes, and take the minimum within a group of detectors that watch the same one. The hero demo's "weakest" rule applies the minimum to all three.

  4. The smoothing weight. Tune the moving average's \beta: plot the detection delay against the false-alarm rate as \beta varies. This is the responsiveness-against-robustness trade you will meet again with CUSUM in chapter 09.
    Hint

    Fix the alarm line, say I < 0.5. Count false alarms on a long fault-free drive, and measure the delays over many runs with different noise. A single NIS sample above 9.21 + 6\ln 2 = 13.37 halves s_{\text{NIS}}.

    Solution

    With the alarm below one half, an hour of fault-free driving for the false alarms, and 30 runs for the delays (medians):

    \betaFalse alarms per hourBias caught afterBias missed
    0.050–29 of 30
    0.104.33 s18 of 30
    0.202.60 s1 of 30
    0.400.55 s0 of 30
    0.7210.00 s0 of 30
    11040.00 s0 of 30

    A small \beta averages over a long window. It raises no false alarms, but it averages the flickering bias evidence away: the mean of s_{\text{NIS}} during the bias is 0.70, above the line. A large \beta reacts at once, but it passes single NIS spikes through. Without smoothing, a sample above 13.37 happens 0.125% of the time, which is about 90 alarms per hour from the NIS alone (104 measured, with the domain monitor's noise). The course's 0.4 sits at the knee: no false alarm in an hour of clean driving, and the bias caught after a median of 0.55 s. The blur is caught at once for every \beta from 0.1 up, because the alarm already starts while the blur is still inside the domain (exercise 3). Chapter 09's CUSUM test handles this trade optimally for a fault of known size.

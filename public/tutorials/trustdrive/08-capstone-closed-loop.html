Part 1

The assembled system

The full closed loop chains every component of the course. The perception feeds three monitors, their scores fuse into one integrity number, and that number sets the shared authority and the speed. Monitoring a learned perception with checks independent of the network is an active research area [4]. The numbers are the chapters:

↓ three monitors ↓

Three architectures use this machinery differently:

  • naive: perception straight into the LQR, full automation, no monitoring;
  • hard handover: switch entirely to the driver whenever the reported uncertainty \mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma^{\text{AI}} crosses a threshold, 0.0045 (nine times its clear-weather value);
  • proposed: filter the estimate, fuse the integrity score, share authority smoothly and contract the speed.
Part 2

Methodology

Controlled comparison. For a given seed, the plant, the perception noise, the driver and the scenario are identical across the three methods; only the use of the perception output differs. Any difference in outcome is therefore due to the monitoring-and-arbitration layer, not to luck.

Scenarios map to failure modes. Each runs 30 s on a gently curving road, from a start 0.3 m off the centre:

  • nominal: everything inside the domain;
  • fog: a visible degradation, blur up to 3 pixels with darkening, and an occluded road from 15 to 20 s;
  • sharp bend (ood_curve): an out-of-distribution regime that still looks like a valid image, curvature 0.028 to 0.047 from 12 to 22 s, where the perception is biased but still claims its usual noise;
  • conflict: an overloaded driver fighting the automation from 14 to 20 s, then absent for 2 s.

Metrics. The RMSE and the maximum of the lateral offset e_y; the number of lane exits (|e_y| > 0.7 m); the steering jerk \sum_k |\Delta\delta_k|, for ride quality; and the unsafe AI usage, the share of time the automation is trusted (\lambda > 0.5) while the car is outside the operating domain, the safety-critical figure.

Statistics. The comparison is paired, with the same seeds for every method. The notebook reports the mean over seeds, and puts a bootstrap 95% confidence interval [1] on the paired difference between methods; an interval that excludes zero is a reliable improvement.

What is an ablation study?

In plain words: to find out what each part of a system contributes, remove it, run the same test again, and see what breaks.

More precisely: a controlled experiment in which one component is disabled at a time, everything else held fixed, so that the change in outcome measures that component's contribution.

Baseline
A simpler method to compare against: here, naive and hard handover.
Paired comparison
Methods run on the same random seeds, and differences taken seed by seed.
Bootstrap
Resampling the observed differences with replacement, to see how much their mean could vary.
Confidence interval
A range that, by this procedure, covers the true mean difference 95% of the time.

In the two degraded scenarios, the proposed method cuts the error by a factor of 3.2 to 5.4. In fog, the naive car leaves its lane four times. In the sharp bend, the hard handover is identical to naive: the reported uncertainty rises by only 11%, far below its threshold, so it never trips. There, both baselines spend a third of the run trusting the automation outside its domain (naive does so in fog too); the proposed method, a single sample.

In nominal conditions and in the conflict, all three are about equal: 80% of the nominal squared error comes from the first 5 s, the recovery from the 0.3 m start. The proposed method is a hair worse there (0.036 against 0.035), almost all of it in those first seconds: while the car recovers from its start, the filtered estimate lags the true state. Without the filter the RMSE is 0.0355; without the 5% of authority left to the driver, 0.0357. Its steering, though, is twice as smooth (jerk 6.2 against 12.1), because the automation steers on the Kalman filter's estimate rather than on raw measurements: without the filter, the jerk is 11.4. In the conflict, naive and handover ignore the driver entirely, so they are untouched; only the proposed method is exposed to the fighting driver, and it out-votes them with 95% of the authority.

Part 4

Mechanism analysis

The aggregate numbers hide why each method behaves as it does. Look inside the proposed method, at what the perception reports and what each monitor makes of it:

True and perceived offset

The three scores and the integrity

true offset perceived uncertainty NIS domain integrity

The sharp bend, the decisive case. The reported uncertainty barely moves, so the hard handover never trips. The model-based checks are what react. The domain margin on the map's curvature falls at once and stays down. The NIS sees the inconsistency too, but intermittently: it averages 8 over the bend, peaks at 69, and crosses its gate on only 29% of the samples, because the filter partly tracks the bias. Together they collapse the integrity score, the authority shifts to the driver, and the car slows to 6 m/s.

Fog. Here the perception's uncertainty genuinely rises, so the integrity falls on that score alone, and authority transfers to the driver smoothly while the automation's noisy command is de-weighted. The NIS stays quiet, since the reported noise is honest. In the conflict, by contrast, the perception is fine, so the integrity stays high and the overloaded driver is out-voted. Different mechanisms, one principle: authority follows integrity.

Part 5

Is the improvement statistically real?

One seed could be a fluke. The notebook repeats every method on 20 seeds per scenario, and bootstraps a 95% confidence interval on the paired difference, proposed minus baseline, pooled over the two degraded scenarios:

def boot_ci(d, n=20000, seed=0):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed); idx = r.integers(0, len(d), (n, len(d)))
    m = d[idx].mean(1); return d.mean(), np.percentile(m, 2.5), np.percentile(m, 97.5)

What the interval does and does not say: the 20 seeds vary only the noise of the same two scripted scenarios. So the interval says the improvement is not a fluke of the noise; it says nothing about other scenarios, other parameters or a real car. That is the job of the next two parts.

Part 6

Component ablation: what is each part doing?

To show that the parts of the integrity score earn their place, the notebook rebuilds the loop with switches and turns components off one at a time. Try it, on the sharp bend where the failure is subtle, and on fog:

full proposed this configuration 

configuration                      RMSE   unsafe%
full proposed                     0.052       0.2
no speed contraction              0.070       0.2
no NIS (ODD still on)             0.053       0.2
no ODD (NIS still on)             0.170      30.2
uncertainty only (no NIS+ODD)     0.192      33.3

The ablation is precise about each part. Removing the speed contraction detects nothing less, but it costs tracking: on the bend, the error rises by 35% in this run (by half on average over ten seeds), in fog by half, and the lateral acceleration on the bend rises from 0.42 g to 1.08 g, a cost the kinematic plant does not penalise (Part 7).

Removing the NIS while keeping the domain monitor barely hurts here, because the sharp bend is a monitored condition: with the map's curvature, the domain margin falls in a sustained way and holds the integrity down on its own.

Removing the domain monitor while keeping the NIS hurts more, but not because the NIS is blind. It sees the inconsistency, as Part 4 showed, but as an instantaneous soft score, under a bias the filter partly tracks, it crosses the gate on only 29% of the samples. On its own, that holds the integrity below one half for only 10% of the bend. This is exactly why a cumulative test, the CUSUM of chapter 09, is the right way to turn the NIS into a robust standalone trigger, and why fusing it with the domain monitor is valuable in the meantime.

Removing both, leaving only the perception's self-reported uncertainty, collapses the integrity signal: the automation stays trusted outside the domain, and the method degrades to naive. That is the same failure that sinks the hard handover, which also relies on the reported uncertainty.

Self-reported uncertainty is not enough. You need at least one external check, model- or domain-based, and, for the intermittent ones, a cumulative test.

Part 7

Limitations and threats to validity

An honest capstone states where the demonstrator is simplified:

  • Perception is synthetic. A learned sensor is emulated analytically, rather than by a network trained on real images. The qualitative conclusions (uncertainty flat but NIS rising out of distribution) should be revalidated on a real network and dataset.
  • Kinematic plant. No tyre slip, actuator lag or longitudinal dynamics. The sharp bend asks naive and handover for 10.6 m/s², 1.08 g, beyond the grip of a dry road; the proposed method's slowdown keeps it at 0.42 g. A dynamic model would make that difference decisive, and the kinematic error metrics cannot show it.
  • A perfect domain monitor. The domain monitor reads the true blur, light and occlusion, and the map's exact curvature. Chapter 06 measured the conditions from the pixels, with noise, and monitoring the domain from uncertain measurements is a research problem of its own [5].
  • A fixed-speed filter. The Kalman monitor keeps its 15 m/s model while the car slows. Scheduling it on the actual speed lowers the NIS while slowed in the bend from 5.3 to 3.7 on average, and changes none of the results.
  • Simple driver. A delayed proportional model is a caricature of a human. Real takeover behaviour, distraction and trust dynamics are richer (chapter 07).
  • Tuning and scope. The score maps, thresholds and gains are hand-set, not learned or certified, and the domain box is a coarse proxy. One vehicle, lateral control only, short episodes, and an integrity score that is a heuristic, not a guaranteed bound. The results are internally consistent, but they are not a safety case [6].

These are exactly the openings that chapter 09 turns into research directions.

Part 9

Exercises

Each exercise has a hint and a solution. Try first, then peek.

  1. More metrics. Add \max |e_y| and the authority split to the 20-seed study, and bootstrap their confidence intervals.
    Hint

    Collect metrics(...)["max_ey"] and the mean of log["lambda"] per run, and reuse boot_ci on the paired differences. Then look at when the maximum happens.

    Solution

    On average over the 20 seeds, the maximum offset in fog is 1.34 m for naive (63 lane exits in all), 0.57 m for the handover and 0.315 m for the proposed method; in the sharp bend, 0.60 m for both baselines and 0.315 m again. Pooled over the two degraded scenarios, the paired difference is -0.65 m, 95% CI [-0.80, -0.52], against naive, and -0.27 m, CI [-0.29, -0.24], against the handover. But notice that the proposed method's maximum is 0.315 m in every scenario, nominal included: it is the start, 0.3 m off the centre, not the fault. A maximum is dominated by its single worst moment, so it needs a careful reading. The mean authority of the proposed method is 0.95 in nominal and conflict, 0.61 in fog and 0.65 in the bend. Its confidence intervals are only about ±0.001 wide, because the integrity depends almost only on the scripted conditions, not on the noise: a narrow interval over seeds says little about robustness.

  2. Ablation in fog. Extend the ablation to fog. Which component matters there, and does the redundancy story change, given that the NIS is quiet under honest-noise blur?
    Hint

    The widget of Part 6 has a fog button. Which score falls in fog without any help from the other two?

    Solution

    In fog, every monitoring configuration gives the same result, an RMSE of 0.045, with no unsafe usage: the reported uncertainty alone catches the fog, because the perception honestly reports its noise growing. The NIS stays quiet and the domain monitor is redundant. What still matters is the rest of the chain: without the Kalman filter, 0.047; holding the speed at 15 m/s, 0.067. So the redundancy story is asymmetric. Visible degradations are caught by every detector, including the cheap self-report; the external checks are insurance for the failures the self-report cannot see, such as the sharp bend, where the ablation showed them to be decisive.

  3. A rank test. Replace the bootstrap with a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test [2], and compare the conclusions.
    Hint

    scipy.stats.wilcoxon(d) on the 40 pooled differences, then on each scenario's 20. With every difference of the same sign, what is the smallest p-value possible?

    Solution

    Same conclusion. Against either baseline, all 40 differences are negative, so the test statistic is 0 and p = 1.8 \times 10^{-12}. For each scenario alone, p = 1.9 \times 10^{-6}, which is 2 \times 2^{-20}, the smallest possible two-sided value with 20 pairs: the test is saturated. The two answer different questions. The rank test only asks whether the differences lean one way, and is robust to outliers and to non-normal data [3]; the bootstrap interval also says how much better, here 13 to 17 cm. For comparing methods over a handful of runs, reporting both is good practice.

  4. Tuning the handover. Vary the hard-handover threshold over a wide range and show that no setting makes it competitive on the sharp bend.
    Hint

    Print the reported trace in clear weather and during the bend first; the synthetic perception's trace has no noise. Then ask what the driver alone does on a sharp bend.

    Solution

    The reported trace is 0.00050 in clear weather and 0.00053 to 0.00061 during the bend: it rises by 7 to 23%, deterministically. Any threshold above 0.00061 never trips, and the handover is identical to naive (RMSE 0.187). A threshold that does trip is worse. At 0.0006 or 0.00055, the handover catches part of the bend: RMSE 0.27 to 0.40, with a lane exit. At 0.00053 or below, it hands the whole bend to the driver: RMSE 0.53 and six lane exits. The driver has no map feed-forward and a gain of only 0.12 on the offset, so on a bend of curvature 0.04 it needs a standing offset of about a metre to hold the steering. Below the clear-weather value, it hands over all the time (RMSE 0.25 even in nominal). Against the proposed method's 0.052, no setting is competitive. In a real perception, the trace would also be noisy, and such a fine threshold would fire constantly. The problem is not the threshold: a self-reported number cannot detect what the network does not know, and handing everything to a weaker agent is no remedy.
